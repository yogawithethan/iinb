Despite all the persuasive chatter from biohackers and AI evangelists, your body and mind will grow old and die. Even if it ends up taking a few hundreds years longer than biologically orchestrated, it is one of two things you can be absolutely certain about.
How any of us can sleepwalk our way through life without really taking this fact to heart is a true mystery. Perhaps we are blocked by misguided faith, or by a belief that such contemplation is a waste of precious time, or by our simply looking too busy for the shock of mortality to come say hi. Whatever the reason, it can sometimes take an agonizingly large series of unfortunate events before one wakes up to the fact that, ultimately, what one calls ‘my life’ is effectively outside ’my control,’ and that, really, one needs to get to the bottom of the human predicament ASAP.
Even those who intellectually accept their destiny (again, the vast majority do not) typically only do so at a surface-level, claiming to want to live ‘more in the present moment’ in the face of it. Such people philosophize that because life is short, it must be all about ‘becoming fulfilled,’ ‘having awesome experiences,’ or maybe ‘feeling as alive as possible, as often as possible’. Such an understandable perspective can only arise after one’s initial consultation with finitude, but can stick around for the remainder of the incarnation. People seem so. dang. certain. that the only way to accept such a bitter ending is by making their brief adventure here on planet Earth as epic and story-worthy as humanly possible. Otherwise…what’s the point?
Because of this, people—the younger generation in particular—go to greater and greater extremes trying to fulfill their projected ideals of a life well-lived. Many try and try again to maximize their humanness by working relentlessly on their profession or passion, striving to rise higher in the ranks, always justtttt on the brink of having made it. Others go about it by participating in adrenalized activities such as drag racing, cold plunging, gambling, partying, compulsively working out, tik-toking, building themselves into a brand, one-night standing, and the like, bypassing natural lows with the creation of artificial highs.
An increasing number, though, are looking for that ineffable something—that sense of one’s life having purpose despite its also having an expiration date—through the collection of spiritual knowledge and mystical experiences. Rarer is it these days to find someone who hasn’t tried at least one session of yoga, meditation, breathwork, tai chi, reiki, ecstatic dance, plant medicine, sound healing, tarot pulling, or shamanic journeying.
Almost no one, though, not even the self-proclaimed spiritual folk, appear to be satisfied with a quiet, unimpressive life immersed in nature and prayer anymore. We’re all too busy seeking! Who among us are willing to completely walk away from the screens and influencers and breaking news to follow the happy rhythms of the sun and moon, not in the least bit tempted to try the latest spiritual technique or wellness tip, free from any and all need to share what we’ve been up to?
In part, this is because western culture—whose influence is now global—has browbeaten the population into equating “feeling alive” with “having an adrenaline rush,” “being wise” with “having spiritual experiences,” and “being fulfilled” with “staying and presenting happy.” More is more! Alas, such perspectives and pursuits are actually the causes of one’s misery, not their solutions.
To explain why, one first needs to understand the mechanics of one’s mind as it frantically searches for meaning through experience, and tries to escape death and irrelevance through creation. Let’s run another thought experiment.
Imagine your ideal life. Really go into your own hopes and dreams. I’ll wait.
What food would you eat? American food? Indian food? What if you could stay healthy and sexy, while still eating whatever you wanted? Remember, this is your ideal life here. Would you then opt for mounds of beef jerky, liters of lattes or piles of Twinkies? Would you decide to transcend the need for food altogether?
And what would you do with your time, here in this utopia of yours? Would you choose to work? What if you knew that money or technological limitation wasn’t an issue? Would you then travel the world? Go to the moon? Would you build an tech empire or a bird-box or a commune? Teach and inspire future generations? Trek through the Amazon and discover a new species of beetle?
And what would your relationships look like? Would you have a lot of friends? Would everyone be happy to see you? Would you be constantly filled with social interaction or would you have more time and space to yourself? What if you were free to have as many lovers as you wanted? Better yet, what if people respected and admired you? What if they truly cared about what you had to say?
In this Universe, you have maximum potential. A million cars and a billion followers, if that’s what you want. No more financial or bodily or relational problems, if that’s more appealing. Whatever, wherever, whenever.
To the uninitiated, such fantasies may sound a paradise, but something about the scenario becomes deeply unsettling the more one meditates upon it. Sure, avoiding starvation, living creatively, and feeling loved are all undeniably great things, and what the future hopefully holds more of. But then again…how many roller coasters, orgies, words of affirmation, countries visited, views, cheeseburgers, lines of cocaine or cups of Ayahuasca, Lamborghinis, olympic medals, influence, and trillions of dollars could anyone handle before existential doubt crept in? What’s the point of all these peaks in and of themselves? 
# Now You See It

To highlight what’s causing the ontological ‘ick’, and why such a dream is a fool’s Shangri-La, look no further than to the Universal reality of impermanence, or annica as the cool kids call it. Impermanence needs no introduction for you are already intimately familiar with it. What happened to the version of you at five years old? What about dinner last week? Impermanence is so near and dear that the moment you’ll have read and processed this next word, it’ll be gone—poof! It’s easy to see (but surprisingly hard to remember) that each moment of experience (yes, even this one!) is dissolving in mid-air like steam rising from a tea cup. Sure, you can try to resurrect a thing through its re-enactment, but that wouldn’t be precisely the same moment, for the subatomic particles, context and mind will have changed between takes. Besides, that would end up disappearing instantaneously, too.
All experiences are transitory in nature, even the life-altering, spiritual ones made cozy in memory and that some build their entire identity around. One of the Buddha's chief disciples captured this perfectly when asked to summarize the dharma (teachings of the Buddha) in one sentence: “That which has the nature to arise also has the nature to pass away.” No matter how high you get, or how well Crypto is doing, or how full-body an orgasm becomes, or how many followers and wealth you attract, or how beatific your visions, or how quiet your mind, or how many people you help, or how optimistically you think, or how magnanimous your character…it won’t last. All of it—all of it—will fade, decay, implode or be dismantled just like the rest of the phenomenal Universe. Sure, this fact also technically means that all of life’s unpleasantries will end, too. But I doubt that fact makes the mind feel better about its short and often painful existence.
The problem doesn’t go away in the face of philanthropy or service. Even if one solves the world’s greatest problems—the homeless homed, the rainforest saved, the wars stopped, the elites dethroned, the ignorant enlightened—the solver still has to live inside their own mind afterwards. And then what? Colonize the stars and create infinite abundance for all beings across space and time? Okay, and once that is accomplished? Enter your villain era, create a mess of it all, and then restart the whole process…? Even if existentialism somehow evades detection for a few trillion years, eventually it will be caught and felt and pondered.
Having a unique and immutable soul does not help the situation as much as one might hope, either. Just as the never-to-be-replicated-again falling snowflake is bound to melt back into water, and water to evaporate back into clouds, the soul is destined to return to its Source. The soul is only permanent insofar as it holds a likeness to its Creator—its presence being beyond time—not that it itself has a kind of separate or permanent identity that persists outside of the Dream. And yet the spiritual seeker routinely takes the soul to be what they are, imbuing it with the same qualities of selfhood they wished to transcend in their ego. This is revealed in those who like to use the phrase my soul, rather than referring to it as I or the soul. Who is the me that claims the soul in the first place? Even here—in one of the more refined spiritual concepts—the mind is doing what it always does: trying to find something permanent to cling to in the face of impermanence, the soul becoming yet another object for the ego to claim dominion over.
Impermanence is the reason for the pesky “is this it?” thought accompanying anyone who really considers the end-game of all their desires being borne out. It’s also the unexamined crack in the philosophy of “life is short, so make it count.” Count towards what, exactly? If a thing’s destiny is always to end, why begin at all? What meaning could this, or any experience for that matter, hold in the face of total erasure? Surely there is a more satisfying and substantial answer than just you shifting attention towards doing and becoming something else.
The honest facing of this—that nothing can be held onto or made secure—tends to generate two different responses in people: the first is a kind of immature ‘fuck it, nothing matters anyway’ nihilism that leads to either hedonistic escapism (oh well, I may as well play video games all week), depressive paralysis (I’m just going to mope around until my AI chat bot tells me what to do), or irate revolt (screw them, they did this to me!) And the second is a gnawing restlessness that drives one to try even harder to make something stick, seeking for that one experience or achievement or relationship that will actually last. 
It’s not uncommon for the new spiritual initiate to spastically toggle between the two depending on the hour, day, week, or year.
Both responses, different as they seem, are based on the same problem of suffering. Not the suffering from a plump mosquito bite or terminal diagnosis—though, those count, too—but a deeper, more pervasive suffering that colors even the most decent of moments. This is the “something’s not quite right” background hum that people in the Western world have gotten so accustomed to that it no longer even registers as suffering anymore—it’s just life. Similarly, both responses share another quality in that neither actually helps the situation: numbing and lashing out don’t make impermanence any less true, nor does grasping harder make anything more permanent. Sorry.
There’s actually something happening underneath the hood in your favor here—the Good News, as it were—but before getting there, let’s put on our ’big-boy pants’ and get even more honest with ourselves, for the mere fact that an experience is impermanent is not the ultimate cause of one’s misery, only the proximate. To better understand suffering, and therefore to overcome it, one needs to first study the anatomy of desire, for desire is the engine of action, and action the cause of manifestation, and manifestation the mother of impermanence.
Thirst Trap

Simply put, desire is the wanting of an experience—any experience—to either begin or end. ‘I want this to happen or not happen;’ ‘I want this to end or not end;’ ‘I want this to have happened or have never happened.’ 
Desire can arise towards literally anything: for an object, a person, a place, an idea, a memory, a revelation, a feeling state, a specific outcome, a version of yourself that doesn’t exist yet, forgiveness, someone’s approval, heaven, death, vindication, and so on. And while it’s a common trope to hear in early-stage Buddhist circles that “desire is the root of all suffering,” this is not precisely so, for one can think of many desires that arise without suffering riding shotgun. An innocent and detached desire to know what time it is, for instance, really has no implications on suffering. It is only when desire is made stronger—when it turns into what Buddhists call taṇhā (thirst) and then upādāna (clinging)—that it morphs into misery.
Taṇhā, or thirst, can be thought of as having two forms: craving and aversion. Likes and dislikes, in a way. Read carefully: either you want reality to include something you believe it lacks (but I wanted a scone!) or you want reality not to include something you believe it has (but I didn’t want this salad!) Every experience of suffering includes both; any craving for a scone necessarily hides an aversion towards that which is believed to be standing in its way—permission, price, availability, this salad, and so on. Similarly, any aversion towards the salad requires a thought that it should be different—saltier, fattier, sweeter, scone-ier—which is craving.
Though the above statements are so obvious as to seem offensive at the intellectual level, one becomes increasingly humbled when observing how often they are overlooked on the actual level. That is, as profound and liberating as it is to realize that one’s dissatisfaction is 100% of the time, without exception, caused by their thirst and clinging for reality to be different than how it is, it is equally as painful to discover how frequently one does so anyways. One can ingest the entire Pali cannon, and all its accompanying sutras, and yet still fail to digest its wisdom.
In defense, such endless yearning is not conscious, and therefore nobody’s fault. Most cravings and aversions slide by undetected not because you’re a wretched little sinner, but because your mind is not yet sharp enough to detect their feeling-tone, or vedana. Much like the saturation of a color determines how vivid or muted it appears against its background, the vedana of an experience affects how much it stands out from the rest. An experience with a strong vedana—the stubbing of a big toe or the delivering of an important public-facing keynote—is unmistakable compared to their weaker counterparts—a feather brushing the elbow, say, or walking past a stranger in the grocery store.
It is only due to modern life having become so noisy, colorful and adrenalized that the mind’s sensitivity and capacity for discerning feelings is at an all-time low. As such, it takes real, deep, honest self-inquiry to discover (let alone admit) that one’s experience, even when it appears quite ‘good’ on the surface, is actually frothing with ‘not enough-ness’ underneath. The ego is masterful at presenting one’s taṇhā—cravings and aversions—as neutral observations.
This is especially the trap for the beginner and intermediate spiritual seeker, who, after making some headway in healing and surrendering, falsely assume they have discovered a stable state free from such clinging. This isn't to say that that initial peace is false—only that staying humble in the face of what one believes one has attained serves the seeker well, for the Path, and its companion states of contentment, will continue to deepen for some time to come. What one considers peace today may very well be seen as suffering tomorrow. Besides, even if such a moment is authentic peace, in virtually all such cases it is a temporary moment of balance that will soon exit stage left. And when it does, the seeker who has identified with being the one who has found the peace, is sure to experience disappointment.
In general, the more unconscious the mind, the longer its list of desires, and the more specific its demands for how the Universe should bring them about. For example, without the wisdom of impermanence, one’s travel bucket list may very well exceed one hundred locations, along with the rather-precise hopes for what each destination will look like, who will accompany them there, how transformed they'll feel upon return, and so on.
One of the many ways such a mindset backfires is that because the phenomenal Universe has an infinite number of forms it can take—endless waves reconfiguring themselves into new patterns—the list of one’s desires can therefore also be infinite. You can want the same pair of shoes in red, blue, silver, tie-dye, or any other conceivable color. And you can want those same pair of shoes with infinite combinations of laces, soles, materials, and stitching patterns. Multiply that across every object, every experience, every relationship, every outcome, and the result is an ever-expanding catalogue of preferences that could never be fully satisfied, even across trillions of lifetimes.
Not to mention: as society evolves, so do the desires of the people inhabiting it. It would have been impossible to want the sleek new solar panel back in the 1320s, for knowledge of materials science and manufacturing weren’t enough to conceive of one being possible. They were too busy dreaming of a sturdier plow or a sharper scythe to care about more efficient ways of converting photons into electricity (terms that would have meant nothing to them). Similarly, the same genetically-scripted alpha males of the 1700s who loved their powdered wigs, silk stockings, and embroidered coats would view those very garments as “beta male behavior” today. 
Annica strikes again!
We are not so dissimilar from people of that age. The vast, vast majority of what is considered ‘imagination’ today is actually just different permutations of what’s already been seen or heard yesterday. [Examples]. [Pivot to desire].
In other words, desire doesn't just multiply—it shape-shifts with culture, ensuring the mind always has fresh fuel for its dissatisfactions. Even as the Universe stretches itself in ways incomprehensible to science today—unlocking time travel, telepathy, interstellar colonies, clones, and digital immortality—without spiritual evolution the mind will continue wanting the next frontier thing, never satisfied, immune to the common sense that it is effectively back in the same place as it was thousands of years prior, just with shinier (and often more dangerous) toys. 
Withdrawals

In truth, taṇhā, or the thirsting for experience to be different than how it is, being the root of suffering, while completely accurate in one sense, does not paint the full picture on a subjective level. It is only by inspecting why one wants an experience that the mechanics are revealed.
As it turns out, any desire for experience is really a desire for sensations in disguise. Read that again, because it may be the most important sentence in this chapter. The desire for the arrival or cessation of an experience in and of itself does not cause suffering; rather, it’s the craving and aversion at the sensation level—known as kāma-taṇhā—that does.
To understand why this must be so, one need only turn inwards and play the ‘So That Game’. The rules are simple: (1) pick one of your desires, any desire, (2) state it out loud and add “so that” at the end, (3) fill in the blank, and then add more and more “so thats” until you can go no further, and you have discovered, at bottom, what it is you really want from said desire.
For example, one of my remaining desires is that this book be read by thousands of people. To play the game I would just add “so that” until I can go no further. I want this book to be read by thousands of people so that the ideas can spread far and wide so that seekers do not end up transforming in what I believe is the ’wrong direction’ so that the world quickly becomes harmonious so that I can experience such a harmonious world in this lifetime so that I can experience peace…
It can take some digging and radical honesty, but what the seeker inevitably comes in contact with is that any desire, be it superficial or deep, apparently selfish or apparently selfless, is ultimately a desire to start (or stop) feeling certain sensations. You say you want a scone, but really you want the sensations you assume will arise from eating said scone—the rush, the bubbling, the radiating. You say you don’t want that salad, but really you just don’t want the sensations you assume will arise from eating the salad—the strange texture, the ‘yuck’, the ache in the belly. Seen clearly, thoughts of scone and salads are symbolic, not literal—the wanting of them being psychological stand-ins for something more physiological, which are the sensations they generate.
This can seem like an arbitrary and unimportant distinction at first. I can assure you, it’s not. In fact, this understanding, once fully embraced, changes the context of all one’s experience, adding a new dimension of understanding to every single preference one claims to have, every single decision one makes, and every single interaction one partakes in.
Oh...and it provides an escape hatch out of suffering. More on that later.
One reason the ‘So That’ game can be so powerful is because it reveals our fundamental error. Inspect any ‘So That’ chain carefully enough and conclude that, actually, all one is really doing is unskillfully holding themselves hostage from experiencing a feeling until the original outcome, often an ill-defined and nebulous one, has manifested. ‘I want a longterm partner so that I can raise kids so that I can feel fulfilled’ actually means ‘I will only allow myself to feel fulfilled once I have a longterm partner.’ ‘I want the high-ranking position so that people will think I'm successful so that I'll be respected and admired so that I can stop feeling inadequate’ means ‘I will not let myself feel adequate until I reach a high-ranking position.’[1]
Because most such outcomes are vague and do not materialize ”on-time,” the aspirant is then left feeling impatient and restless, incessantly stalking their ambiguous idea of success until it at least ‘kinda happens’ and they receive their long-awaited (but impressively short-lived) prize: a surge of feel-good brain chemistry. This is the game business moguls, self-help wizards, and even some so-called manifestation experts try to help people get better at: how do I work hard in the right ways for just long enough so that reality conforms to my will, and I get to feel good again?
Sometimes, though, the coveted outcome is never achieved, and one’s mind is left in a perpetual state of dissatisfaction and frustration, locked out of its desired feeling forever, blaming itself or others for its shortcomings and failures (unless, of course, one moves the outcome goalpost to reignite hope and inspiration, in which case the whole cycle starts over again.) But even in the best case scenario—when this strategy works as planned, the outcome secured and the feeling felt right on schedule—all it does is end up confirming one’s false assumption that outcomes cause feelings, instead of the more accurate one that beliefs cause feelings.[2]
Relying upon sensations to lead one away from suffering and towards happiness is even more foolish than relying upon outcomes to do the same, for sensations are even more fleeting—even more obviously impermanent—and always come with a distinct and noticeable crash. This can be understood in any number of ways. In spirituality, for example, sensational ups being paired with sensational downs is a consequence of duality—the idea that the phenomenal world is actually just a play of light and shadow, things being unable to exist in relativity without their opposites (heads without tails, loudness without silence, health without disease, etc).
In mathematics, sensational swings are nothing more mystical than regression to the mean, a term that refers to the tendency for values to move back toward the average over time. Such statistics reveal that anything that can be measured and plotted—from stock prices, average points per game and breath rate—swivel above and below its own self-contained average.
In physics, the view of impermanence is again different and useful. Here the Universe is said to be made up of countless wavelets fluctuating at all different frequencies, amplitudes, and wavelengths. These waves or vibrational patterns are defined by their relative peaks and valleys, which oscillate back and forth over a period of time. As such, there is strictly no such thing as a permanent peak, for every crest necessarily implies a trough, and every trough a crest. 
And finally, in neuroscience, such a sensational crash is said to be due to the combination of homeostasis plus a process called hedonic adaptation—the nervous system's built-in tendency to return to baseline after any pleasure spike. When you first bite into a salty, fatty dish, say, the tongue’s taste receptors fire intensely, triggering a cascade of dopamine release in the brain's reward centers. But within seconds—literally seconds—those same receptors desensitize. The neurons that were joyously screaming “yeehaw!!” start to quiet down, because the receptors are fatiguing and the dopamine system recalibrating. Indeed, by the third or fourth bite, the sensation has dulled considerably (unless you drown that sucker in even more sauce!!), and by the time you've finished, you might barely even register the taste at all. 
It works like this because the sensory nervous system evolved to detect changes in the environment, not to maintain constant signals about static conditions. A hottub stops feeling hot after a few minutes just like a loud noise stops being jarring if it's continuous. This is a feature, not a bug—the system is designed to alert you when something shifts so that the organism can respond appropriately to threats or opportunities.
Put differently, constant stimulation, no matter how pleasant, eventually becomes invisible to the very receptors meant to detect it. This can explain a lot, including why even the wealthiest and most famous get unhappy. Because the mind only ever measures experience based on its past, were the baseline to get pulled up (a bump in salary, a personal record, a new partner), and the wisdom of impermanence left behind, one would then begin comparing experience to a new perceived normal, elated at the relative highs and depressed at the relative lows, very much ignorant that the real problem remains unaddressed. [3]
All of this is bad news for anyone seeking fulfillment through experience and sensation (aka the vast majority of humanity), for they always end up right where they started—dissatisfied. And it’s doubly bad news for the self-proclaimed, spiritual ‘goddesses’ and ‘kings’ who misrepresent the teachings of tantra—practices designed to transmute sensual energy—to justify their ego’s insatiable hedonistic urges, for they end up both dissatisfied and having missed the point of Awakening.
But why are certain sensations wanted or not wanted in the first place? Why does it matter if the heaviness goes away or the expansiveness comes back? Because actually, in peeling back the psyche once more, underlying one’s craving or aversion towards a particular sensation is the even more fundamental desire.

Enough!

The next time one of your desired experience comes to fruition examine your mind-body system closely and find not just the feelings of joy, say, or pleasant tingling, but something vastly more subtle—this sense of dropping back and basking in…something.
That something is called contentment, or sukkha. Sukkha is not like the golden tremors of ecstasy or the gentle swells of pride—sensations that can be felt in one or more parts of the body. It’s more accurate to say that contentment is a mental state—a feeling of mind, rather than of body. In its purest form, it is a state completely free from thirsting, clinging, rejecting or hating—aka free from taṇhā and upādāna. Contentment is without preference, expectation, or need.
Contentment comes around often, though mostly briefly, and almost always unrecognized. Unrecognized because, again, contentment is not a feeling of relief or absence of pain, for it can be known even in the direst, most unpleasant of circumstances. It is simply ‘enoughness’, a very inconspicuous and overlooked flavor of experience that most often arises in the mind during the attainment of a desired feeling state. If one is craving an orgasm, and that orgasm then occurs, contentment is there. If one wishes that the feeling of low back pain go away, and it does, contentment is there. Contentment, basically by definition, is when the search is over and the treasure found, if only for now. 
Actually, if it’s helpful, one can think of contentment as being synonymous with not wanting, for not wanting is where one naturally and obviously lands after wanting has been satiated. Ironically, this implies that what one actually wants, deep down, is to not want! Peculiar though that statement is, it is useful to momentarily look within and reflect upon whether this holds true experientially. Peer past all your desires for experience, for control, for pleasure, for being and becoming, and discover that, actually, there is only ever one desire: to be content. To recognize this moment as enough. To not want anything at all.
Simply reading those words is not sufficient to transfer their intended wisdom over. Instead, all such spiritual realizations need to be had at a root operating system level—at the subjective level—if they are to inspire anything useful. It is therefore recommended to play the ‘So That Game’ again, perhaps in a journal this time to slow down and capture the rapid-fire thoughts, asking why you want the feelings of peace or joy or adequacy or safety in the first place? What purpose are they serving? In doing so, I suspect you will have the same epiphany described above: what feels so good about getting the sensations you want is actually the cessation of wanting them.
Why? Because wanting but not getting is very uncomfortable, physiologically speaking. Wanting to pee, wanting to travel Japan, wanting world peace. All desires, no matter how altruistic their core, create an itchiness or pressure in the body that slowly builds until some form of resolution occurs.
Most people take the long road of resolving these desires, often through engineering certain experiences which are believed to produce the ideal sensations. Want to stop feeling terrible? A luxury car oughta help! Now just work hard for a while, make a ton of money, and then maybe one day check it off the bucket list. Want to feel deep, sustained peace? Try enlightenment! Just renounce the worldly life, enter the monastery, and then maybe one day it’ll all click. The wiser discover a loophole: by realizing that it's not the luxury car or enlightenment that is wanted, but rather the not wanting that arises in the midst of pleasant sensations, one can then choose to surrender the car or search for enlightenment on the spot, and in doing so, receive what one has been after all along.
Is this starting to make any sense? You can just skip the whole circus by letting go of the wanting right now. And by doing so you’ll have solved your problem just as effectively as if you had achieved the outcome. Brilliant!
Our plan is foolproof except for two teensy, tiny details. The first is that it’s bat-shit-crazy. Who in their right mind would give up not having the thing in exchange for not wanting the thing?! One would have to be absurdly fed up with the game of cat and mouse—desire and manifestation—in order to just drop it all. And the second is that wanting to not want is still wanting. The desire to let go of desire is itself a desire. And reminder: that desire breeds the very suffering one was hoping to transcend by letting go of the initial desire!
Oops!
Despite these speed bumps, surrendering desire is still a project worth taking on, for unlike literally everything else in the phenomenal world, not wanting (aka contentment), once found, can be made permanent. And that’s because not wanting is free from the rules of duality: the lows following the highs. Yes, wanting always begets more wanting, but, for reasons that will soon be made clear, not wanting doesn’t the same. There is a loophole by which contentment can actually give birth to itself over again and over again, endlessly, without fail.
To help you see how this might be so, consider another thought experiment. Let’s say that each moment of one’s life can somehow be given an Objective Rating of Happiness—ORH—on a scale from 1 → 10, where the bounds are constrained by one’s most (10) and least (1) happy moments on record. Of course, things are much messier when taking into account the fact that terrible memories often end up being comical, while happy memories can be made sour, so in this thought experiment we will take a measurement during the actual moment of life. Let’s imagine you have an extremely experience—one with an ORH of 9.5. Understanding regression to the mean (as briefly described above), statistically speaking, your peak experience is far more likely to be followed by a down than an up. Same with so-called negative experiences. If you hit a 1.3 on the ORH scale, things can really only get better[4]. 
Of course, most experiences fall in between these extremes, so it should be to no one’s surprise to find the majority of waking life punctuated by dull, ordinary moments. In fact, the math works out such that a human life has an almost identical number of upswings as downswings. It is only because emotional experiences graft themselves so strongly onto the memory and subconscious that one reports and expects a world overflowing with extremes. In real life, however, the vast majority of what one will do be pretty damn average—washing dishes and popping pimples in the mirror. A rather unremarkable existence, if we’re being honest.
Now, if the tendency is to compare current feelings to previous ones, even a small dip in ORH will disappoint. An ice cream cone after a roller coaster ride just doesn’t hit the same as an ice cream cone after a scraped knee. And again if one measures life point by point, one might feel pretty good about this state-change. 
But there is another axis to this chart that is so often overlooked—that of contentment—whether or not one is truly equanimous with what’s arising, be it happy, sad, terrified, or some shade in-between.

While on the surface it can seem like there are degrees of contentment—i.e. one can be slightly more content in this moment compared to the next—this is an illusion. What’s really going on is that there one toggles between light contentment and dark non-contentment so quickly as for one’s experience of it to seem grey.
To state it more plainly, contentment doesn't depend on where you are on the happiness scale. A 9.5 experience can come with contentment or without it. A 1.3 experience can come with contentment or without it. The ORH will inevitably fluctuate—duality and regression to the mean guarantees it—but contentment doesn't have to. Why? Because contentment isn't a position on the happiness scale; it's a meta perspective towards the position itself. Being okay with this moment doesn't necessitate being not-okay with the next one. Contentment can perpetuate itself precisely because it asks for nothing, depends on nothing, and therefore can't be disappointed by anything.[5]
To view it from a slightly different angle, it can be said that contentment transcends duality—the requirement of opposites—because it exists prior to the pleasant-unpleasant distinction. Enough isn’t some abstract middle point between happiness and sadness, but the very ground upon which both stand. Sure, scientists can map its neural correlates—the specific brain states and firing patterns associated with what people call “feeling content”—but contentment itself can't be found in any brain scan, for it’s a felt quality, a quality which lives in subjective experience, not in the neurons themselves. You can measure the physical mechanisms that accompany contentment, but you cannot weigh, photograph, or dissect contentment itself, for it exists in awareness, which is of a different order than materiality.
Believing that the arrival or cessation of particular sensations is the precursor to contentment is not just wrong—it’s self-confirming. If after thousands of clothing purchases, you still experience that oh-so glorious moment of not wanting right after (along with that brief sparkle of sensational glitter that comes with it), the mind will forge a strong connection between the two: “Oh…I guess buying things equals contentment?!” Such is the never-ending game of seeking through acquisition—scratching that itch will always feel good in the moment, but doing so never address the source of the itch, and is thus bound to be replaced by a new one sooner or later.[6]
Seen together, these realizations can be personally transformative, if one lets them. It opens the door to other possible solutions for accessing contentment besides the Sisyphean or Midas-like manifestation of one’s desires. What if contentment were possible even in the midst grueling work or terminal illness? What if contentment were possible even while one does absolutely everything in one’s power to help relieve the world of its suffering? What if contentment were possible without the perfect life?
What matters most is that you are satisfied with your life, not that it look a particular way. Simple enough in the abstract, but unspeakably challenging to embody. The habit of wanting these sensations and experiences over here at the unavoidable price of having to hate those sensations and experiences over there are embedded so deeply in the psyche as to take a wild and vivid hero’s journey to uproot. 
But such is the promise of living a truly spiritual life—that one may no longer need to “feel alive” to enjoy what’s already here, to “have awesome experiences“ to cultivate wisdom, or to “be fulfilled” in order to be content.
Progress 

As one’s understanding of the Path deepens, seekers can look forward to a number of checkpoints confirming that their realization is growing stronger and more stable, as well as a number of obstacles and pitfalls assaulting the seeker in their quest to understand what is Real.
Before Awakening, there is absolutely zero recognition that one’s desires for sensations are contributing to one’s misery at all, let alone that they are entirely culpable. Having and fulfilling desires are seen as the only worthwhile past-time. Such a person might occasionally partake in activities that temporarily withhold their own needs to meet someone else's—charity work, acts of service—but mostly this is done in hopes of receiving some future payback, keeping score of who has done what and whom owes whom, not out of unconditional love where it seen that all is already repaid. At this stage, there is no space between thinker and thoughts. It can even be said that such a mind does not even really suffer, for there is effectively no self-awareness at all, no higher perspective available by which one can even stand and view their suffering from. Life is simply a series of actions and reactions with literally everything confirming one’s victimized viewpoint. The world is happening to them, and if only circumstances would change, they'd finally be happy.
And then the Spark—the start of the journey—characterized by the courageous realization that one’s miseries have more to do with what’s happening “inside” one’s mind than “outside” one’s mind (though, eventually, all is seen to be inside one’s mind). At first, the seeker accepts this fact more at an intellectual level than an experiential one, but over time, especially after consuming some self-help and spiritual content, the ratio flips. Day by day one comes to know—intimately, in their own words—that the very suffering they were trying to overcome by wrestling reality into submission is a direct result of them having resisted the fact that Reality cannot be pinned.
Even so, the beginner sees only the briefest of glimpses of how their defiled mind is contributing to their own madness, still prone to outbursts and finger-pointing. As such, the fact that purification—the lessening of one’s cravings and aversions—is both possible and beneficial is mostly forgotten about, which means the seeker continues their same, rather unremarkable and unhappy experience of life that was present before the Awakening.
As far as obstacles are concerned, doubt is most present with regards to desire and impermanence. Beginners along this section of the Path will often ask themselves: ‘Is my releasing this attachment really worth doing?’ ‘Will surrendering my wanting actually work?’ ‘If everything is impermanent, why focus on letting of craving and aversion at all? After all, aren’t those impermanent, too?’ Having come from a world of complicated solutions, the beginner is skeptical that the end of suffering is as easy and straightforward as it sounds.
As such, one major pitfall is that anything promising salvation through acquiring, achieving, or seeming a certain way is still very alluring and convincing to the mind. It takes either a lot of reinforcement in the opposite direction—reading calibrated books, daily prayer and meditation, attending retreats with genuine teachers—or a lot of willpower to avoid such programming.
Mid-Journey is marked by the falling away of such doubt, comprehending that craving and aversion is truly what keeps one tied to suffering. This is the intermediate stage as it relates to knowledge—being able to explain how, in all areas of life, suffering is present because of kāma-taṇhā. Seeking attention? Craving sensations. Thinking it’s too hot? Aversion to sensations. Wanting the experience to last just a tad bit longer? Craving sensations. Hating being the one who has to heal the lineage? Aversion to sensations. Yearning enlightenment? Craving sensations. Resisting resistance? Aversion to sensations.
Indeed, at this stage, one will have released enough preferences to know just how freeing non-clinging to sensation really is, even when that non-clinging is unfamiliar and therefore brutally uncomfortable. Here, true contentment—the kind that rests upon nothing but itself—can be felt, if only momentarily. As such, the lows one inevitably runs into are generally accepted, even welcomed, if only because one sees the utility and necessity in feeling them to get back to their newly-found palace of balance.
The doubt one faces here is in thinking there might not be an end destination at all. Perhaps healing is eternal, and the mind can never be made truly free? Perhaps all striving is a waste of time? The ego shields itself from annihilation by turning the teaching of impermanence against the seeker—'If even my progress is temporary, what's the point of trying?' At this stage, ironically, one also becomes particularly susceptible to the tantric-style philosophy suggesting that the human incarnation is entirely about embodiment and feeling—that life is all about the senses. On the surface this seems entirely plausible, especially given that teachings’ helpful emphasis on the non-resisting of emotions. But such a school of thought can also be seen to downplay what one may witness beyond the veil, beyond the senses. As every holy text emphasizes, the senses are fallible and misleading. The promise of taking a human form is to include and transcend them—leaving the sensing to the animal kingdom—to view reality and Divinity from an entirely different strata of vibration.
In the advanced stage, on the final ascent towards Bliss, the tide turns again, the seeker seeing with remarkable clarity just how bound the mind has become, and all the myriad ways such a mind has contributed to the evils of the world. One is confronted with a shocking repulsion toward their own remaining defilements. It is as though the lights were being turned on for the first time, revealing just how pervasive and ugly the patterns of greed and hatred truly are.
One then begins to see whatever arises in experience—pleasant or unpleasant, desired or resisted—not as a “fun way of filling time,” but rather as the very fuel that will burn away the wanting itself. In other words, whatever remains of one's karma—whatever desires and aversions still appear in consciousness—is recognized as the very curriculum that will free the seeker once and for all. Each craving that arises is an opportunity to release it. Each aversion a chance to surrender. On and on until, hopefully, closure.
One's destiny and the pace at which understanding deepens also become more and more accepted, for in Truth, one comes to find that sensations, and their allowance, have never been controllable in the first place. The illusion of managing one's inner weather through external manipulation is seen through completely. The delusion simply breaks apart of its own accord. It is also here that one becomes willing to release the deepest of addictions—those of base sensuality (like eating and having sex), knowing, being, and becoming.
Fortunately, because contentment is now (intellectually) accepted to be infinite, and the peace of God far better than any conceivable worldly pleasure, all such apparent highs lose their appeal, and the letting go of them becomes much easier. Unfortunately, the gap between intellectual understanding and full embodiment now becomes agonizingly obvious. One knows that kama-taṇhā begets suffering. One knows that all such things are anicca. And yet the old patterns persist, the preferences continuing to arise at every turn.
This creates an excruciating tension—yearning to finally be free from the tyranny of desire, coupled with the humbling recognition that subtle grasping remains and one is powerless to speed up its dissolution. One can see the last chains clearly, can feel exactly where they bind, can even understand how they'll eventually fall away—but cannot simply will them gone, for the pace is set by forces far beyond the ego’s control. Extreme reverence and awe for those who have crossed this final threshold—the saints, prophets, and avatars—naturally follows.
While the traditional doubts, obstacles and pitfalls of the Path are essentially gone at this point, new ones take their place. For instance, doubt in one's own capacity to fully let go becomes ever-present. While one wholeheartedly believes the promise of the great masters—that a realm beyond lust and resistance is available to exist in while incarnated—one questions their own purity and ability to join them there. This is because, at this stage, the world becomes a hall of mirrors, and all one tends to see is one’s own subtle clinging reflected back. Despite cognitive understanding that the mind can be freed from all such craving and aversion, every moment becomes a devastating recognition of how much remains. It can seem that no matter how deeply one intends to become perfectly non-thirsting and non-clinging, there is always more. The pitfall here, though practically impossible not to feel from time to time, is pure despair—the thought that perhaps one is uniquely incapable of the final letting go.
