It isn’t unusual walk away from an encounter with Buddhist wisdom (Part 1) with a bitter taste in your mouth: “Everything is impermanent,” “the mind is hopelessly brainwashed,” and “I am not real in the way I think I am.” Great, thanks for the heads up.

In fact, you may even want to adopt nihilism in the face of it all, believing that because life and the self are conditioned, your actions don’t ultimately matter. That everything we've discussed so far is accurate and that the opposite conclusion can and should be drawn requires a substantial spiritual journey to see and comprehend. For how can both exist simultaneously? If you’re born to die, and your mind is purely a product of its circumstances, and you are nothing more than a convincing illusion, why should it matter if you adopt the role of selfish sinner instead of selfless saint? Why take accountability at all?

To one who is already charitable and honest, such questions seem absurd—blasphemous, even. You do the right thing because it’s right! You treat others well because that’s how you would like to be treated!

But for one genuinely caught in existential angst—perhaps just discovering that their entire sense of self was constructed on shifting sand—these aren't absurd questions at all. In fact, they're the only questions that matter. If there's no permanent, separate self to benefit from good behavior, and no permanent, separate self to suffer from bad behavior, then on what basis does morality rest? Why care at all?

Some strands of popular Christianity ground morality solely on the Word of God and post-mortem punishment, suggesting to avoid certain actions purely because they will lead the soul to a metaphysical plane filled with something like everlasting torture, fire, cauldrons and goblin sounds. Though compelling, even if that were true (which…hell, it might very well be), the idea that the sole reason you shouldn’t stab that man in the back is because God is judging you like a peeping Santa Claus and could sentence you to roast over a bed of coals like the naughty boy you are is fairly patronizing. You’re telling me the best reason you can come up with to explain why murder is bad is because Moses said God said so?! Surely there must be a more intuitive, resonant answer that doesn’t rely on he said, she said, but on a deeper, heartfelt Truth about how the Cosmos operates.

The karma and rebirth story told by Hindus and Buddhists—that every self-generated action creates an energetic indent that must eventually be ’worked through’ and resolved via incarnation—is a step in the right direction, though it can lack the weight needed to fully endorse conscionable living. While the possibility of spending eternity in hell might be enough to dissuade someone from innocently stuffing their pockets with candy bars from the convenience store, karma is often not, for the mind can easily brush away any such innocuously devious behavior with a shrug of its shoulders and a swiping of the reincarnational credit card: “oh well, just add it to my karmic tab and I’ll get to it next life.”

Worse still, both ideas (when divorced from the deeper truth they're meant to point toward) can be used to make compassion void or pointless. If suffering is divine punishment or karmic payback, well then everyone, including the starving babies in war-torn countries, kinda has it coming, no? That creates a very convenient excuse for people to do nothing in the face of others’ excruciating circumstances. The homeless man? Ah, don’t mind him—he’s just living out his saṃskāras. The abused daughter? Well, yeah…someone’s gotta pay for that whole apple and Eve incident.

Plus, neither framework gives us precise answers on how to navigate the grey areas where life actually unfolds. Lying is always bad…except when it's used to avoid violence. But how can you know for certain that telling the truth will lead to violence? Violence is totally unacceptable…except when it's to protect innocent lives from being stolen. But how can one be sure their violence won’t escalate the situation? Stealing is never okay…except when your sick child needs medicine and you have absolutely no money. But how can you know that stealing is truly the only option, or that getting caught won't make everything worse?

On a more practical level, the real problem for most people isn’t so much navigating genuine moral complexity, but that their mind will seize upon any ambiguities to justify its preferred shortcuts. “I rounded up my volunteer hours because it's basically accurate”; “I didn't mention the dent when selling the car because they didn't ask”; “I kept the extra change because the cashier made the mistake, not me”. The ego will even weaponize spiritual teachings to rationalize itself, dressing up its lies, laziness, violence, greed, and hubris in the very language of liberation: “I’m practicing non-attachment, so no need to feel guilty about my past shady decisions,” or “If everything is impermanent, why should I honor this commitment?”

Even when we think we're living clean, ordinary lives—just trying to stay out of the way— we're still entangled in this problem of right and wrong. We invest in retirement funds that profit from weapons manufacturers companies. We pay taxes that fund drone strikes and corrupt bureaucrats. We nibble on chocolate, scroll on smart phones, and sip on lattes that were made possible by forced child labor.

So if we can't rely on punishment, can't defer to karma, can't trust our own rationalizations, and can't even claim innocence through inaction, what about turning to scripture for clear guidance on how and why to act?

Alas, scripture often reveals more about the reader than Truth, as it can be quoted to support virtually any position. Krishna in The Bhagavad Gita outright urges Arjuna to kill his enemies lest they kill him first, yet also teaches that the wise see the same Self in all beings and that true knowledge means transcending all action. Fight or renounce? To most unspiritualized minds, such directives appear contradictory—irreconcilable even—and give the seeker free reign to either cherry-pick their favorite verses, or conclude that the whole framework is a load of hoo-hah, and that, really, we should all just “do what feels good, man.”

Let me again be clear that morality exists, that living in accord to it matters enormously for one’s spiritual ascent, and that one shouldn’t have to depend upon what the Bhagavad Gita, Buddha or Bible say for guidance (although we should be very grateful for what they do say). Had Moses never received the Ten Commandments from God, or the rishis not preserved the Vedas, would it then have been okay to rape, torture and plunder? Of course not.

But again…why?

## Warped

When you lie, steal, or more generally harm another, the act isn’t actually affecting some fundamentally separate entity—it’s introducing discord into the very thing that you yourself are an expression of. If you had a body with multiple personalities, and one personality hurt another part of the same body, would you say “well, they're different personalities, so no harm done”? Obviously not. The harm is real because the substrate is shared, regardless of how many different experiences of selfhood arise within it.

Put differently, it’s not that defiled acts—big or small—are outside of God (because nothing can be). It’s that any immoral act is like scoring the worst kind of own goal: not only do you pervert that which you are an essential part of, like defecating in the very river you drink from, but the act always ends up boomeranging back around and clipping you in the nuts.

The first of such clips is what happens to your physical body. After you lie to someone, even about something small, there is a brief muscular contraction and heart-rate spike, the body admitting to what the mind denies. This quickly adds up, clogging your energy body and contributing to illness in the process. And then the mental gymnastics begin—having to remember what you said and to whom, managing everyone’s perception of you, and so on. These tensions and stressors are the immediate price of having lied, separate from how it might come back to haunt one in the future. This is a distortion in your field that you now have to spend energy maintaining.[[The obvious counterexamples are the psychopaths and sociopaths who can outperform even a lie detector test. This doesn’t negate the idea; it just makes it more interesting and complex. While perhaps these parasitic individuals are able to momentarily benefit materially from their selfishness, consider the steep costs of having such a tyrannical mind—an inability to feel genuine love or connection, the isolation that comes with treating others as objects, a life of power that lasts a few short decades at most in exchange for never experiencing what makes being human truly meaningful. Better I think to have a moral compass intact but occasionally ignore it than to have a faulty one that dutifully leads you to hell.]]

This in comparison to what happens after an act of sincere generosity—not the ‘look how noble I am’ kind, but one where you helped simply because you could. More often there's an ease that follows, an opening, a sense of contentment that has nothing to do with anyone's opinion of you.

Are we to say that these patterns are merely biological? That the tension from lying and the ease from generosity are yet more conditioned responses, varying by culture, upbringing and DNA, and not a Universe that reveals its moral framework through real-world outcomes? Some say yes: it's all biology. The tension after lying and the ease after generosity are simply evolutionary leftovers—our ancestors who felt these things survived and reproduced, while those who didn’t…well…didn’t. Case closed.

If you choose to believe this, fine. But observe what happens when you actually live that way—how treating morality as an invention, rather than a discovery, erodes any stable ground for you to distinguish between right and wrong. On what basis are you now able to tell the Nazi that his racism is immoral? Who are you to denounce a genocide? Why complain about the politician who lies straight to your face? If what’s right is only in the mind of the beholder, all views and behaviors are now equally arbitrary, and suddenly justification is possible for literally any belief or behavior—yours, mine, the saint’s, and the tyrant’s.

In observing the very long run of biological evolution, one finds the trend actually pointing away from pure self-interest. Single-celled organisms competed until they learned to cooperate, forming multi-cellular life. Insects developed colonies where individuals sacrifice themselves for the hive. Mammals evolved to care for their youngsters, to form packs and feel empathy for their wounded. The loooonnng arc, with its undeniable ups and downs, bends not toward ruthless competition but towards something like a shared mind.

Perhaps, then, cooperation and care are woven into the rules of the Game in the same way aerodynamics is woven into the physical world. The butterfly whose wings align with the laws of lift and drag eventually receives the gift of flight. The human whose behavior aligns with the moral laws of the Universe eventually receives the gift of the peace of God.

The reverse must then hold equally true: misalignment with Truth must create measurable, propagating discord (think how a single inharmonic note makes an entire piano chord sound out of tune, or how pulling one thread warps the whole sweater.) In the realm of spirituality, such discord refers to seeding that which is a lie, however nanoscopic, into an otherwise perfectly coherent Field of consciousness. This seed often ends up sprouting and multiplying, much like a cancer cell which, if the immune system is suppressed, replicates and spreads through its host.

In other words, anything which is not True—anything born from fear—doesn’t tend to stay localized. Unchallenged, its nature is to ripple outwards, creating yet more fear and fragmentation as it expands.

Why is fear not True? For one very simple reason: it is based on separation, and separation, as we’ve already spent time unpacking in Chapter 3, is a lie.

Take the fear of what others might think of me. Such a worry only makes sense if there is a “me” separate from “others”—if their thoughts are entirely foreign powers which can threaten my world. The fear of not getting what I want presumes separateness from what is desired, the self subtly incomplete without it.

“But what about real threats?” the mind might ask. “Surely it's rational to fear the gunman or the apocalypse?” Even these extreme cases rest on the same illusion of separation: a vulnerable me, a hostile them, an unbridgeable gap between. What would there be to fear if one knew that the whole Cosmos was part and parcel of their very being—that in the same way that I am, the mugger and doomsday are, too? To be clear, this recognition takes time to develop and doesn't eliminate wise action—it's entirely possible to calmly hand the thug your wallet or donate money to protect the Amazon rainforest without the anxious spinning of what-if, what-if, what-if, or creating an enemy out of circumstance. It just eliminates the self’s worry that suffering or tragedy means it is being punished or abandoned.

This might sound abstract or even naïve—believing that the Universe is anything but aloof to the human condition. But conceptually it’s incredibly straightforward: in the same way that you at least kind of care about what makes you up (your body’s cells, your mind’s opinions, etc.), it makes sense that the Universe would also care about what makes it up (the souls born from it, the energy sustaining it, and so on). The only reason it feels challenging to trust this is because immature science has conditioned your mind to believe that life somehow emerged from dead, unintelligent matter—a view that makes the Universe seem fundamentally indifferent. The mind argues: “In the past, things didn’t work out for me in the way I wanted them to; therefore the Universe must be indifferent to my circumstances; therefore, I must take charge and always be prepared for the worst.” This not trusting is the very fear that must be uprooted to spiritualize and free the mind from yourself.

Lucky for us, any thought or decision born from fear is immediately met with a cosmic cytokine storm of corrective pressures—a guilt that intensifies until you return what you stole or a persistent whisper that you should call and apologize. These naggings can be overridden, of course, but not without cost. Any lie, especially with respect to being a separate self, is bound to be exposed, for it is, by definition, not reality, and thus needs but one tiny contradictory detail to topple its standing.

Resisting realignment—allowing fear to spawn lie after lie to cover its tracks—generates a lot of unnecessary strain on the system. It’s like trying to hold a beach ball underwater or keep a heavy barbell fixed overhead. Using the ego to fight the laws of gravity or karma doesn’t work forever. Eventually the relentless buoyancy of reality or the inevitable fatigue of the separate self becomes too great, and the whole system either neutralizes or collapses into something much more sustainable and harmonious.[[Lest you think the full correction will happen anytime soon, I will remind you that even by optimistic Vedic astrologer calculations, humanity still has thousands of years to go before reaching the age of Truth and enlightenment. If this feels inconveniently slow, that may be part of the lesson.]]

This process of fear begetting fear plays out on many stages. For example, some humans today avoid the very sun, soil, rivers, and natural sugars their bodies are made from and fueled by, yet praise their processed protein powders, pills, and perfumes which make them sick and tired. Feeling unwell can then seem to further confirm their backwards suspicion that nature Herself is to blame, driving them even more towards synthetic solutions, making them sicker still and tightening the screw with each turn.

The self-proclaimed “awake” ones aren’t free, either, for their engagement and obsession with conspiracies can be just as fear-driven and imprisoning as the sheepish behavior they ridicule. The wisdom of this age is recognizing that evil, being based on the lie of separation, doesn’t need to be fought, so much as starved, for without more fear to feed it, it has nothing to consume but itself.

Still, one might reasonably ask: if fear spreads so easily and effortlessly, what guarantees it won't simply consume everything, everywhere before it starves?

God’s immune system—the Law of karma—cannot be infinitely suppressed. Imagine an elastic rubber band: the further you pull back on it, the harder it wants to slingshot forward. You can maintain the stretch through effort and denial, but that tension never disappears; it only builds and builds until one day…snap.

In the objective world, when you throw a ball in the air, you would say that it must return back to the Earth so that the forces equalize. But if that ball threw itself up in the air—you would say that it must return back to the Earth because that’s its karma. It was always destined to return to its source!

Whatever is set in motion must eventually settle, or else an energetic debt is ‘owed’. Any action based on a lie creates potential energy in the Field that must (eventually) be transformed into the kinetic energy of reparations and healing. On a soul level, this restorative process is known as a spiritual awakening.

Being restored means all defects in a system are stripped down. This can occur gently, piece by piece, or all at once with a single swing of a wrecking ball. Metaphysically speaking, the unique way these imbalances get corrected—the careful replacement of rotting pieces, the massive detonation—is the very life you’re living right now. That which you would resist—the divorce, the diagnosis, the death—are the breaking of the bones so they may be reset correctly. Again, and I feel this must be restated over and over: such a reset is not God’s punishment for personal failings. It’s a grace-filled, systematic preparation which reveals those parts of one’s mind which have been built upon the lie of separation. No need to hold a personal vendetta against the wrecking ball or demolition crew—they’re just doing their job, clearing the way so that a sturdier, more stunning house can get put up in its place.

Given all this, perhaps now we can more easily answer the questions posed at the beginning of this chapter: How can 'doing the right thing' exist in a world filled with impermanence and conditionalities? Because, while yes, everything one sees is fleeting, Truth is not. What’s True is always True—forever and always. And, while also yes, everything depends on prior conditions —your preferences were shaped by culture, your beliefs formed by experience—Truth does not. Whether known or denied, Truth is very the essence of all existence, the very ground from which conditions arose and the very sky into which they dissolve.

And why should it matter if I adopt the role of selfish sinner instead of selfless saint? It matters because virtuous living (the saint's path) aligns with what is always True—that separation is an illusion and all beings share one Source—while the sinner's path is built on the obvious lie—that one exists in a kind of selective vacuum where harming another does no harm to me. One path accords with Reality; the other denies it.

And finally: why take accountability at all? Because that’s the only possible way to align back to Truth—seeing with open eyes the creations one has made out of fear, and committing to live differently as a result.

## Ahimsa | अहिंंसा

Every major religious tradition suggests adhering to an ethical code—the Ten Commandments in Christianity, the Five Precepts in Buddhism, the Quranic prohibitions in Islam, and so on. I adopt the yogic code in this book—the five yamas (restraints or containments) and five niyamas (observances or inner disciplines)—for a few reasons:

For one, they are, I believe, incredibly refined and comprehensive guidelines for right action. Take cleanliness, or saucha as one example. On the surface, it sounds like Mom’s advice to shower daily and keep your fingernails clean. But examine the etymology of the word (śuc is to shine, be clear, or purify) and discover that it is more about reducing signal distortion—keeping the window of body and mind spotless so more light may pass through unobstructed—than obsessing over one’s appearance. Much more important that one’s energetic field remain spick and span than obsessing over de-boogering one’s nose.

Another favorable reason to use the yamas and niyamas is that they are intended to springboard one into expanded and more peaceful states of consciousness. Many Western religions, and even some Eastern ones, advise followers to do good and be righteous without recognizing the full scope of where it will take them—union (Bliss) with God within the human incarnation, not just after death. The yamas and niyamas, on the other hand, live in the more panoptic and ancient system of yoga—a system that’s been stretched and refined over many thousands of years, and has the explicit purpose of purifying and quelling the mind and energy bodies so as to take part in and complete the transformative process of Awakening and reunification with God. The yama and niyamas function is to prevent one’s vital energy from leaking into dead ends—the thousand and one ways the ego bleeds you dry through the papercuts of worry, craving, reactivity, and chaos. Only then can meditation and prayer do the work they were meant to do.

And finally, the yamas and niyamas carry very few impressions or cultural baggage in the Western mind, as compared to other popular religious codes. Consider how the word “commandment” from the Ten Commandments might distort what it attempts to help correct. If God Almighty, the Great Ruler of this and all Universes, commands me to do something, and yet I ignore His call, it would be quite understandable that I receive punishment for my defiance. Yet believing this prevents the deeper lessons from this code to surface: that God is Reality, that Reality is Truth, and therefore that “commandments" are not mandates from a wrath-filled, punitive tyrant who hates freedom, but strong pointer-outers of how to align with, rather than fight against, what is Real. Going against these principles doesn't put you on a jealous God's hit list. It pulls you out of harmony with the coherent whole, creating an imbalance that the Law has no choice but to correct.

With that said, rather than viewing the yamas and niyamas as rules to blindly follow so as to appease a picky Creator, it is more helpful to view them as pressure valves that can be opened or closed depending on how much one wishes to step into or step out of the flow of Life. The first of these pressure valves, and arguably the yama that encompasses all other yamas, is ahimsa. Literally translated, ahimsa means non-violence, though in the yogic understanding violence refers to any action that introduces unnecessary disturbance into the Field—far broader and subtler than simply telling someone “don’t murder.” It is broader because violence also includes attacking, hating, coercing, exploiting, wishing others harm, lying, and stealing—really anything that falls outside of the ‘do unto others as you would have them do unto you’ distinction. And subtler because disturbances can occur on very minute levels, including white lies, complicity in or celebration of others’ violence, speaking from unexamined anger, and accidentally impressing one’s fears into the mind of others.

Ahimsa also includes avoiding violence towards oneself—self-harm, negative self-talk, depriving the body of rest or nourishment, staying in abusive situations, or any action that treats oneself as lesser than another. That one should decide to literally starve themselves in an attempt to quicken their Awakening, as some extreme ascetic practices might recommend, would be breaking this vow just as much as eating a diet full of innocent, factory-farmed baby chicks would.

The mind may convince you that an all-out commitment to ahimsa will yield a very ‘boring’ life, simply because most modern activities and conveniences fall outside its narrow domain. Fair enough. Flying to the islands for your cute vacay dumps thousands of pounds of jet fuel into our airways and collective lungs. Supporting bars and pubs create environments that enable drunk driving, violence, and self-destructive behavior. Your cell phone requires strip-mining rare earth minerals using exploited labor. Golf, coffee, Netflix, driving, festivals, new clothes, the printing and distribution of this book—virtually every engagement has a hand in exploitation, pollution, or destruction.[[Heck, even wiping your ass with toilet paper contributes to deforestation and polluting waterways. Does that mean you should accept a chronically scratchy butthole to stand in solidarity with the Amazon Rainforest?]]

To truly do “zero harm”, then, one would need to grow or forage their own food, make their clothing by hand from locally-sourced materials, never travel beyond walking distance, and consume zero media or entertainment requiring electricity, essentially returning oneself to a pre-industrial subsistence lifestyle.

Actually, even that kind of ludditical existence isn’t technically zero harm, for by simple virtue of being alive, we contribute to death. Our immune cells ‘murder’ billions upon billions of bacteria and viruses daily. Our homes become accidental death traps for flies that wander in and can't find their way out. Our bodies require ripping off the poor limbs of herbs and leafy greens. Our honest speech gets misinterpreted and ends up triggering pain in another. One disciple of the Buddha famously came to him distressed, saying he couldn't even meditate because everywhere he tried to sit, he saw ants which would be crushed beneath him.

Sigh…maybe it’d just be best if us humans were magically wiped off the face of the Earth (can’t commit suicide, since that would be considered self-harm) so as not to contribute to this madness any longer…?

Alas, even this would not be a proper solution, either, for even if we were all to disappear tomorrow, nature would surely continue her bloody dance of transforming bodies into other bodies.[[This reminds me…with regards to eating meat: the line is not as clear-cut as some vegans would like it to be. There are Tibetan monks who are committed to the Buddhist precepts of non-killing, but who require the flesh of the yak to sustain themselves in the Himalayas where little else grows. There is the indigenous hunter who lives off the land, taking game with sincere reverence and gratitude for the cycles of life. And there is the happy, grandmother collecting eggs from her beloved backyard chickens. These scenarios, and many more, present different ethical considerations than the obviously horrendous industrial factory farming system. Not to mention, if eating meat, eggs, or dairy were indeed necessary for health, one could argue it's unethical not to consume them, as depriving oneself or one's children of that essential nutrition would itself be harm. (One needn't worry about this dilemma, for animal products really are not necessary for optimal health—more on this in chapter six.) That said, if one has the option of not contributing to the systematic devastation of billions of murdered and tortured animals—and, let’s be honest, almost everyone reading this does—then choosing to partake anyway out of convenience or habit undoubtedly breaks the ethical code of doing no harm. That argument falls short, however, when noting that plants have not evolved pain receptors (that would be extremely unadvantageous in the face of being unable to move out of the way of munching predators.) These organisms do not experience life in the sensory realm, but rather in the etheric realms where things are very edgeless and dream-like. Losing a limb or dying here is much like losing a limb or dying in a dream— alarming, not ideal, but not painful in the same way that a pig might feel their tail being lopped off or nose being mutilated. Eat up!]]

What to do, what to do?!

Well, intention clearly plays an outstanding role here. Accidental harm is drastically more aligned than deliberate cruelty. That said, the moment you learn about the harm you're causing and continue anyway—rationalizing, delaying, making excuses—the karmic weight shifts away from accidental and towards something closer to reckless disregard.

This doesn’t mean that the one who unknowingly buys chocolate picked by child slaves or unwittingly purchases vegetables sprayed with pesticides is totally off the karmic hook, even while ignorant and innocent on the surface. That which is anti-life creates dissonance in the interconnected Field—a disturbance which can always be felt precisely because who you are is the Field experiencing itself as you. Ready that again to make sense of it. Since it would be impossible for you to exist outside of what is Real or True—only deny or imagine that you do— you always know harm (separation) from harmony (Truth), even if it’s not registered by the conscious mind. It's only because one has lived with internal discord for so long that the sensitivity to its signal has dulled.

This is why cultivating a willingness to discover where one causes harm, and to change immediately upon learning, matters so much for one’s purification of self. The all-out commitment to ahimsa restores your sensitivity to the signal, reversing the dulling and normalization that created ignorance in the first place. The moment you learn that fast food wrappers are coated in forever chemicals is the moment you ditch the drive-throughs forever! Not next month, not after you finish your rewards card. Now! It’s the only way to dis-cord yourself from the discord.

There's a third consideration, though, one that Krishna in the Bhagavad Gita brought wise attention to. Here’s the context: the Bhagavad Gita is the story of an encounter between Krishna —the Supreme Lord appearing in human form—and Arjuna, a warrior-prince who embodies the ideal virtues of a spiritual seeker: devotion, courage, and a genuine desire to do what is right.

The scene opens on a battlefield. Two armies face each other, braced for war. Arjuna stands between them in his chariot, Krishna serving as his charioteer. As Arjuna surveys the opposing army, he recognizes his own relatives, beloved teachers, and childhood friends standing in the enemy ranks. His heart breaks. How can he kill those he loves? How can righteous victory be won through such violence? Overcome with grief and moral confusion, Arjuna throws down his bow and refuses to fight.

Oh, the drama!

What follows is Krishna's teaching—the Gita itself. In it, Krishna actually urges Arjuna to fight, saying that it's actually his duty as warrior to do so, even if it means killing his fellow kinsmen. This often shocks first-time readers, for it seems to directly contradict ahimsa. How can the Supreme Lord advocate for such violence?

The battlefield operates on two levels simultaneously—both literal and allegorical. On the inner level, the relatives and beloved teachers Arjuna faces represent the ego tendencies within one’s own mind: the comfortable patterns, the pleasurable vices, the greed, the unconscious addictions one has lived with, become accustomed to, even enjoyed over the years. Some of these tendencies seem helpful, even enlightened—like justifying credit card debt because “abundance mindset,” or taking advice from the mystical-sounding guru who actually just wants to bang your wife.

But when Krishna sounds his conch shell, the call rings clear: “kill” these tendencies— spiritually awaken, moralize the mind, root out ignorance and unconsciousness and selfishness wherever it hides. This call can be momentarily devastating—it should feel like grief. After all, you're being asked to allow the complete destruction of those parts of the mind which have been identified with and defended—whose entire sense of self has been built around.

Lest you feel like Arjuna and refuse to fight the good fight, keep in mind that these parts would have absolutely no problem killing you, if they could. If one’s mind were to fully surrender to the ego's demands—cravings overpowering reason—the physical form would undoubtedly end up either obese, addicted, or isolated, and then ultimately destroyed. On many levels of the game, the ego isn't a competitor playing by gentleman's rules—it's a lethal adversary unafraid of stooping low, backstabbing, camouflaging, or exaggerating to maintain its grip. It strangles slowly, often pleasantly, but fatally nonetheless—not just the body, but the soul's sensitivity for Truth, connection, and freedom.

Yet Krishna's teaching also addresses the literal battlefield, raising a profound question about the external world: when, if ever, is actual harm—even killing—the righteous action? Krishna's sermon to Arjuna suggests that sometimes, destroying what seeks to destroy consciousness itself becomes a sacred duty: the gardener which removes invasive species that would choke out the ecosystem, the surgeon who’s knife harms tissue to remove cancer, the parent who physically, sometimes violently, restrains the toddler running toward oncoming traffic.

In each case, the apparent harm serves life and consciousness, opposing evil, or that which actively denies or distorts what is ultimately True, which is non-separation. So long as one does not revel in causing harm, and so long as the action arises from necessity rather than hatred, then eliminating what would eliminate becomes a form of duty, or dharma.

Wars against terror, one might argue, fall into this category. Stopping those who would destroy innocent life and impose tyranny through fear sounds reasonable enough. But then again…the crusaders believed they fought for God just as colonizers believed they were civilizing savages just as the Inquisitors believed they were saving souls from the influence of demonic witches just as every tyrant in history claimed to fight evil while perpetrating it. So how can one distinguish between genuine defense of Life versus defense of the ego's caricature of righteousness? How can one tell the difference between Krishna's call to spiritual warfare and the ego's thirst for attack dressed in holy language?

The mind actually cannot possibly solve this riddle, for, as the entire bloody history of our species demonstrates, the intellect, brilliant as it is, can be used to rationalize literally anything. We've used reason to justify slavery, genocide, torture, exploitation, and every conceivable cruelty, all while believing ourselves righteous.

The mind is too easily captured by itself, which is why another virtue is needed, one that transcends the calculating intellect—a safeguard which inherently cuts through the ego's endless capacity for self-deception.

## Īśvara-Praṇidhāna | ईश्वर प्रणिधान

Just as ahimsa can be seen as the yama (restraint) which encompasses all other yamas, Īśvara-praṇidhāna—often taken to mean ’surrender to God’—is the niyama (inner discipline) which encompasses all other niyamas. Literally translated, Īśvara means Lord, organizing principle, or ’that which presides’, and praṇidhāna is a placing down, offering, or releasing into. Put together, then, Īśvara-praṇidhāna means a surrendering toward the governing principle of Reality.

Surrender within this frame does not mean passivity, per se, but rather the release of the compulsive effort to control that which is already being governed (and governed just fine, thank you!) It is the relinquishment of the belief that the individual self is responsible for managing outcomes, correcting the world, and healing itself. It is relaxation based on the fact that whatever intelligence brought the Cosmos into being before the body and mind were born, call it God or the laws of physics, is still very much present as one lives and will remain long after one dies.

Actually, now is the perfect time to discuss in greater depth what is meant by God, for the term has been used scattered throughout this text and will only become more relied upon going forward. In practice, two distinct senses of God have been used rather interchangeably:

The first is God as Creator—the familiar, relational sense of God as emphasized in Christianity and devotional strands of yoga. This is not God as lightning-bolt-thrower, but as omnipotent, governing intelligence: that which creates, sustains, and orders the Cosmos, and with which one can orient, align, and even enter into relationship or Union with. In yogic language, this is Īśvara—Lord of Lords, source of order, the Manifested Absolute.

The second sense is God as Being Itself: the unconditioned ground of existence, beyond form, action, and relation. This is the Unmanifested Absolute, referred to in different traditions as Brahman, the Dao, or the Godhead. This is not a Creator acting within time or caring about its creations, but that which is prior to manifestation altogether—prior not in time, but in ontology and explanation. Such a God has no proper name, for any name would impose limitation on It. It neither Is nor Is Not.

Going forward, I’ll be more careful in maintaining this distinction, referring to the Manifested Absolute as Creator, God, or Īśvara, and to the Unmanifested Absolute as Brahman or Source, depending on context.

Seen clearly now, Īśvara-praṇidhāna refers not to blind submission to a distant deity or dogma, but to a conscious release of agency into the governing intelligence of the Manifested Absolute, or Creation itself. This spiritual surrender is not meant to be a terrifying collapse into an endless, featureless chasm, but a release into the Greater Intelligence that is already holding and directing the world.

This is precisely why this niyama becomes indispensable when facing the practical questions of how to act so as to do the most good with the least harm. Left to its own conditioning, the intellect rationalizes whatever it already wants, and intuition gets distorted by fear and unconscious bias. Īśvara-praṇidhāna offers a third option: releasing the demand to decide from a contracted sense of self and allowing action to emerge from alignment with a larger intelligence—one that sees further, holds more variables, and is far less invested in defending the ego’s narrative.

Exactly how one purifies the mind through praṇidhāna to maintain continuous alignment with Īśvara will be explored in far greater detail next chapter.

## Brahmacarya | ब्रह्मचर्य

The complement to Īśvara-praṇidhāna is brahmacarya (a yama), coming from the root brahman, referring to Brahman—that which Is, the Unmanifested Absolute—and carya, meaning conduct, movement, or way of living. At its most literal and essential level, then, brahmacarya points to movement in alignment with Reality itself: living, acting, and relating in ways that do not contradict what is True. Whenever conduct moves away from what is Here toward what is imagined, exaggerated, or hoped for, energy leaks, clarity diminishes, and harm—subtle or overt—becomes unavoidable.

Seen this way, brahmacarya addresses a different dimension of alignment than surrender. Where Īśvara-praṇidhāna concerns receptivity to divine guidance, brahmacarya concerns the regulation of behavior in response to that guidance—ensuring that conduct does not contradict what has been made clear. Īśvara-praṇidhāna reveals the path; brahmacarya is the restraining of oneself—the discipline—of not stepping off it.

As such, sex soon became the focal point of brahmacarya teachings, for it is arguably the most potentially corrupting and intense energy channels within the human form. This despite the fact that in the seminal text on yoga, the Yoga Sūtras, brahmacarya is mentioned with literally no sexual commentary alongside it—zero mention of intercourse, ejaculation, marriage, or abstinence. Just this: “From establishment in brahmacarya comes vitality (vīrya).”

It should be obvious that sex and sexual energy are not “bad”, for without them Life would not and could not be. It’s just that these forces are very, very (did I mention very) powerful. Powerful enough, in fact, to override reason, distort perception, and reorganize an entire culture’s value system in its image (i.e. the scale of the pornography industry and global economy built around sexualized self-presentation). This makes sexual energy a potentially powerful tool for confirming, rather than dissolving, the ego’s illusion of separation: I am this body here pursuing satisfaction through that other body over there.

When unexamined, sex very easily mutates communion into consumption, and presence into fantasy. For this reason, most spiritual traditions have recommended celibacy outside of marriage—not as an outright rejection of sex, or because it is a trap for impure sinners, but as a protection against one’s own misguided perception. As anyone who has practiced this teaching can dutifully confirm, practicing celibacy is an incredibly helpful and freeing lifestyle, for one closes off any energy valves which would otherwise be spent on the hamster wheel of pursuit or looking good for the camera. Wise restraint on this front prevents the mind from reducing other beings to instruments of gratification, thereby preserving the clarity required to live in alignment with what Is and freeing up vast amounts of life-force for other, more service-oriented tasks.

Of course, under this broader definition, brahmacarya extends far beyond what can be fucked or fooled around with. It also applies to overeating, overtraining, and overstimulating, as well as doomscrolling, obsessive productivity, fantasy loops, reliance on AI, validation-seeking, and spiritual ambition. Basically, adhering to brahmacarya means cutting off any behavior that can used as an escape from what the present moment is actually asking of you, as determined by Īśvara-praṇidhāna.[[Leaving aside the classically harder drugs such as cocaine and heroin, what about the restraint from other intoxicants such as alcohol, weed, caffeine and psychedelics? Alcohol doesn’t require much argument. It’s a consciousness depressant that lowers inhibitions, dulls awareness, and reliably gets used as an escape hatch from whatever the present moment is asking of you. Same with weed, though, which has the added complication of being marketed as a spiritual medicine. Are you escaping or are you engaging? No one can truly say but you. Caffeine deserves a longer look, because it's the intoxicant we've all collectively agreed to pretend isn't one. The truth is we're so chronically exhausted as a society—from poor sleep, depleted soil, ambient anxiety, screen overuse, and adrenal fatigue from previous caffeine—that morning coffee or matcha feels less like a choice and more like a survival tool. Touché. But that doesn't make it brahmacarya. You're still artificially inducing alertness your body isn't naturally producing, still borrowing energy from tomorrow to function today, still using a substance to override what your system is actually communicating (which is usually: please, for the love of Īśvara, rest). The honest spiritual position isn't “caffeine is fine because everyone does it”—it's “I’m leaning on this because I haven't yet built the foundation that would make it unnecessary.” Psychedelics are the most complicated case, and worth dwelling on because they've become something like the official sacrament of the modern spiritual scene. The promise is alluring: skip past years of meditation and pierce the veil directly. And to be fair, real insights and genuine healing absolutely happen on these substances—I'm not going to pretend otherwise. The therapeutic potential for PTSD, treatment-resistant depression, and end-of-life anxiety is well-documented and legitimate. But why, then, were the great yoga masters so emphatically against them? Yogananda advised his disciples to avoid them entirely. Neem Karoli Baba famously told Ram Dass they weren't the “real thing.” Osho warned they could actively impede the natural flowering of awakening. These weren't scolds from religious maniacs—they were loving recommendations from realized beings who recognized that the states induced by these compounds, however profound they feel, are biochemically distinct from genuine Realization. In truth, the deeper problem is actually spiritual dependence. Plant medicines atrophy the very muscles the spiritual seeker is trying to develop. Feeling low-vibration today? Drink some cacao. Trauma resurfacing? Lean on your old friend psilocybin. Need clarity on a life decision? Bit of tobacco oughta help. Each use trains the system to outsource what should be developed internally—the willpower, the discernment, the capacity to sit with difficulty without numbing or escaping. One final call for reducing one’s psychedelic usage is that these substances are extremely rough on the physical body. Why this is surprising to some is beyond me. In the realm of duality and biology, there are no free lunches. Of course one must pay a physical toll to receive such beatific visions and downloads. What goes up must come down. Neurochemistry gets used, neurochemistry must be replenished. Again, none of this means these substances have no use—they may genuinely help people through specific therapeutic crises (as they have for me), and they can occasionally inspire someone toward a more committed practice. But the inspiration is the gift, not the destination. At some point the raft must be left behind, and the unsexy work of meditation and prayer—done in the chamber of your own heart, without this type of assistance—must actually begin.]]

## Tapas | तपस्

Releasing an old, energetically taxing pattern in favor of one that is more aligned with what God is asking of you is not all love and light. Very often—dare I say always—physical discomfort arises as the behavior falls away. Letting go of the porn means riding the itchy cravings of a handsy mind that wishes to satisfy itself. Going cold turkey on the caffeine could mean weeks of exhaustion as the adrenals reacquaint themselves with their original homeostatic set-point. Cutting out gossip means sitting with the awkward silence that follows when you realize you have nothing to talk about with certain people. The experiential fact that it sucks does not mean you are doing anything wrong—this is simply karma playing out—what was up is now coming down.

Neutrality, acceptance, and eventually welcoming this kind of physical discomfort highlights is tapas. Tapas, from the Sanskrit root tap (to burn or heat), is the willingness to stay in that discomfort without collapsing or hardening.

Without hardening is an important phrase to understand, for this is not a grit-your-teeth, white-knuckle, David-Goggins-style affair. This is discomfort as grace—celebration, even. The pain one may experience during the brief withdrawal period of ‘not getting what I want’ can at first be reframed, and then eventually felt, weirdly enough, as an ecstatic experience. The body, mind and soul are being liberated from what would have otherwise harmed them. “Hallelujah! Thank God I am feeling these cravings, for it means my energy systems are recalibrating and upgrading to hold more light moving forward!” This process is talked about more in Chapter 6. Alas, because modern society has so generously provided each of us a white, padded room and straightjacket to safely move about in—extreme creature comforts that sedate us with the illusion of security—we can easily overlook the fact that we're all just inmates in an insane asylum. Truly, we have engineered a world in which one can go an entire lifetime without being meaningfully uncomfortable, and then wonder why a single day of real difficulty sends the whole system into a frenzy. Some of us have become so sensitive to minor inconveniences, in fact, that we suffer when the Wi-Fi drops for thirty seconds, or when the parking lot is full and we have to—God forbid—walk an extra two minutes.

We have become uncalloused. And the Path, which must be walked barefoot, demands callouses.

This doesn't mean seeking out suffering for its own sake—that would just be a subtler form of ego, the spiritual masochist who confuses pain with progress. The discomfort tapas refers to is the natural consequence of living differently, of letting go of what was familiar and stepping into the unknown of what Īśvara is asking of you.

Luckily, all discomfort one feels while being made new is, as we've established, about sensations. And sensations, by their very nature, have a half-life. They arise, peak, and fade over and over and over again. The craving for ice cream won’t last forever. The anxiety of not checking your phone subsides. The restlessness of a quiet evening eventually settles into something that, if you're honest, actually feels quite nice. The key is to neither give in to the old mental pattern—just one more hit, just this once, please—nor escape into a new one. The smoker who quits cigarettes but immediately takes up stress-eating hasn't practiced tapas; they've just changed costumes. Stay with the heat. See it as neutral. Let it do its purifying work. Trust that what's burning away will leave you lighter and happier.

## Santoṣa | संतोष

While the discomfort of release arises and subsides, arises and subsides during one's spiritual ascent, one needn't be miserable for its ride. This is where santoṣa—contentment— enters as the gentle counterpart to tapas. If tapas is the willingness to endure the heat of transformation, santoṣa is the discovery that the fire is also warm and cozy, not just hot and incinerating.

The two work as brother and sister: Tapas asks you to let go of what is false while santoṣa asks you to notice what is already true. Does the hand open to release the old pattern, or open to receive a beautiful gift? Perhaps the two happen simultaneously?

Consider giving up salty, processed, heavily flavored food in exchange for something like bananas and spinach. On the tapas side, one must learn to sit with the cravings—the mind screaming for Doritos at 10pm, insisting that this bland, leafy nonsense is a form of punishment. But on the santoṣa side, the tongue starts to actually taste food again. A ripe mango becomes almost obscene with how sweet it is; a bowl of steamed broccoli for dinner somehow satisfies in a way that an entire pizza could never. This is nothing more than a palette that was numbed into submission being cleaned and restored to its original sensitivity.

This same principle applies across literally every domain: the bodybuilder discovers that slow, intentional movement reveals more about their body than their max deadlift ever had. The audiophile blasting bass-heavy tracks at full volume begins to hear entire worlds inside a single acoustic guitar. The thrill-seeking skydiver starts to notice that a morning walk holds just as much wonder as a free-fall. As the mind returns to its sensitive nature, even the warmth of sunlight on the forearms is enough to send one to the stars. Now, true…a life free from all addiction may sound boring to the addict. Vices require escalation and novelty to sustain themselves, after all. But to the one who is deeply content, such temptations literally make no sense. Why harm myself or others just to snag a high I can surpass during meditation?

Like all the yamas and niyamas, santoṣa deepens and broadens with practice. At first, contentment might only be accessible on a good day—sun out, belly full, nothing urgent. But over time, one learns to locate it in increasingly unlikely places: in the delay, in the rejection, in the illness, in the loss, and even in the face of one’s own death.

Through this commitment, it is discovered that more is not always more. One can learn to relish the quiet music of the wind through the trees, the flavor of a steamed potato, the simple miracle of breathing without any effort whatsoever. These are not consolation prizes for the person who ‘couldn't hack it’ in the material world. They are the actual treasure every human heart is aching for, buried beneath a thousand louder, flashier, more expensive distractions that promise the same thing, but never deliver.[[One final note: santoṣa is not toxic positivity or ’settling’ for less than what life is offering. It is the radical act of saying: hey, this is enough. Actually, it must be enough because it is present. The irony, of course, is that the moment one genuinely arrives at this contentment, more tends to flow in. This paradox is not because the Universe has a cruel sense of humor, but because a system that is no longer hemorrhaging energy into craving and resistance simply has more capacity to receive.]]

## Satya | सत्य

In cultivating contentment, one must be careful not to confuse santoṣa with delusion— pasting a smiley face over a rotting foundation and calling it peace. This is where satya, truthfulness, becomes essential to the practice, for contentment that is built on a lie isn't contentment, but denial.

At its most basic level, satya simply means don't lie. Don't tell someone you're fine when you're not. Don't fabricate credentials on your résumé. Don't promise what you have no intention of delivering. For many at the beginning of the spiritual journey, this level of commitment alone is revolutionary—and genuinely difficult. Most people have no idea how frequently they lie until they resolve to stop: little exaggerations to make a story land better and “on my way” texts from the couch. Just attempting basic honesty for a single week will reveal the sheer volume of untruth that lubricates ordinary social life.

Satya doesn't stop at the mouth, but extends all the way inward—to the stories one tells oneself. “I’m not that upset about the breakup.” “I don't care what people think of me.” “I’ve already dealt with that childhood stuff.” These inner lies are often far more damaging than the outer ones, because at least when you lie to someone else, part of you knows the truth. When you successfully lie to yourself, you lose even that. As we've already seen, any distortion in the Field—inner or outer—creates discord that must eventually be corrected for. Better to catch it early.

This is also where non-stealing, or asteya, naturally fits. At first glance, asteya seems straightforward: don't take what isn't yours. But under the lens of satya, stealing reveals itself as just another form of untruth—acting as though something belongs to you when it doesn't. This includes the obvious—pocketing the extra change, ”borrowing” a friend's idea and presenting it as your own—but also the less obvious like stealing someone's time by showing up perpetually late or stealing someone's energy by dumping your unprocessed emotions on them without invitation. Each of these acts rests on the same quiet lie: I am more entitled to this than the situation actually warrants.

As one progresses along the Path, satya sharpens from a blunt instrument into something almost surgical. Early on, the work is just to stop the outright lies and gross exaggerations. But over time, subtler forms of dishonesty begin to surface and become intolerable. The white lie told to spare someone's feelings was, upon honest examination, really told to spare your own discomfort. The half-truth that technicallyyyyy isn't false but deliberately withholds what the other person actually needs to hear. The opinion stated with confidence merely to avoid looking uncertain. The list is endless.

As the sensitivity increases—and it will, relentlessly—the feedback loop between untruth and discomfort tightens to the point of near-instantaneity. In the early stages, a lie might not catch up with you for days or weeks. But further along, the smallest dishonesty is met with an almost-immediate jolt to the system. The body contracts, the energy dips, the feeling is off, and you know exactly why. This is why certain monks take vows of silence altogether—because at the finish line of one’s Awakening, the margin for error becomes so thin that even casual conversation becomes a minefield of potential half-truths.

This doesn't mean one walks around delivering brutal, unsolicited truths to everyone within earshot. Satya is always tempered by ahimsa. If the truth would cause unnecessary harm and serves no constructive purpose, silence is the more aligned option. The goal is not radical honesty as a personality trait the way someone may proudly boast “I just say it like it is.” The goal is speech that arises from clarity: not distorted by the desire to impress, not inflated by the need to be right, not withheld out of cowardice, and not weaponized out of anger.

Which brings up a crucial point about the yamas and niyamas as a whole: they are not a checklist. One does not “complete” ahimsa and then move on to satya the way one might finish a page and turn it. They are an interconnecting web—each one reinforcing and depending on the others. Truthfulness without non-violence becomes cruelty. Non-violence without truthfulness becomes cowardice. Contentment without discipline becomes complacency. And discipline without contentment becomes self-harm. These ten commitments function as a single, living system—a continuous recalibration of one's entire being toward alignment with what is Real.

This recalibration continues for some time, longer than the ego would like. That means the yamas and niyamas must be revisited at deeper and deeper levels as awareness lifts and expands. What was “good enough” at one stage becomes a recognizable distortion at the next. Imagine the guitarist who, after years of diligent practice, can hear the slightest deviation in pitch that they never would have noticed as a beginner. The same note, the same string, but now with an ear refined enough to detect subtlety. This is the nature of the soul’s Path: not necessarily learning new rules, but hearing the same Truth with increasing fidelity.

## Svādhyāya | स्वाध्याय

I haven't covered every yama and niyama here—aparigraha (non-possessiveness), saucha (cleanliness), asteya (non-stealing) and others have been left unaddressed or only briefly touched upon. This is partly for the sake of space, but mostly because, as should now be clear, these disciplines are not ten separate items on a spiritual grocery list, but facets of a single diamond. Look at any side with sincerity—truly commit to non-violence, or truthfulness, or contentment—and you will inevitably find yourself face to face with the others. The person who stops lying soon discovers how much they've been stealing. The person who stops stealing soon confronts how much they've been hoarding. The person who stops hoarding soon realizes how cluttered their inner world has become. One discipline, followed honestly, opens the door to the rest.

Which is precisely why svādhyāya—self-study—ties them all together. For without the willingness to examine oneself, earnestly, none of the other disciplines hold. You can memorize every ethical code ever written and still be a world-class douchebag if you never turn the lens inward.

Svādhyāya operates on two levels: The first is the study of sacred texts—scripture, wisdom literature, and the writings of those who have already completed the Path before you (and, ideally, the direct guidance of a living teacher who has walked it themselves.) This is not the same as reading for entertainment or intellectual accumulation. My guru Paramahansa Yogananda used to say that if you read for one hour, you should write for two hours and contemplate for three. The ratio is instructive: for every unit of information taken in, far more time must be spent digesting it—turning it over, questioning it, sitting with it in silence until it stops being someone else's words and starts becoming lived. Otherwise, that knowledge just becomes another form of consumption: collecting spiritual insights the way someone collects trading cards—cool to show-off, but useless where it really counts.

The second and vastly more consequential level is the direct study of one’s self. Not the curated, social media-worthy version of self-reflection, but an absolutely ruthless investigation into the mechanics of one’s own mind. Why did I react that way? What was I actually afraid of? Where did that impulse come from? What am I avoiding right now, and why? This is the contemplative life—a constant, quiet examination running beneath the surface of every interaction, decision, and moment of discomfort or ease.

Applied correctly, svādhyāya is really just satya turned inward and sustained lifetime after lifetime—truthfulness-turned-lifestyle. If this is followed with enough courage and persistence, it inevitably leads to the question that sits beneath all other questions: Who am I? Not what do I believe, not what do I want, not what have I been told I am—but what is actually here when all these layers are peeled away?

This question cannot be satisfactorily answered by reading another book, attending another workshop, or asking AI to read your natal birth chart. It can only be answered by looking directly, patiently, and with the full force of one's attention at the mind itself. For as essential as a commitment to ethical living is to the spiritual journey—and it is essential—willpower and moral effort alone cannot carry one to Bliss. The yamas and niyamas prepare the vessel. They seal the leaks, straighten the hull, raise the sails. But it is the mind, and specifically the quality of one's attention, that determines how far and quickly the vessel actually travels.
