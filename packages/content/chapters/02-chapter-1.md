Despite all the persuasive chatter from biohackers and AI evangelists, your body will grow old and die. Even if it ends up taking a few hundreds years longer than biologically orchestrated, it is one of two things you can be absolutely certain about.

How any of us can sleepwalk our way through life without really taking this fact to heart is a true mystery. Perhaps we are blocked by misguided faith, or by a belief that such contemplation is a waste of precious time, or by our being too busy for the shock of mortality to come say hi. Whatever the reason, it can sometimes take an agonizingly large series of unfortunate events before we wake up to the fact that, ultimately, what we are calling ‘my life’ is effectively outside ’my control,’ and that, really, we need to get to the bottom of the human predicament fast, before it’s too late.

Actually, even those who intellectually accept their destiny (again, the vast majority do not) typically only do so at a surface-level. Such people claim to want to live ‘more in the present moment’ in the face of death, thinking that because life is so short, it must be all about ‘becoming fulfilled,’ ‘having awesome experiences,’ and ‘feeling as alive as possible, as often as possible’.

Such a perspective can only arise after one’s initial consultation with finitude, but can stick around for the remainder of the incarnation. People seem *so. dang. certain*. that the only way to accept their bitter ending is by making their brief adventure here on planet Earth as epic and story-worthy as possible. Otherwise…what’s the point?

Because of this, people—maybe you—go to greater and greater extremes trying to fulfill their projections of a perfect life. Many try and try again to maximize their time here by working relentlessly on their profession or passion, striving to rise higher in the ranks, always justtttt on the brink of having made it. Others go about it by participating in adrenalized activities such as drag racing, cold plunging, looksmaxxing, one-night standing, building themselves into a brand, gambling, partying, compulsive exercising, tik-toking, and the like. But an increasing number are looking for that ineffable something—that sense of one’s life having purpose despite its also having an expiration date—by collecting spiritual knowledge and mystical experiences. Rarer is it these days to find someone who *hasn’t* tried at least one session of yoga, meditation, breathwork, tai chi, reiki, ecstatic dance, plant medicine, sound healing, timeline hopping, tarot pulling, or shamanic journeying.

Why is it that almost no one, not even the self-proclaimed spiritual folk, appear to be satisfied with being a nobody? With living a quiet, unimpressive life immersed in nature and prayer anymore? Who among us are truly willing to walk away from the screens and influencers and breaking news to follow the happy rhythms of the sun and moon, not in the least bit tempted to try the latest spiritual technique or wellness tip, free from any and all need to share what we’ve been up to?

In part, it’s because western culture—whose influence is now global—has browbeaten the population into equating “feeling alive” with “having an adrenaline rush,” “being wise” with “having spiritual experiences,” and “being fulfilled” with “staying and presenting happy.” Alas, such perspectives and pursuits are actually the causes of your misery, not its solutions. To explain why, you first need to understand the mechanics of your mind as it frantically searches for meaning through experience, and tries to escape death and irrelevance through creation. Let’s run another thought experiment.

Imagine your ideal life. Really go into your own hopes and dreams. I’ll wait.

What food would you eat? American food? Indian food? What if you could stay healthy and sexy, while still eating whatever you wanted? Remember, this is your ideal life here. Would you then opt for mounds of beef jerky, liters of lattes, piles of Twinkies? Would you decide to transcend the need for food altogether?

And what would you do with your time, here in this utopia of yours? Would you choose to work? What if you knew that money or technological limitation wasn’t an issue? Would you then travel the world?  Go to the moon? Would you build a tech empire or a bird-box or a commune? Teach and inspire future generations? Trek through the Amazon and discover a new species of beetle?

And what would your relationships look like? Would you have a lot of friends? Would everyone be happy to see you? What if people respected and admired you? What if they truly cared about what you had to say? Are you in a committed, monogamous partnership? Or would you rather be free to have as many lovers as you wanted?

Remember: in this Universe, you have maximum potential. A million cars and a billion followers, if that’s what you want. No more financial or bodily or relational problems, if that’s more appealing. Whatever, wherever, whenever.

To the uninitiated, such fantasies may sound a paradise. But something about the scenario becomes deeply unsettling the more one meditates upon it. Sure, avoiding starvation, living creatively, and feeling loved are all undeniably great things, and what the future hopefully holds more of. But then again…how many roller coasters, orgies, words of affirmation, countries visited, views, cheeseburgers, lines of cocaine or cups of Ayahuasca, Lamborghinis, olympic medals, influence, and trillions of dollars could anyone handle before existential doubt crept in? What is the point of all these peaks in and of themselves?

## Now You See It

To highlight what’s causing the ontological ‘ick’, and why such a dream is a fool’s Shangri-La, look no further than to the Universal reality of impermanence, or anicca as the cool kids call it. Impermanence needs no introduction for you are already intimately familiar with it. What happened to the version of you at five years old? What about dinner last week? Impermanence is so near and dear that the moment you’ll have read and processed this next word, it’ll be gone —poof!

It’s easy to see (but surprisingly hard to remember) that each moment of experience (yes, even this one) is dissolving in mid-air like steam rising from a tea cup. Sure, you can try to resurrect a thing through its re-enactment, but that wouldn’t be precisely the same moment, for the subatomic particles, context and mind will have changed between takes.[[Besides, that would end up disappearing instantaneously, too. And then what?]]

One of the Buddha's chief disciples captured this perfectly when asked to summarize the dharma (teachings of the Buddha) in one sentence: “That which has the nature to arise also has the nature to pass away.” No matter how high you get, or how well Crypto is doing, or how full-body an orgasm becomes, or how many followers and wealth you attract, or how beatific a vision, or how quiet your mind, or how optimistically you think, or how magnanimous your character…it won’t last. All of it—all of it—will fade, decay, implode or be dismantled just like the rest of the phenomenal Universe. Sure, this fact also technically means that all of life’s unpleasantries will end, too. But I doubt that fact makes the mind feel better about its short and often painful existence.

This problem, by the way, *doesn’t* go away in the face of higher ideals such as philanthropy or service. Even if one solves the world’s greatest problems—the homeless homed, the rainforests saved, the wars stopped, the elites dethroned, the ignorant enlightened—the solver then still has to live inside their own mind afterwards. Now what? Colonize the stars and create infinite abundance for all beings across space and time? Okay, and once that is accomplished? Enter your villain era, create a mess of it all, and then restart the whole process…? Even if existential thoughts are somehow evaded for a few trillion years, eventually they must be caught and felt and pondered.[[Having a unique and eternal soul does not help the situation as much as one might hope, either. Just as the never-to-be-replicated-again snowflake is bound to melt back into water, and water to evaporate back into the clouds from which they came, the soul is destined to return to its Source. The soul is only permanent insofar as it holds a likeness to its Creator—its presence being beyond time—not that it itself has a kind of separate or permanent identity that persists outside of the Dream. And yet the spiritual seeker routinely takes the soul to be what they are, imbuing it with the same qualities of selfhood they claim to want to transcend. This is revealed in those who like to use the phrase my soul, rather than referring to it as I or the soul. Who is the me that claims the soul in the first place? Even here—in one of the more refined spiritual concepts—the mind is doing what it always does: trying to find something permanent to cling onto.]]

Impermanence is the reason for the pesky “is this it?” thought accompanying anyone who really considers the end-game of all their desires being borne out. It’s also the unexamined crack in the philosophy of “life is short, so make it count.” Count towards what, exactly…? If a thing’s destiny is always to end, why begin at all? What meaning could this, or any experience for that matter, hold in the face of total erasure? Surely there is a more satisfying and substantial answer than just shifting attention towards doing and becoming something else.

The honest facing of this—that nothing can be held onto or made secure—tends to generate two different responses in people: the first is a kind of immature *‘fuck it, nothing matters anyway’* nihilism that leads to either hedonism (oh well, I may as well play video games all week), depression (I’m just going to mope around until my AI chat bot suggests what to do), or revolt (screw them, they did this to me!)

The second response is a gnawing restlessness that drives one to try even harder to make something stick, seeking for that one experience or achievement or relationship that will actually last. The magnum opus, the soul mate, the afterlife.[[It’s not uncommon for the new spiritual initiate to spastically toggle between the two—nihilism and restlessness— depending on the hour, day, week, or year.]]

Both responses, different as they seem, are based on the same problem of suffering. Not the suffering from a plump mosquito bite or terminal diagnosis—though, those count, too. This is a deeper, more pervasive suffering that colors even the most decent of moments. Similarly, both responses share another quality in that neither actually helps the situation: numbing or lashing out don’t make impermanence any less true, nor does grasping harder make anything more permanent.

Sorry not sorry.

Actually, there’s something here happening underneath the hood in your favor—the Good News, as it were. But before getting there, let’s put on our ‘big-boy pants’ and get even more honest with ourselves, for the mere fact that an experience is impermanent is only the proximate cause of suffering, not its ultimate. To better understand suffering, and therefore to overcome it, you need to first study the anatomy of desire, for desire is the engine of action, and action the cause of manifestation, and manifestation the holy mother of impermanence.

## Thirst Trap

Let’s set the scene. Try to follow carefully, because each term builds upon the one before. Simply put, desire is the wanting of an experience—past, present or future—to either begin or end. ‘I want this to happen or not happen;’ ‘I want this to end or not end;’ ‘I want this to have happened or have never happened.’ Desire, desire, desire.

Desire can arise towards literally anything: for an object, a person, a place, an idea, a memory, a revelation, a feeling state, a specific outcome, a version of yourself, forgiveness, *someone’s* approval, heaven, death, vindication, enlightenment, and so on. While it’s a common trope to hear in early-stage Buddhist circles that “desire is the root of all suffering,” this is not precisely so, for one can think of many desires that arise without suffering riding shotgun—an innocent and detached desire to know what time it is, for instance. It is only when said desire becomes for an outcome and is made stronger—when it turns into what Buddhists call *taṇhā* (thirst)—that it morphs into misery.

Taṇhā has two forms: craving and aversion. Likes and dislikes, in a way. Either you want reality to include something you believe it lacks (*but I wanted a scone!*) or you want reality not to include something you believe it has (*but I didn’t want this salad!*)

Every experience of suffering includes both craving and aversion. That is, any craving for a scone necessarily hides an aversion towards that which is believed to be standing in its way— permission, price, availability, this salad, and so on. Any aversion towards the salad requires a craving for it to be at least somewhat different—saltier, fattier, sweeter, scone-ier, etc.

The above statements are so obvious as to seem offensive at the intellectual level. And yet introspection humbly reveals how often they are overlooked on the actual level. As profound and liberating as it is to realize that your dissatisfaction is 100% of the time, *without exception*, caused by your desire for reality to be different from how it is right now, it is equally as painful to discover how frequently you do so anyways.

In your defense, these endless yearnings are not conscious, and therefore not your fault. Most of your cravings and aversions slide by undetected not because you’re a wretched little sinner, but because your mind is not yet sharp enough to detect their feeling-tone, or vedanā. Much like the saturation of a color determines how vivid or muted it appears against its background, the vedanā of an experience affects how much it stands out from the rest. An experience with a strong vedanā—the stubbing of a big toe or the delivering of an important public-facing keynote—is unmistakable compared to their weaker counterparts—a feather brushing the elbow, say, or walking past a cantaloupe in the grocery store.

It is only due to modern life having become so noisy, colorful and adrenalized that the mind’s sensitivity and capacity for discerning feelings is at an all-time low. As such, it takes real, deep, honest self-inquiry to discover (let alone admit) that your experience, even when it appears quite ‘good’ on the surface, is actually frothing with ‘not enough-ness’ underneath.[[This is especially the trap for the beginner and intermediate spiritual seeker, who, after making some headway in healing and surrendering, falsely assume they have discovered a stable state free from such clinging. This isn't to say that that initial peace is false, only that staying humble in the face of what one believes one has attained serves the seeker well. The Path, and its companion states of contentment, will continue to deepen for some time to come. What one considers peace today may very well be seen as suffering tomorrow. Besides, even if such a moment is authentic peace, in virtually all such cases it is a temporary moment of balance that will soon exit stage left. And when it does, the seeker who has identified with being the one who has found the peace, is sure to experience disappointment.]] In general, the more unconscious one’s mind, the longer its list of unspoken desires, and the more specific its demands for how the Universe should bring them about. For example, without the wisdom of impermanence, one’s travel bucket list may very well exceed one hundred locations, along with the rather-precise hopes for what each destination will look like, who will accompany them there, how transformed they’ll be upon return, and so on.

One of the many ways such a ravenous mindset backfires is simply because the phenomenal Universe has an infinite number of forms it can take. You can want the same pair of shoes in red, blue, silver, tie-dye, or any other conceivable color. And you can want those same pair of shoes with infinite combinations of laces, soles, materials, and stitching patterns. Multiply that across every object, every experience, every relationship, every outcome, and the result is an ever-expanding catalogue of preferences that could never be fully satisfied, even across quadrillions of lifetimes.

This without even considering how, as society evolves, so do the desires of the people inhabiting it. It would have been impossible to want the sleek new solar panel back in the 1320s, for knowledge of materials science and manufacturing weren’t enough to conceive of one being possible. They were too busy dreaming of a sturdier plows and sharper scythes. Similarly, the same genetically-scripted alpha males of the 1700s who loved their powdered wigs, silk stockings, and embroidered coats would view those very garments as “beta male behavior” today.

In other words, desire doesn't just multiply—it shape-shifts. The evolution of technology and culture ensure that the mind always has fresh fuel for its dissatisfactions. Even as the Universe stretches itself in ways incomprehensible to science today—unlocking time travel, telepathy, interstellar colonies, clones, and digital immortality—without spiritual evolution the mind will continue wanting the next frontier thing, never satisfied, immune to the common sense that it is effectively back in the same place as it was thousands of years prior, just with shinier toys.

## Withdrawals

In truth, *taṇhā*, or the thirsting for experience to be different than how it is, being the root of suffering, while completely accurate in one sense, does not paint the full picture on a subjective level. It is only by inspecting why one wants an experience that suffering’s mechanics are revealed: Desire for experience is really a desire for sensations in disguise.

Read that again, because it may be the most important sentence in this chapter. The desire for the arrival or cessation of an experience in and of itself does not cause suffering; rather, it’s the craving and aversion at the sensation level—known as *kāma-taṇhā*—that does.

To understand why this must be so, one need only turn inwards and play the *‘So That Game’*. The rules are simple: (1) pick one of your desires, any desire, (2) state it out loud and add “so that” at the end, (3) fill in the blank, and then add more and more “so thats” until you can go no further, and you have discovered, at bottom, what it is you really want from said desire.

For example, one of my remaining desires is that this book be read by tens of thousands of people. To play the game I would just add “so that” until I can go no further. *I want this book to* *be read by tens of thousands of people so that the ideas can spread far and wide so that seekers do not end* *up transforming in what I believe is the ’wrong direction’ so that the world quickly becomes harmonious* *so that I can experience such a harmonious world in this lifetime so that I can finally feel at peace*… It can take some digging and radical honesty, but what the seeker inevitably comes in contact with is that any desire, be it superficial or deep, apparently selfish or apparently selfless, is ultimately a desire to start (or stop) feeling certain sensations. You say you want a scone, but really you want the sensations you assume will arise from eating said scone—the rush, the bubbling, the radiating. You say you don’t want that salad, but really you just don’t want the sensations you assume will arise from eating the salad—the strange texture, the ‘yuck’, the ache in the belly. Seen clearly, thoughts of scone and salads are symbolic, not literal—the wanting of them being psychological stand-ins for something more physiological: the sensations they generate.

This can seem like an arbitrary and unimportant distinction at first. I can assure you it’s not. In fact, this understanding, once fully embraced, changes the context of all one’s experience, adding a new dimension of understanding to every single preference one claims to have, every single decision one makes, and every single interaction one partakes in.

Oh...and it provides an escape hatch out of suffering. More on that later.

One reason the ‘So That’ game can be so powerful is because it reveals our fundamental error. Inspect any ‘So That’ chain carefully enough and conclude that, actually, all one is really doing is unskillfully holding themselves hostage from experiencing a feeling until the original outcome, often an ill-defined and nebulous one, has manifested. ‘I want a long-term partner so that I can raise kids so that I can feel fulfilled’ actually means ‘I will only allow myself to feel fulfilled once I have a longterm partner.’ ‘I want the high-ranking position so that people will think I'm successful so that I'll be respected and admired so that I can stop feeling inadequate’ means ‘I will not let myself feel adequate until I reach a high-ranking position.’

Because most such outcomes are vague and do not materialize “on-time,” the aspirant is then left feeling impatient and restless, incessantly stalking their ambiguous idea of success until it at least ‘kinda happens’ and they receive their long-awaited (but impressively short-lived) prize: a surge of dopamine. This is the game business moguls, self-help wizards, and even some so-called manifestation experts try to help people get better at. How do you work hard in the right ways for just long enough so that reality conforms to your will and you get another hit of feel-good neurochemistry again?

Tragically, sometimes the coveted outcome is never achieved, and one’s mind is left in a perpetual state of dissatisfaction and frustration, locked out of its desired feeling forever, blaming itself or others for its shortcomings and failures. But even in the best case scenario— when this strategy works as planned, the outcome secured and the feeling felt right on schedule —all it does is end up confirming one’s false assumption that outcomes cause feelings, instead of the more accurate one that beliefs cause feelings.[[Important to note is that what so often are referred to as feelings—feeling fulfilled, feeling inadequate, feeling lonely—are actually just stories built atop feelings. Real feelings are somatic: a sense of lightness, heaviness, temperature, expanding, contracting, burning, pulsing, tingling, aching, soothing, etc. One who “feels betrayed” is simply interpreting the feelings of heartache, a clenched jaw, and a knotted belly to mean betrayal. Such associations are obvious when reflecting on how, although the butterfly sensations of boarding a rollercoaster ride (fun!☺) with stage fright (not fun! ☹) are nearly identical, they are related to differently simply because of how one tells their story. It is also helpful to keep in mind that, because of the intense level of conditioning on planet Earth at this time, even after numerous lifetimes of spiritual seeking and meditation, the mind will still have a tough time separating the narrative from the sensation the narrative is based upon. Give the mind a little grace as it untangles what is being thought from what is being felt.]]

Relying on sensations to lead you away from suffering and towards happiness is even more foolish than relying upon outcomes to do the same, for sensations are even more fleeting—even more obviously impermanent.

In spirituality, they refer to these sensational ups and downs as being a consequence of duality—the idea that the phenomenal world is actually just a play of light and shadow, things being unable to exist in relativity without their opposites (heads without tails, loudness without silence, health without disease, etc).

In mathematics, on the other hand, sensational swings are nothing more mystical than *regression to the mean*, a term that refers to the tendency for values to move back toward the average over time. As it turns out, anything that can be measured and plotted—from stock prices, average points per game and breath rate—swivel above and below its own self-contained average. More on that below.

In physics, the view of impermanence is again different and useful. Here the Universe is said to be made up of countless wavelets fluctuating at all different frequencies, amplitudes, and wavelengths. These waves or vibrational patterns are defined by their relative peaks and valleys, which oscillate back and forth over a period of time. As such, there is strictly no such thing as a permanent peak, for every crest necessarily implies a trough, and every trough a crest.

And finally, in neuroscience, such a sensational crash is said to be due to the combination of homeostasis plus a process called *hedonic adaptation*—the nervous system's built-in tendency to return to baseline after any pleasure spike. When you first bite into a salty, fatty dish, the tongue’s taste receptors fire intensely, triggering a cascade of dopamine release in the brain's reward centers. But within seconds—literally seconds—those same receptors desensitize. The neurons that were joyously screaming “yeehaw!!” start to quiet down, because the receptors are fatiguing and the dopamine system recalibrating. Indeed, by the third or fourth bite, the sensation has dulled considerably , such that by the time you've finished, you might barely even register the taste at all.

Said different, constant stimulation, no matter how pleasant, eventually becomes invisible to the very receptors meant to detect it. This explains a lot, including why even the wealthiest and most famous get unhappy. Because the mind only ever measures experience based on its past, were its baseline pulled up (a bump in salary, a personal record, a new partner), and the wisdom of impermanence left behind, it would then begin comparing its experience to a new perceived normal, elated at the relative highs and depressed at the relative lows, very much ignorant that the real problem remains unsolved.[[The only conceivable loophole would be to hook oneself up to a machine that intravenously pumps feel-good hormones into the body at ever-increasing quantities, rising in volume until the blood vessels pop and incarnation expires. Perhaps not the worst way to go?]]

All of this is bad news for anyone seeking fulfillment through experience and sensation (aka the vast majority of humanity), for they always end up right where they started—dissatisfied. And it’s doubly bad news for the self-proclaimed, spiritual ‘goddesses’ and ‘kings’ who misrepresent the teachings of tantra—practices designed to transmute sensual energy—to justify their ego’s insatiable hedonistic urges, for they end up both dissatisfied and having missed the point of Awakening.

## Enough!

The next time one of your desired experience comes to fruition examine your mind-body system closely and find not just the feelings of joy, say, or pleasant tingling, but something vastly more subtle—this sense of dropping back and basking in…something.

That something is called contentment, or sukkha. Sukkha is not like the golden tremors of ecstasy or the gentle swells of pride—sensations that can be felt in one or more parts of the body. It’s more accurate to say that contentment is a mental state—a feeling of mind, rather than of body. In its purest form, it is a state completely free from thirsting, clinging, rejecting or hating—aka free from *taṇhā*. Sukkha, contentment, is without preference, expectation, or need. Sukkha comes around surprisingly often, though mostly briefly, and almost always unrecognized. Unrecognized because, again, sukkha is not a feeling of relief or absence of pain, but the cessation of desire itself. It is simply ‘enoughness’, a very inconspicuous and overlooked flavor of experience that most often arises in the mind during the attainment of a desired feeling state. If one is craving an orgasm, say, and that orgasm then occurs, sukkha is sure to be there. If one wishes that the feeling of low back pain go away, and it does, sukkha comes and visits. Actually, if it’s helpful, one can think of sukkha as being synonymous with not wanting, for not wanting is where one naturally and obviously lands after wanting has been satiated. Ironically, this implies that what one actually wants, deep down, is to not want! Are you following? Peculiar though that statement is, it is useful to momentarily look within yourself and reflect upon whether this is true experientially. Peer past all your desires for experience, for control, for pleasure, for being and becoming, and discover that, actually, *there is only ever one* desire: to be content. To recognize this moment as enough. To not want anything at all.

Simply reading those words is not sufficient to transfer their intended wisdom over. Instead, all such spiritual realizations need to be had at a root operating system level—at the subjective level—if they are to inspire anything useful. It is therefore recommended to play the *‘So That Game’* again, perhaps in a journal this time to slow down and capture your rapid-fire thoughts, asking why you want the feelings of peace or joy or adequacy or safety in the first place? What purpose are they serving? In doing so, I suspect you will have the same epiphany I’ve had: what feels so good about getting the sensations you want is actually the cessation of wanting them! Why is this the case? Because wanting is extremely uncomfortable, physiologically speaking. Wanting to pee, wanting to travel Japan, wanting world peace. All desires, no matter how altruistic their core, create an itchiness or pressure in the energy body that slowly builds until some form of resolution occurs.

Most people take the long road of resolving these desires, often through engineering certain experiences which are believed to produce the ideal sensations. Want to stop feeling terrible? A luxury car oughta help! Now just work hard for a while, make a butt-ton of money, and then maybe one day check it off the bucket list. Want to feel deep, sustained peace? Try enlightenment! Just renounce the worldly life, enter the monastery, and then maybe one day it’ll all click.

The wiser discover a loophole: by realizing that it's not the luxury car or enlightenment that is wanted, but rather the not wanting that arises in the midst of pleasant sensations, one can then choose to surrender the car or search for enlightenment on the spot, and in doing so, receive what one has been after all along.

Is this starting to make any sense? You can just skip the whole circus by letting go of the wanting right now. And by doing so you’ll have solved your problem just as effectively as if you had achieved the outcome. Brilliant!

Our plan is foolproof except for two teensy, tiny details.

The first is that it’s bat-shit-crazy. Who in their right mind would give up not having the thing in exchange for not wanting the thing?! You’d have to be absurdly fed up with the game of cat and mouse—desire and manifestation—in order to just drop it all. And the second is that wanting to not want is still wanting. The desire to let go of desire is itself a desire, which breeds the very suffering one was hoping to transcend by letting go of desire.

Oops!

Despite these speed bumps, surrendering desire is still a project worth taking on, for unlike literally everything else in the phenomenal world, not wanting (aka sukkha), once found, can be made permanent. And that’s because not wanting is free from the rules of duality: the lows following the highs. Yes, wanting always begets more wanting, but for reasons that will soon be made clear, not wanting needn’t the same. There is a loophole by which contentment can actually give birth to itself over again and over again, endlessly, without fail.

To help you see how this might be so, consider another thought experiment. Let’s say that each moment of your life can somehow be given an Objective Rating of Happiness—ORH—on a scale from 1 → 10, where the bounds are constrained by your most (10) and least (1) happy moments on record. Here’s a visual:

![Objective Rating of Happiness — a moment-to-moment happiness curve rising and falling around a midpoint](/images/orh-image.jpg)

Let's imagine you have an extremely pleasurable experience—one with an ORH of 9.5. Now watch what happens next: the mind, having tasted that high, immediately starts trying to get back there. But the laws of *regression to the mean* (as briefly described above) make this nearly impossible. Peaks are, by definition, exceptional. The promotion glow dulls by the second paycheck. The new relationship butterflies become Tuesday night reruns. The mountain-top vista, captured on a thousand phones, fades into just another desktop wallpaper.

The same logic applies in reverse, though it's no consolation. Yes, a 1.3 will likely give way to something better. But that something better will eventually give way to something worse, which will give way to something better, on and on, forever. The ORH graph oscillates, but it never technically ascends.

The trouble only compounds from here, for the mind, by default, doesn't measure each moment on its own terms; it measures each moment against the ones that came before. So even when life is “objectively” fine—even when the ORH is holding sits at an admirable 7.8, say—the mind may very well register it as disappointment because yesterday it was a 8.0.

Actually, the cruelest part is that most experiences fall in between these extremes. The majority of waking life is punctuated by dull, ordinary moments. It's only because emotional experiences graft themselves so strongly onto memory that one expects a world overflowing with extremes. In reality, the vast majority of what one does is pretty damn average—washing dishes and popping pimples in the mirror.

The good news? There is another axis to this chart that is so often overlooked. And this axis isn't subject to *regression to the mean*, doesn't oscillate, and can't be ruined by comparison. It’s the axis of sukkha—whether or not one is truly equanimous with what’s arising, be it happy, sad, terrified, or some shade in-between.

To state it more plainly, contentment doesn't depend on where you are on the happiness scale. A 9.5 experience can come with contentment or without it. A 1.3 experience can come with contentment or without it. The ORH will inevitably fluctuate—duality and *regression to the mean* guarantees it—but contentment doesn't have to. Why? Because contentment isn't a position on the happiness scale; it's a meta perspective towards the position itself. Being okay with this moment doesn't necessitate being not-okay with the next one. Contentment can perpetuate itself precisely because it asks for nothing, depends on nothing, and therefore can't be disappointed by anything.[[Importantly, acceptance doesn’t mean inaction. Just because you accept the sensational discomfort of being without air on your exhale doesn’t mean you don’t inhale. It just means you don’t hate the moment, or cling to what could have been. By all means: keep doing things! Just don’t reject or cast sensations out because they are not what you would have chosen for yourself.]]

To view it from a slightly different angle, it can be said that contentment transcends duality —the requirement of opposites—because it exists prior to the pleasant-unpleasant distinction. Enough *isn’t* some abstract middle point between happiness and sadness. It’s the very ground upon which both stand. Sure, scientists can map its neural correlates—the specific brain states and firing patterns associated with what people call “feeling content”—but contentment itself can't be found in any brain scan, for it’s a felt quality, a quality which lives in *subjective experience*, not in the neurons themselves. You can measure the physical mechanisms that accompany contentment, but you cannot weigh, photograph, or dissect contentment itself, for it exists in awareness, not in atoms.[[While on the surface it can seem like there are degrees of contentment—i.e. one can be slightly more content in this moment compared to the next—this is an illusion. What’s really going on is that there one toggles between light contentment and dark non-contentment so quickly as for one’s experience of it to seem grey.]]

Despite the sound logic, the mind will likely continue behaving as if contentment depends entirely on feeling certain feelings. This is because every previous experience has seemed to prove it true. If after thousands of clothing purchases, you still experience that oh-so glorious moment of not wanting right after (along with that brief sparkle of sensational glitter that comes with it), the mind will forge a strong connection between the two: “Oh…I guess buying things equals contentment?!”

Such is the never-ending game of seeking sensation through acquisition for the purpose of peace. Scratching that itch will always feel good in the moment, but doing so never addresses the source of the itch, and is thus bound to be replaced by a new one sooner or later.[[This pattern extends even to one's owns thoughts. That is, having a belief confirmed generates its own subtle sensational hit, a micro-dose of satisfaction that reinforces the belief regardless of whether it's true or not. More on this in Chapter 3.]]

Seen together, these realizations can be personally transformative, if one lets them. It opens the door to other possible solutions for accessing sukkha besides the Sisyphean or Midas-like manifestation of one’s desires. What if contentment were possible even in the midst grueling work or terminal illness? What if contentment were possible even while one does absolutely everything in one’s power to help relieve the world of its suffering? What if contentment were possible without the perfect life?

Such is the promise of a truly spiritual life: not freedom from the lows, failures, deaths, worries, or doubts. Freedom within them.
