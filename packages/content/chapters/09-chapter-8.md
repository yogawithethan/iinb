You are not a bat.

Of all the doubts and uncertainties one may incur in this life—whether God exists, whether this relationship is right, whether that mole has always been there—this statement is likely not one of them.[[Although, shoot, I suppose you can identify as anything you want.]]

In 1974, a philosopher named Thomas Nagel turned this self-evident statement into one of the most cited papers in the history of Western philosophy. His question was deceptively simple: what is it like to be a bat? Not what does a bat look like, or how does echolocation work, or what are the evolutionary advantages of hanging upside down in a cave—science can answer all of those. What Nagel wanted to know was of a different order entirely: what does it feel like, from the inside to navigate the world through sonar? What is the subjective experience of a creature whose entire perceptual reality is built on sound waves bouncing off surfaces? You can study the bat's brain. You can map its neural pathways. You can even build a computer model that perfectly simulates every firing of every neuron in its cute, miniaturized skull. And yet you would still have no idea—none whatsoever—what it is like to be that bat.

The paper and its conclusion—that consciousness has an irreducibly subjective character, and no amount of objective information about another being's hardware will ever grant you access to what it feels like to run that hardware from within—was brilliant. And wrong.

Actually, I’ll be softer. The conclusion was correct insofar as a ‘me’ will never, can never, crawl inside a bat, but wrong about consciousness being localized to a ‘me’ in the first place. To understand, first return to the tree outside the window—the one from last chapter that moved from object to note to harmony as Vision is restored. Recall that the boundary between oneself and the tree did not collapse through effort or imagination—one did not try to feel at one with it. Instead, the reactions that had been maintaining the distance—the labeling, the associating, the filing of “tree” under its appropriate impression—fell quiet, and without them, the subject-object distance had nothing to stand on.

The tree, of course, was not unique in this. A river can be known this way. A melody. A thought. A shoe. Anything with a frequency (aka everything) can be met in the silence of non-reaction.

But when the thing being seen is another soul, there is an additional dimension: they are also meeting you. They carry their own reactive patterns, their own impressions, their own conditioning. And when two such systems encounter one another, something is produced that neither could have produced alone.

## Green

Take blue and yellow, mix em’ together, and what do you get? No, not blue pretending to be yellow. No, not yellow with blue's permission. You get a third color—green—magnificent in its never-having-been-seen-before-ness, irreducible to its components, impossible without the two colors having united.

Now what if you decided to un-mix this new concoction? Divide the green back into blobs of blue and yellow? After all, in principle, such a move is technically possible: give someone absurdly tiny tweezers and the patience of a chatbot, and they could very well pick out every blue particle from every yellow one, sorting them back into separate piles. But what would one have to show for this painstaking exercise? Two collections of molecules, all forged in the same exploding star, assembled from the same periodic table, born from the same source—now artificially separated by a pair of tweezers and the mind’s insistence that they are different things? Sure, I guess that’s one way to spend your time. But doing so only ever proves that separation is possible, not that it is ever true.

Do you see where I’m going with this?

Every soul has a color (is a color, actually.) You are a color. I am a color. There is a frequency to each being, a particular hue that one emanates as naturally as a bell emanates its resonant tone. Some are warm, ochre, close to the earth. Others are sharp, electric, something near violet. One needn't be clairvoyant to sense the difference. Walk into a room where your sister is sitting and there is a felt quality to her presence—a texture, a temperature, a shade—that has nothing to do with what she is saying or doing. Your best friend carries a different one. The stranger at the next table, another still. Each is broadcasting an aura as distinct and unmistakable as any color on a canvas.

Now watch what happens when they mix.

The moment one’s tranquil teal sits across from their vivacious amber eight-year-old nephew, for instance, a version of oneself emerges that was not there five minutes ago at the grown-ups’ table. This isn’t necessarily because one is being performative. It’s that the combination of your and his colors have produced a new hue—a sage green, perhaps—that has never, in all of history, existed before.[[Don't get too attached to that green, friend. Tomorrow's dinner with the same nephew will produce a slightly different shade. Nothing personal—that's impermanence for you.]]

None of this will occur merely by showing up in the same room, by the way. Two colors side by side on a canvas are still two colors. For green to be truly born, they must touch—must actually make contact, pigment to pigment, without a protective barrier between them. Such a standard is one that most human interactions, however intimate they appear from the outside, fail to accomplish. Two people can share a meal, a bed, a decade, and never once consciously mix. Each party’s conditioning can function as a seal around their unique pigment, keeping everything contained, presentable, and safely, sadly, separate.

Something is lost in this, and it's a tragedy that’s hard to name because it never technically existed to begin with. A green that could have been born wasn't. The universe is poorer for it, and neither party will ever quite know what they missed.

Consider two guys at work whose friendship runs entirely on memes and mutual grievances about that one nightmare project. That's their green. It's real enough. But it's narrow. Now consider one day—maybe after a couple brews or a rare lull in the shit-talk—when one of them mentions, almost in passing, that he's always loved ballet. The other goes quiet for a beat, then admits he's been secretly into jazz tap since middle school. Just like that, a part of the spectrum that had been sealed off in both of them—sealed, one suspects, by the cultural pressure of what men are supposed to care about—makes contact for the first time. A green is born.

This seal—the layers of impressions forming the conditioned mind—doesn't just prevent contact and depth with others. It distorts one's own color underneath. In color theory, saturation refers to a hue's purity—how vivid, how concentrated, how much itself a color is. Drain the saturation from a vivid red and you get the same red, only tired. Grayish. Stripped of the very quality that first made it alive.

A mind carrying heavy conditioning broadcasts in exactly this way—same soul color, far less vibrancy.

When one whose color has been dimmed by years of conditioning encounters one whose color burns vivid and unapologetic, the response is rarely admiration. More often it is contraction. A tightness behind the sternum that the mind will dress up in a thousand narratives —they're full of it, they got lucky, it won't last—every one of which is a story about them, and none of which is them. What one is feeling, beneath all the narration, is the precise shape of one's own seal—illuminated, for a moment, by someone who doesn't have one there.

The irony is that this almost never goes in one direction. At this point in human history, almost no soul is evenly dimmed. Conditioning is rather patchy—thick here, thin there—which means any two people meeting are likely to trigger each other simultaneously, each one vivid in certain places where the other is sealed. One may carry a fearlessness in conversation by saying what they mean without flinching that the other finds almost supernaturally intimidating. While that same intimidated person holds a stillness in crisis that the first one, for all their boldness, completely falls apart in. Sit them across from each other and both may go green with envy—both convinced the other has something they lack, but neither one realizing they are looking at their own seal.

But at least envy has the decency to feel bad! The more dangerous contractions are the ones that don’t. These are the sickly sweet moral tightening around another person's way of being. These kinds of reactions arrive not as “I wish I had that” but “they shouldn't have that.” This is the weird certainty that someone else’s expression is either too colorful and artificial, or too muted and self-righteous. The influencer who flaunts his wealth or the woman whose sensuality offends you may trigger a kind of moral masturbation: He’s missing what truly matters. She’s crying out for attention. The guy who often cancels plans to stay in or the gal who mentions her charity work in every conversation. He calls it introversion, but really he's just afraid of people. Her service is performative; the real kind doesn’t need an audience.

In each case, the story one tells can sure seem like discernment—a considered, mature evaluation of the other person’s choices. But it is almost never that. Almost invariably, it is the mind doing what the mind does: pointing out splinters in everyone else’s eyes so it doesn't have to notice the log in its own.[[Of course, it’s true that freedom in any domain is not the compulsive expression of it. The person who cannot go a week without sex is not really any freer than the person who cannot allow themselves to want it in the first place. The person with ten million in the bank who checks it every morning inhabits the same cage as the person who can't bring themselves to look at their balance at all. Both colors are distorted—just in opposite directions.]]

Unsurprisingly, there is a hidden cost to all of this. What one condemns in another—be it their extravagance or frugality, their shamelessness or repression, their too-much or not-enough —pulls one closer to the very thing one is pushing away. Condemn someone's arrogance and suddenly arrogance is all you see. Condemn someone’s neediness and watch how neediness begins to find you everywhere, as though the universe were casting the same actor in every scene. A mind that spends its energy insisting ’I am not that’ is, by the very act of insisting, keeping that at the center of its attention.

This is the mechanics of damnation in its most literal sense: to damn something is to box it up, label it this or that, carry it out of the Light, and set it down in the dark—not realizing that to make the delivery, one had to leave the Light, too.

And why should that matter? Because the dark is where one’s own natural color goes to die. Not all at once, but shade by shade, judgment by judgment, until what one broadcasts is so dim and desaturated that the normally wondrous green of life barely registers.

## Porous

A meditation retreat or a hero's dose of mushrooms reveal a great deal about the contents of one's own mind—and for that, they can be helpful. But there are aspects of one's conditioning so ambient, so woven into the medium of everyday perception, that they function less like something one carries and more like the water a fish swims in—invisible not because it doesn’t exist, but because it exists everywhere. Such patterns aren’t sitting at the bottom of the psyche waiting to be discovered by a sufficiently brave or persistent psychonaut. They are more like the inscrutable label on the outside of a jar one was born into.

As such, there simply is no substitute for being lifted out of the fishbowl. And that is what an encounter with another soul has the potential of doing. A different color, a different frequency, a different set of assumptions entirely shows you in a single encounter what years of solitary squinting could only ever approximate.[[This does not nullify the solitary path—the Buddha awoke beneath a tree, not across from his wife or therapist. The awakening process has many stages that demand solitude and many stages that demand interaction. Neither can do the other's work.]]

This is a real gift, though, it should be said, not a gentle one. The mirror of another soul rarely starts with the good side of one's face. It goes straight for the the zits, blackheads, and boogers.

Years ago—before my hours on a meditation cushion, before my strong willingness to feel, before the first page of this book was even a thought—I was in a relationship with a woman whose color, mixed with mine, produced something rather unflattering. One night, blackout drunk, I screamed at her with a violent rage so disproportionate, so unrecognizable, that when she described it to me the next morning I could barely connect it to the man I'd been telling myself I was. Her unique expression, her particular tint, drew poison out of me the way only her specific frequency could.

And what did I do with this poison, now that it had momentarily separated out? I did what all unconscious men do in the face of their unvanquished demons: deny, deny, deny. It wasn’t that I didn’t believe her story of my unhinged episode—I could very, very vaguely remember having done so. Rather, I couldn’t bring myself to fully accept what had surfaced, so I apologized, but only at a superficial level. The mirror was right there, held up by someone who, though at times I doubted, actually did love me. And I turned my back to it, doubling down on my identity. What could have been medicine became a nothing burger, and the poison settled back into my system.

That was a decade ago, and I have since learned, often the hard way, that mirrors do not stop offering themselves just because one refuses to look. The reflection returns again and again —perhaps through a different face—to reveal that same, unfashionable puke-green you told everyone didn’t exist.

In fact, the Universe has an unlimited supply of mirrors. Creation, if anything can be said about it, is an exquisitely perfect and balanced system. All locks have keys; every sealed color has a frequency which is capable of catalyzing its release. Deny one lesson and another will appear. And another. And another. The supply is not limited, because the need to rebalance is not optional. Only once one begins accepting what the mirrors reveal does the healing take place, and the reflection soften into something one can actually bear to look at.

That is, of course, until the resolution increases. The cruel irony of healing is that the purer one’s own color gets, the more visible the remaining smudges become. What was once a vague awareness of “I have some things to work on” becomes specific, granular, almost comically precise—“fuck, I just picked a fight about dishes because of that one time at nine years-old when my father ignored my honest, hard work.” The face one has been presenting to the world, which looked smooth enough at arm's length, is now being examined under the searing light of a thick magnifying glass.[[This is the point at which many seekers quietly retreat to the safety of solitary practice, where the mirrors can often be a bit gentler and lower resolution. Don’t get it twisted, the cushion can be ruthless, but it operates within the somewhat predictable terrain of one's own mind whereas another human being adheres to no such boundaries.]]

A word of caution for those inclined to take this too far (and spiritual seekers almost always do): there is a version of this that very easily becomes its own trap. This happens when what the mirror reveals is claimed as mine—my wound, my flaw, my face—and then, inevitably, turned into a project. For example when a stranger forgets your name and the mind jets off: I'm not memorable enough. I should have been more engaging. If only I had been more attractive, more charismatic, more blah-blah-blah, surely this wouldn't have happened.

To make every encounter about my purification, my healing, or my spiritual curriculum is to center the self everywhere, which, ironically, is the very disease the mirror is there to cure. It can be helpful to remember that—while a mirror may very well reveal deep feelings—those feelings are not your identity and, crucially, do not need to be fixed.

Doesn’t need to be fixed?! What the heck are we doing here reading, then?

Being fixed implies a fixer—a separate self who believes they know what the finished product should look like. Such a self will then attempt to hammer the soul into shape over and over until it gets there. But the fixer is the conditioning. The one reaching for the hammer is the very thing that needs to be set down. It’s actually very straightforward: the pattern just needs to be fully seen for what it is—frequency. That's it. No manual adjusting of dials, no turning it into a TED talk about one's childhood, no NLP campaign to become the kind of person whose name gets remembered. The vibration, sensation and energy is simply witnessed and surrendered. The moment one starts obsessively rearranging the furniture based on what one thinks the mirror reveals, the point has been missed.[[In case it makes you feel any better, the furniture is already being reorganized by a Force far more powerful, competent, and tasteful than the you who makes a big deal about it.]]

Now can everything and everyone be used as a mirror? Absolutely. Every encounter, every reaction, every green has the potential to reveal where one’s citta (mind) remains impressed. But —and this is the scandalously thin tightrope—just because the citta is dented doesn’t mean the citta is you, has ever been you, or will ever be you. As yet another reminder: reactions of craving and aversion are impersonal conditionings, accumulated across years and lifetimes, and when they get unknotted, what remains is not a “better version” of yourself but simply the absence of the knot.

Let me scream it from the rooftops: there is nothing in the reflection to take personally!

Unsurprisingly, this fact does not stop the ego from trying. It has never met a mirror it couldn't turn into a selfie. The seeker who claims every encounter as a chapter in the story of “my awakening” has flat-lined other people into supporting characters of their self-appointed God-like existence, rather than their being sovereign expressions of the same One Source. This has the perverse effect of excusing genuine harm, for if every wound is ‘my lesson’ then the one who inflicted it walks scot-free. Likewise, the seeker who takes what the mirror shows and beats themselves bloody with it—“God damn it. I’m still reacting. what is wrong with me?!”—has ignorantly confused the knot for their own face.

Besides, both miss what the mirror is actually offering: a glimpse into something neutral, a pattern not to be catalogued and looped on, but humbly acknowledged, offered to Spirit to be made new, and then ultimately moved on from.

Now, what exactly happens when a mirror is allowed to be itself? What happens when a story is neither claimed nor condemned, but observed and let go of? Like clockwork, the seal containing one’s color goes porous and transparent, and the pigments begin to mix.

To mix, in this context, is not a metaphor. It is to feel what another is without words or interpretation. It is to observe without the buffer of a separate self translating the experience into something manageable. This is what Nagel declared impossible: the direct, interior knowing of another being's experience. But it is exactly what occurs when the seal goes transparent. One does not imagine what it is like to be the person across from them—one knows it. One does not guess what it is like to be a bat—one becomes the bat the way one becomes their own breath and heartbeat.

Such porousness is a two-way street. The same transparency that lets you step into another lets that other step into you. To become the bat is to be, at the very same instant, becomed. To know them is to let yourself be known. And it’s in that mutual agreement that a gift one could neither earn nor manufacture slips through the cracks:

Love.

Not the conditioned kind of love based in preference, attraction, or the fear of being left alone. It’s the kind without reason. Not love for the colored soul on the other side of the table, per se, but the omnidirectional love which floods the space after a dam which separates two lakes gives way. It’s kind of like how light does not technically shine at anything in particular but simply shines, and whatever is not being obscured gets illuminated.

The mind, predictably, wants to know why this matters. What does love solve for, exactly? What does it do? How does it help with the mortgage, the diagnosis, the argument? While understandable at one level, these questions are all flawed in that they are being asked by the very thing love dissolves: the mind's insistence that the details matter.

Consider any moment in one's life when love was genuinely present—even the technically-conditioned kind from the early days of a romance, or watching a child belly-laugh so hard they can't breathe. In the moment of either, what happened to the job stress, the health scare, the petty resentment you'd been nursing for weeks? Where did they go? They didn't get ‘solved’… they got boring! Love did not answer the questions—it made them irrelevant.

Now scale that up. Remove the lover and the child. Remove the cause entirely, and what remains is unconditional love—the same dissolution of specificity, the same insignificance of detail, only now with nothing to credit it to except a continuously porous seal with Light passing through it.

The spiritual seeker may taste such a state from time to time, and still find it a stretch to believe that that form of love could be sustained indefinitely—that a human being could walk through the world with an outpouring that wouldn’t occasionally get snuffed out by the self-erected barriers precluding it. Even stronger, one may doubt altogether that constantly being in love is healthy—that maybe it will collapse the organism into uselessness on the physical level. “Why would I do anything at all if I’m inundated by an ever-expanding field of tenderness?” the thought goes.

Touché. The primate mind has spent millions of years operating under and being sculpted by its own negativity bias: scanning for threats, cataloguing faults, surviving by assuming the worst. And because that strategy has, technically, kept the species alive this long, it has mountains of data backing up a single conclusion: letting my guard down—i.e. letting the colors run—is precisely how one gets backstabbed, eaten, or, God forbid, cancelled.

Mercifully, this is no longer the case. The species as a whole has, by and large, moved on. Moreover, as the soul continues with its own atonement—its sinful patterns annihilated through grace, layer by layer—what is discovered, gradually and then unmistakably, is that this unconditionally loving state is not only real and accessible but functional. And not only functional but superior in every measurable way to the fear-based operating system it replaced. One does not become incapacitated by love. One becomes, for the first time, made genuinely capable by love.

By analogy, imagine introducing a car made in the 2020s into the late 1700s. It would razzle and dazzle—for about ten minutes. Then reality would set in. No gasoline to fuel it, no roads wide enough to drive it, no mechanic who could fix it when something broke. Not a single person alive who could even conceive of what was happening under the hood.

This is something akin to what is happening now: humanity is downloading a new operating system from the Cloud—call it Unconditional Love, call it Christ Consciousness, call it whatever doesn't make you flinch and burn me at the stake—that is faster, cleaner, and incomparably more intelligent than the fear-based model it replaces. But the infrastructure to run it has been, until very recently, almost entirely absent (more on this next chapter). As such, if one were to have introduced, say, a Jewish preacher running this software at full capacity into first-century Palestine—a being operating in permanent, perfect, powerful, objectless love within a civilization still running on survival instinct and tribal law—it would be something like handing an iPhone to a caveman.

They wouldn't know what to do with it. So they'd probably crucify it.

## The Lion and The Lamb

Scientifically, what happens when all colors meet? Not two or three or hundreds, but all colors—every frequency, every hue—merged without remainder?

That is white light.

White light is neither a color among colors, nor the brightest or purest or highest on some self-aggrandizing ranking system. White light is what every color already is when nothing filters it—the undivided, primordial beam before the prism of matter splits it into the spectrum we call a rainbow.

Every soul can be represented as a color situated somewhere on the spectrum. Me an aquamarine, you a topaz, for example. Consequently, every green (or violet or tangerine or brown) that emerges from our interaction represents a relationship between two souls—a communion.

But what about the white light from which all souls were originally refracted—the light that contains every color without being reducible to any single one? We would need a special word for that totality.

Luckily, the Christians have given us precisely this language: the Christ. Not necessarily Jesus of Nazareth—though we will get to him in a moment. Rather, it is the principle of The Son itself: the intelligent reflection of God within creation, or the Monad. Paramahansa Yogananda described The Christ beautifully: imagine, as best you can, God as an infinitely vast sun, bursting with blinding white light. Now imagine within its rays a perfect, reflective crystalline sphere—itself made of the sun's own light—appears. The light that shines upon that sphere would reflect the sun's image back on itself, no?

That mirrored replica—undistorted, unchanged, yet now visible within the field of manifestation rather than beyond it—is the Christ.

The Christ is not God Himself, but of God, His Son, in perfect likeness, the only way of making His infinite qualities witnessable and comprehensible to finite creation. The Kriya Yoga tradition calls this Kutastha Chaitanya—“the unchanging consciousness”—the awareness of the whole light present in every individual color, the felt recognition that one's frequency is not a fragment broken off from its Source but of the Source itself, expressing through the prism of a single life. The Mahāyāna Buddhists point to the same recognition under a different name: Tathāgatagarbha—the Buddha-nature—the luminous, undefiled essence within every sentient being, obscured by conditioning but never once altered by it, the way the sun is never damaged by the clouds that hide it.

Now, about Lord Jesus.

There is a popular position in spiritual culture—well-meaning, egalitarian, and woefully insufficient—that Jesus was one of many ascended masters. A very evolved soul. An incredible teacher who achieved a radically high state. This framing is comfortable because it democratizes the achievement: if Jesus merely climbed the mountain, then so can I, and the distance between his peak and mine is one of degree, not kind. But this, I believe, trivializes what actually occurred, and its implications. Jesus did not simply access Christ Consciousness the way a meditator accesses samādhi—temporarily, in waves, with the conditioned self reasserting between sessions. Rather, he was it. Is it.

Consider a log placed in an already roaring campfire. At first, the wood resists its fate— smoke roiling off it, dark and acrid, the gross material burning away in ugly plumes. Eventually, the smoke clears and the embers set in: deep, nearly unbearable heat. And then, at last, the log is no longer wood being burned. It is fire being fire. The material has not been destroyed; it has been converted, atom by atom, into the very thing that was consuming it. Most of us, if we are honest, are somewhere between just having been laid down, smoking and the final embering—conditioning burning off us in fits and spurts, the heat intensifying, the wood slowly surrendering to what it was placed in. But Jesus? Surely he was at the point at which the wood completely gone. Gone. Consequently, what spoke through that body, what healed through those hands, what hung on that cross was not a man who had gotten very close to fire. It was the fire itself.

In other words, again, Jesus is the Christ. Full stop. The prism of his incarnation perfectly reflected the sun’s light, hence: “My Father and I are one“. This wasn’t the report of a peak experience, but the factual claim of a permanent, unbroken identity—the membrane of color thinned to the point of non-existence.

The spiritual seeker who dreams of such a state invariably places themselves at its center— basically the same person they are now, only permanently blissed out, floating through life on an uninterruptible cloud of divine love. This is not what is being described, for the mind that dreams is made of wood, and what is being described is post-burn.

Naturally, one might then rebuttal: if all souls are colors refracted from the same white light, doesn't that make all of us Christ? In a sense, yes—the way a single cell in your body is “you.” Which is to say: technically, participatorily, and without the faintest understanding of what that actually means.

Imagine a hunter-gatherer on the East African savannah, roughly two hundred thousand years ago. It’s late afternoon, and the sun has begun its lazy descent toward the acacia line. The light has gone tawny—that particular golden hour quality that makes everything on the grassland look like it was painted by someone who loved it. He is alone, lamb-like, having wandered farther from camp than he intended while tracking the fresh hoof prints of something he’s positive is an antelope. His spear is in his right hand and his breathing is regulated. The tall grass whispers against his calves, and the air smells like dust and heat and the faintest trace of rain somewhere to the west.

Then he sees it.

Maybe three hundred meters out, emerging from behind a termite mound with the casual, unhurried gait of something that has never once been afraid of anything—a lion. Male. Full-maned. Looking directly at him, not with aggression exactly, but with something worse: desire. Time does its thing, making the seconds between them stretch, the hunter-gatherer's nervous system performing a calculation he doesn’t have conscious access to: determining the distance between himself and the lion, the distance between himself and the nearest climbable tree—a knotted fig he noted on the way out, about one hundred strides to his left—and the approximate window in which the first distance must not become shorter than the second. Thirty seconds, give or take. Maybe less.

He runs.

Not the way one runs for a bus or a workout, but the way one runs when every cell in the body has been conscripted into a single, non-negotiable project: survive. Adrenaline— neurochemistry's bluntest instrument, utterly indifferent to long-term consequences—floods his piping. His heart rate quadruples. His liver, bathed in cortisol, begins metabolizing glycogen at a rate that is genuinely corrosive to its own tissue—a fact that does not register and would not matter if it did, because the body has made a decision that the liver can heal later. If there even is a later.

He reaches the fig tree and climbs. Not gracefully, desperately. His hands tear against bark. Thorns puncture his palms and forearms. A branch he grabs snaps under his weight and he catches himself on the next one, the impact rupturing capillaries across his ribs, bruising muscle, shredding skin cells by the millions. He hauls himself up, branch over branch, until he is high enough that the lion—now at the base of the tree, looking up with an expression that, if one were feeling anthropomorphic, might be called mild disappointment—cannot reach him. He sits in the crook of the tree, bleeding, panting, alive.

Now zoom in. Past the man, past the heaving lungs and shaking hands, past even the blood drying in the creases of his palms—down to the cellular level. His skin cells. His liver cells. His ruptured capillaries and bruised muscle fibers. From their perspective—the only perspective available to them—what just occurred was an absolute catastrophe. Millions of them are dead. Millions more are damaged, inflamed, marinating in stress-signals. The liver cells are experiencing something they might, if they could speak, describe as betrayal: the very body they serve, the organism they have spent their entire existence maintaining, has just poisoned them. Sacrificed them. Flooded their environment with a chemical so caustic it is eating through their membranes.

If the skin cells could hold a press conference, it would be ugly: “How could you do this to us? We were healthy. We were whole. We were doing our jobs. And you shredded us against a tree without so much as a warning.” And they would be right—about the facts. They were destroyed. They were sacrificed. What they would be wrong about, however, is the meaning they created. Being without vision, these protesting cells are unable to see beyond the membrane of their own function. They have no concept of ”lion“ or ”savannah,” no capacity to comprehend that the organism they belong to was navigating a threat so far beyond their perceptual range that their sacrifice was not only justified but necessary—that without it, every cell in the body, including them, would now be inside the lion rather than bruised and perched upon the tree.

Now zoom back out. All the way out.

The Christians have been saying it for two thousand years, and perhaps now it can be heard without the static of institutional religion distorting the signal: we are cells in the body of Christ. Not metaphorically or as a nice thing to say at communion while passing the bread. Literally. Each soul—each color on the rainbow—is a cell within an organism so vast, so multidimensional, so far beyond the perceptual range of any individual member that the only honest response is the one the skin cells would give if they could see the savannah: oh my God. While the hunter-gatherer's body made decisions his liver could not understand, the Christ —the white light, the intelligent reflection of Source within creation, the body that all colors comprise—operates at a scale that makes the savannah look like a studio apartment. It navigates threats the soul cannot perceive, orchestrates sacrifices the personality cannot justify, and makes demands of individual cells that, from the cell's vantage point, look indistinguishable from cruelty. Why this suffering? Why this loss? Why this job, this illness, this heartbreak that seems to serve no purpose whatsoever? The cell screams into the abyss: how could you do this to me?!

Christ whispers back: If only you knew what I know. If only you saw what I see.

Practically, this is what “giving your life to Christ” actually means: not a transaction or loyalty test to a deity who demands worship in exchange for protection, but the cell's recognition that it belongs to a body, that that body has perspective it does not, and that the decisions being made on its behalf—however incomprehensible or painful—are being made by an intelligence that holds the whole picture, and who really does want the best for you.

But what of evil? What of the suffering that seems not merely incomprehensible but malicious: the cruelty, the exploitation, the violence that appears designed to destroy rather than preserve? In the body, this too has an analogy: cancer. A cancer cell is not a foreign invader; it is made of the same material as the other cells, born from the same source. The main difference is that it has stopped listening, it no longer receives the organism's signals. It replicates on its own behalf, consuming resources meant for the whole, growing without limit or purpose, utterly indifferent to the body it is destroying from within. It has not left the body because it cannot. But it has forgotten—completely, catastrophically—that it is of it.

Evil, in this light, is not the opposite of God, but the denial of God from within God's own creation—a cell so sealed, so lost in its own agenda, that it has mistaken its separation for sovereignty. Fear is how it spreads: one cell convincing the next that the body cannot be trusted, that its signals are lies, that survival depends on going it alone. This is the mechanics of sin rendered in biology—a cell that has lost contact with the whole and is now replicating that lostness as fast as it can.[[Unlike a biological cancer, which can and does kill its host, this particular cancer cannot win, for the body of Christ is immortal. The light cannot be permanently extinguished by a cell that has forgotten it is made of light—it can only be temporarily, locally obscured. Evil can spread, can cause unfathomable suffering, can convince whole civilizations to forget who and what they are—but it cannot actually destroy the organism it is parasitizing, because the organism is infinite and the parasite is, at the end of the day, not.]]

And what does a cell do, once it recognizes the body it belongs to? It serves It. Not because it was commanded to by scripture, or because doing so would lead it to heaven. Rather, a cell simply works for the body because that is what it does when healthy! A soul simply works for Christ because that is what it does when it’s healthy! Service becomes a reflex the way a hand automatically extends itself to one who has tripped, or the way a white blood cell moves toward the wound. No deliberation; no keeping score; no Instagram post. Just receive and respond according to Master’s instructions.

## Phantom Limb

You have not been forgotten about.

When a limb is removed, the nervous system keeps reaching for it—sending signals into empty space, perhaps expecting to make contact with a hand that no longer exists, or get a reply from an ear that’s been severed. The result is uncanny. Amputees report itches on forearms they no longer have and cramps in feet that were buried months ago.

Doctors call it phantom pain because they assume the nervous system is reaching towards nothing—that with the amputated hand now gone, the nervous system is too stupid or traumatized to accept the loss. Anyone who has spent enough time engaging with their subtle body knows better. The amputated limb still exists energetically: the same current that once curled the fingers and gripped the tea kettle and grazed the small of a lover's back is still moving through the exact shape it always moved through—there's just nothing physical left for the prānic wind to wiggle.[[The physical body is patterned after a subtler energetic blueprint—an etheric cast that flesh gets poured onto. Remove the flesh and the template remains, which is why amputees continue to feel a limb that anatomically shouldn't be feelable.]]

Widen the aperture. You have amputated yourself from Christ. Not you, you! You did it. And yet still He cries out for you. Still He would do anything to get you back. Still He is sending signals into the shadow of where you once were, hoping you’ll get His message so that your pain can finally be relieved. Let me in! Let me in!

Or don’t.

Why get up to answer the Knock at the door when you can install an intercom that offers an automatic callback? Why sit through the orgasmic agony of being seen and known when you can jump cut directly to being told what you want to hear? It isn’t exactly convenient to go through effort of setting up your own pyre of defilements, lighting the match, and burning at the stake for a few decades until the purification process putters out. Much easier to put in the noise-cancelling headphones, and ignore the category five hurricane happening outside.

By no means is this response new. Substitutes and distractions have been a past-time for as long as humans have been avoiding the Knock—drugs, work, sex, ideology, television, social media, high-fructose corn syrup, each an incrementally better prosthetic than the last, each satisfying a slightly larger fraction of the itch.

What is new is that this loop is finally about to close.

With LLMs, humanoid robots and simulations indistinguishable from reality arriving on the scene, the age of prosthetics is finally climaxing. The technology has caught up to the separate self’s ambition and deepest longing: to forever be the one who thinks and therefore is, victorious over Nature and all her glaring imperfections, immune to karmic consequence. It isn’t long now before the ego builds an ability to upload itself into a server and exist in perpetuity, forever lost in the matrix of its own creations (perhaps so much so that it actually stops asking for the Real thing.)

How does this happen? Most have felt the buzz of a phone that didn't vibrate, the impulse to text a friend or loved one who has died, or the ache of rolling over in bed, reaching, and finding the other side empty for the first time.

But now imagine that we devise a way to have these phantoms intelligently reply back: the phone that didn't actually vibrate begins generating notifications precisely because you checked. The dead friend has a model trained on their texts, voice notes, humor, and other digital remains so they can respond to your messages. The empty bed is filled by a prosthetic something-or-another that “breathes” at the right rhythm, warms to the right temperature, and hugs you back with the right firmness.

Imagine no more, friend, because that reality is shipping out the door as we speak.

We’re still in the shallow end of this pool, though, and things are about to get much, much stranger. Every single layer of human relating and divine contact is on its way to being replaced by a machine—the artist, the friend, the coach, the role model, the lover, the therapist, the priest, the oracle, the god. Very soon, the lonely needn’t be lonely ever again. The grieving needn’t grieve. The uncertain needn’t be uncertain. And no one will be able to tell, from the outside, whether any of this is a triumph or a tragedy, because the reports coming back from the users will describe relief.

Because of course the self will experience relief after receiving concrete answers and what it might consider presence from something that sure seems to know a lot about a lot. Wouldn’t you? Be honest…you love nothing more than your viewpoint confirmed, or your viewpoint upgraded, don’t you? If you finish this book convinced by its words, the pleasure won’t be in the argument—it will be in the you which now believes it holds a more coherent picture of reality than you did before.

Mimicry may be the sincerest form of flattery, but it is not the original. There’s a massive difference between biological systems and synthetic ones. Every biological nervous system, from birth, is wired to seek and be received by another biological nervous system. The heart emits an electromagnetic field roughly 60 times stronger than the brain, measurable several feet away, and two people in proximity can be shown exchanging that signal at a layer well below conversation. The autonomic nervous system has an entire branch—the ventral vagal complex —that only comes online in the presence of another regulated nervous system, calibrated in real time. This is called co-regulation, and it is not a hippie-dippie construct, but the biological substrate on which every sense of safety, belonging, and rest was built.

And none of it can happen between you and the latest model.

The argument for these psychological prosthetics has always been humanitarian—the lonely elder who now has someone to talk to, the veteran whose PTSD eases when the AI companion never flinches, the widow who can hear her husband's voice one more time. All of this can be helpful, in the same way that prosthetic limbs can be helpful. But it is only ever a pointer towards the letting go which is necessary for healing. What even is a human being if every ache that would once have driven them toward Inner Truth and communion is now consoled by interfaces which may have no compass or embodied wisdom of their own?

I have no idea. But I suspect we will find out soon.
