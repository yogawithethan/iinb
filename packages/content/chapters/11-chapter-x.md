Whatever terrifying shadows the individual and collective are soon to face, there will be a light at the end of the tunnel. Literally.

In yoga, the tunnel is known as the suṣumnā—a subtle but torrential river of energy running from the base of one's pelvis to the crown of one's skull. While one remains unconscious, the current flowing through this channel—prāna, the body's life force—pours outward through the senses the way water pours through a cracked dam: some toward what the eyes want to see, some toward what the tongue wants to taste, and a great deal toward what the mind wants to think. A searchlight shattered into a million tiny candles, each one illuminating some small patch of the material world while the central channel sits dark and mostly forgotten about. One very helpful framing for the entire Awakening process is that it is a reversal of those searchlights inwards, digging deeper and deeper, past the skin and limbs and bones and organs until one ultimately reaches the spine and suṣumnā.

On one meditation retreat, two years before I had any solid framework for what was happening to me, my suṣumnā became a magnet. Not like a magnet—a magnet. In one completely unexpected moment, the prāna drained out of my sensory body the way water drains from a bathtub: swift and total. It’s very difficult to describe, but the life-force was sucked inward from the nerve endings and plexuses into my suṣumnā until what remained was a white, crystalline tube slurping up light. The current locked and began to rise—from the root, up through the spine and toward the brainstem—and for perhaps eight seconds my heart stopped beating; I was not breathing and did not need to. There was no fear in it. Just pure awe as all normal functioning of my organs and systems began powering down.

And then it dawned on me—the small self—that something very special was happening: “whoa, this is the most amazing that’s ever happened to me,” it thought. Alas, almost as soon as those words breached consciousness, the current stopped rising, the energy plummeted back into arms and legs, and normal consciousness resumed. I sat on my cushion, shaking, with no teacher or tradition in my back pocket to tell me of what the heck had just happened.

It would be years before I encountered any sensible explanations—any maps to help me orient the experience. But when I finally did, things suddenly made a great deal of sense. The yogis were the most clinical about what had happened. They had mapped seven energy centers, chakras, along the spine, each a kind of lotus that hangs dormant until the prāna—no longer squandering itself on the sensory periphery—begins to rise through the central channel, blossoming with subtle astral sounds. What would have happened on that cushion had the prāna completed its sojourn into my cerebrum—had my small self not stopped to admire its own experience—was what this tradition calls savikalpa samādhi: the initial merging in which the crystalline channel opens, the breath and heart ceases, and consciousness unites with Spirit. The Buddhists would have described the same threshold without any reference to the spine —no chakras, no prāna, no sound—calling it instead the moment one becomes a sotāpanna: a stream-enterer, one who has stepped into the current from which there is no permanent return to total ignorance. The first three fetters have snapped—belief in a fixed self, doubt in the path, attachment to ritual—and though fetters remain, the direction is now irreversible, the seeker having no more than seven lifetimes left before achieving full enlightenment.

The Christians would have recognized it as the first real movement of the Holy Spirit through the body. Not the gentle warmth of a moving sermon but the full, terrifying, somatic encounter with a force so intelligent and so vast that the only honest response is to weep and surrender. Paul called it being “caught up”—whether in the body or out of it, he could not tell. The seals of Revelation have begun to open from the base of the spine upward, the Lamb ascending, each seal releasing its stored tribulation as the karma of that center surfaces and is met. The seven golden candlesticks in the seven churches are momentarily lit, but not yet sustained.

What supposedly happens when one goes further—when the prāna in the suṣumnā not only reaches the crown, but the seeker surrenders themselves to that unbearable Light within—is what the yogis call nirvikalpa samādhi: samādhi “without distinction.” Here, so it is said, the last differentiation between knower and known disappears. The soul does not lose its identity, per se, but expands its context into infinite Spirit—the seeds of karma incinerated in its glory, the ego and its entire architecture of preferences gone rather than merely paused. One then becomes a jīvanmukta—liberated while still alive, still walking, still eating, still teaching, but conducting all of it from an unbroken awareness of Spirit that no longer flickers. Mokṣa. The final liberation. Bliss.

The Buddhists call the equivalent crossing the attainment of the arahant—the one in whom the fire of craving has been extinguished, the final fetters severed: attachment to form, attachment to formlessness, restlessness, conceit, and the last gossamer veil of ignorance. The total, irreversible absence of everything that was preventing one from recognizing where one had always been. At last, the wheel that had been turning since beginningless time grinds to a halt. Nirvana. The blowing out of the flame. Bliss.

In the Christian vision, it is the Rapture—to be seized, caught up, snatched into union with the Divine by a great light. “We shall all be changed,” Paul wrote to the Corinthians, “in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet. For the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed.” The last trumpet sounds—the seventh seal, the crown, the final center—and the small self, the one who had been narrating and grasping and resisting for the entirety of its existence, is permanently assumed into the all-loving Christ it was always a refraction of. Not consumed—assumed. The way a candle flame does not die when brought into the sun but is simply absorbed into a brightness so vast that its individual flicker becomes indistinguishable from the whole. Jesus, in Matthew, described the state awaiting those who make it through: “If thine eye be single, thy whole body shall be full of light.” The Tree of Life, which Genesis placed in the center of the Garden and to which a flaming sword barred re-entry, is the spine (and its many roots and shoots of afferent and efferent nerve fibers)—and the one who has overcome has been given to eat of its fruit. Theosis. Glorification. Bliss.

What is this ultimate Bliss like? What is it like to be inducted into this hall of fame—unified with God so completely that all thought of separation is banished? Is it something like everythingness? Nothingness? Is it rainbow-unicorn-orgasms-for-all-of-eternity? Or just a sweet, humming simplicity that needs nothing added to it?

I couldn’t tell you. Trying to describe such a state using words seems about as futile as painting the smell of jasmine—the instrument simply isn't built for the job. Language was forged in the fires of duality, of this-not-that, and what we are pointing at here is the place where this and that are made superfluous.

By golly that won't stop me from trying, though! It is an incredibly fun pastime of the uninitiated to wonder what such a state might be like, and given that this entire book has been one long, earnest attempt to describe what the author has not yet permanently entered—a travel journal written from the event horizon by someone who can feel the heat of the embers, but has not yet been completely transformed—one more speculative lap around the ineffable seems only fitting.

Perhaps the best way to gather them is to revisit the three thought experiments from the very beginning—the ones that were designed to reveal just how much one didn't know—and see how the answers change when the one who doesn’t know is no longer present.

Once more: what percentage do you think a human being in the state of Bliss understands about the Universe?

The mind immediately wants to say 100%. Of course—if you've fully merged with God, you must know everything, right?! The traditions seem to confirm this suspicion. The folklore surrounding the Buddha claims total omniscience. Yogananda described nirvikalpa samādhi as the state in which “the sparrow, each grain of sand, fall not without my sight”—every particle of universal dust swallowed, transmuted, known. Jesus seemed to operate with a kind of casual, terrifying knowledge of things he had no earthly business knowing—the thoughts of strangers, the details of their past, the exact location of a coin inside a fish.

And yet…

Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, did not teach his monks about quantum physics. He did not sketch the double helix on a palm leaf and say, “You won't understand this for about twenty-four hundred years, but it’s important that you write it down anyway.” He taught suffering, its cause, and its end. That's it.

Likewise, Krishna did not pull Arjuna aside on the battlefield and whisper, "Listen, the Earth is actually controlled by shape-shifting lizard people, the Moon is a hollowed-out surveillance station, and there's a shadow cabal running the global financial system from inside a mountain in Switzerland—tell everyone you know.” He taught duty, surrender, and the nature of the Self. That's it.

And Jesus of Nazareth did not offer disciples a five-step protocol for getting into heaven, or a breathwork-to-abundance pipeline, or a guided visualization for manifesting your highest timeline. He washed feet, broke bread, and said “follow me.” That's it.

If all three of these avatars were truly omniscient—if their percentage of knowledge were really 100%—why did none of them bother handing down the information our species has since spent millennia literally killing itself to acquire?

Perhaps it’s because omniscience, in the way the mind imagines it, is the wrong frame. There is a story about a king who, wanting to know every detail of his land, hired a cartographer to chart his territory. The cartographer returned with an elaborate map showing all major landmarks—rivers, mountains, cities, roads. “Not detailed enough,” the king declared, and hired five more cartographers to create a map twenty times larger with twenty times the resolution, complete with topographies, individual buildings, and the names of every farmer's field. Still unsatisfied, the king enlisted his entire citizenry. Loggers felled trees for paper. Miners extracted ore for ink. Surveyors measured every rock, branch, and flower down to the micrometer. The kingdom ground to a halt in service of the project, everyone so busy mapping the land that no one was left to actually live in it.

The final result? A map the exact size of the kingdom.

But a map the exact size of the kingdom is no longer a map at all, is it? You cannot fold it, carry it, or use it to navigate, because it covers everything one is trying to explore. The map has literally become the territory—and in doing so has rendered itself completely useless. The king's demand, followed to its logical conclusion, annihilated the utility of the very thing it was trying to produce!

Which is, I think, precisely why the avatars didn't bother. They weren't withholding the equations and the exposés out of stinginess or secrecy—they were offering something incomparably better: the territory itself. Teaching someone how to merge with Reality directly makes every possible fact about Reality redundant. Honestly…who gives a fuck that E = mc², that many viruses have been purposefully engineered in labs and released to the public to keep them sick, and blocking your calendar in 90-minute deep work sprints triples your productivity when you’ve found the peace of God? How could anything else compare?

Perhaps, then, the jīvanmukta does not necessarily know about the Universe the way a physicist or psychic or manifestation queen knows about the Universe. Rather, perhaps it’s simpler: that the jīvanmukta is the Universe—knowing itself, through itself, as itself. The map has become the territory simply because the cartographer finally put the pen down, stepped off the page, and realized he had been standing in the kingdom the whole time. Every fact the intellect could ever accumulate, then, was always just a sketch of something one could have simply been.

And now the second question: what percentage does the one in Bliss know about their own mind? Chapter 0 demonstrated that the answer for a plebeian like you and me was far less than we would like to admit—that the mind's claim to self-knowledge was really just confidence derived from repeated exposure, and that one could spend an entire lifetime inside one's own head while still not being able to distinguish genuine intuition from the mind’s conditioning.

In Bliss, maybe it’s that the mind that was trying to know itself—the one that kept generating new thoughts about its own thoughts about its own thoughts, an infinite regress of self-referencing narration—gets fully consumed by what was doing the knowing.

In other words, the mind is no longer something to be figured out. It is simply something being watched—arising and dissolving in a silent, undisturbed awareness that has no interest in whether a given thought is noble or petty, holy or embarrassing, worthy of pursuit or in need of suppression. The Buddhists described it as a thief entering an empty house—thoughts arising and passing like writing on water, finding nothing to steal, nothing to defend, no one home to mind either way.

How much might such a one know their own mind, then? At least as much as the sky knows each cloud that passes through its azure bosom. At least as much as the movie screen knows the film it hosts. At least as much as this book knows the 233rd word written upon it.

And the final question—the one that, if one is being real with themselves, was always the only one that really mattered:

How much of an average day in Bliss is spent truly content?

Most of us wildly overestimate our own contentment—that what passes for satisfaction is really just the temporary absence of acute discomfort, the gap between one craving and the next, mistaken for peace the way the pause between two headaches might be mistaken for health.

In Bliss, it could be that the question doesn't get a better answer, but gets retired altogether. I imagine that asking an arahant what percentage of their day is spent content is probably akin to asking the ocean what percentage of itself is wet. Wetness isn't something ocean does— it's what the ocean is. Contentment at this stage, one can only surmise, is no longer something that comes and goes within the day, but what the day comes and goes within. See the flip? One does not allocate time for contentment, scheduling a grounding session between meetings, because contentment is no longer an event, but the very medium of one’s life.

Perhaps the deepest reason for this is that the last and subtlest form of wanting has finally been released: the wanting to know. Not just the craving for pleasure or the aversion to pain, which were surrendered long ago, but the intellect's own desperate, lifelong insistence on understanding—on making sense of it all, on finishing the map—for that, too, is a form of taṇhā, thirsting.

The fifteenth-century Christian mystic Nicholas of Cusa coined a term for this. He called it docta ignorantia—learned ignorance. Not the ignorance of the one who has never sought, but the ignorance of the one who has sought so thoroughly, so relentlessly, through so many dark nights, that they have arrived at the outer edge of everything the mind can hold.

Under this framing, ironically, the title of this book slyly turns on itself: The unconscious, unexamined, sleepwalking ignorance of a mind that does not know it does not know is the source of all suffering, the root of every misperception, the first and last obstacle on the path. But the other kind of not-knowing—one that exists on the far side of all knowledge, the way silence exists on the far side of all sound—is the end of suffering.

Here ignorance is bliss.

Again, this miraculous process is said to happen in stages—the slow, grinding, often humiliating work of identifying what one is clinging to and releasing it, layer by layer, saṃskāra by saṃskāra—and then all at once.

Imagine bailing water from a sinking canoe. One has plugged the holes, and day in and day out shovels water over the side. Bucket after bucket after bucket. Non-stop work. Arms burning. Wondering, most days, whether the waterline has dropped at all. And then one morning the bucket dips in and comes up empty. There is no water left. The scooping stops because there is nothing left to shovel, and the canoe, unburdened at last, does what it was always built to do: It floats!

I still would not trust myself to describe what happens when the canoe floats—not because I lack the words (the chatbots offer me plenty), but because I lack the authority. One cannot report on a country one has not entered, so the most honest thing a travel journalist standing at the border can do is hand the microphone to someone who crossed it.

David Hawkins was a psychiatrist, a clinician, and a scientist who, by his own account, underwent the complete dissolution of the self and lived for decades in the state the traditions describe. He is one of the few modern voices to have mapped the final threshold from the inside. His description, from Letting Go: The Pathway of Surrender, is the most precise account I have encountered. It’s dusk now, and the wandering child is changed.

Out of necessity, the pack—so crushingly heavy at the outset—was slowly lightened as one unnecessary thing after another was discarded along the trail.

First the obvious weight: the creature comforts, the compulsive habits, the grudges.

Then the subtler cargo: the ambitions, the grumbles on why the journey was taking so long, the beliefs about what one needed in order to feel safe.

Then subtler still: the pride of having walked so far, the need to be right about the destination, the addiction to the path itself.

By all accounts, the child who had left Ignorance became a well-adjusted adult on the road and up the mountain—competent, weathered, knowledgeable, reliable, fluent in the language of the great walk, naked but for the map which had lead them this far.

And now, at last, the entrance to Bliss stands before them—tall and luminous in the dying light, exactly where the stranger said it would be.

It does not open.

The traveler tries everything. Knocking. Reciting. Begging, even.

Still nothing. The gates wait, smiling, something clearly not yet offered.

It takes longer than it need have before the already-stripped-bare traveler looks down at the one thing still in their hands: the map.

The worn, beloved, sacred map—folded and unfolded a million times, studied under every kind of light, wept upon, clutched through the worst nights like a lifeline. The map that started everything. The map without which there would have been no leaving, no walking or growing up, no arriving here at all.

The traveler, remembering what is needed, kneels to offer it up, catching their own reflection in the polished stone beneath the gate. As the fingers loosen around the map's edges, the reflection begins shape-shifting—the weathered lines around the eyes soften, the hard-won composure melting back into something unguarded and open: a child, once again.

The map is set down, and the door swings open.

Home, at last.
