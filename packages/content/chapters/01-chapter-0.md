There is a child, born to a small village set deep in the western wilderness, thousands of miles away from civilization. Perhaps we call this little village ‘Ignorance,’ not as an insult, but as a simple fact. The child and their community grow up knowing only what the environment calls for: how to survive the cold, how to hunt, how to tend fire. Sure, living conditions in Ignorance are quite harsh by modern standards, but because no one remembers anything different, no one has any motivation to leave or change.

The little village of Ignorance has existed in isolation for so long that not even the elders carry stories of the past anymore. Accordingly, there is only what one is told is here and nothing more. A few villagers have left over the generations, seeking something beyond the tree line, but none have returned, only confirming what everyone already claims to know: *nothing worthwhile* *exists outside of Ignorance*.

Years pass and the child matures. Then one day, without warning, the village receives a visitor. The stranger talks with born conviction: “There is a great city—to the East—atop a high mountain. The city is called ‘Bliss’. Here is a map; you can reach it by foot in a matter of years if you hurry. Yes, the Path is long and arduous, but the payoff dwarfs any sacrifice, for it is a place of infinite warmth, abundance, and freedom. A place without even a trace of fear or lack. A place you can call home. Plus, you won’t be walking alone. You’ll meet others who’ve left their villages, too. And together the journey will become lighter.”

Because this claim contradicts everything the villagers have known, they brand the stranger a demon and lunatic, cast them out, and seize the map—burying it where it won’t tempt anyone. ‘Surely, Bliss cannot be real,’ the villagers convince themselves, ‘otherwise, why would anyone choose to stay in Ignorance?’

But while the other villagers carry on, the child is haunted by the visitor’s message. ‘What if it’s true?! What if this glorious city in the East is real!? And what if it can actually be reached?!’ Whenever this curiosity is expressed to family, a number of concerns are immediately brought up: ’The stranger might be lying.’ ‘You could die out there in the wilderness.’ ‘You’d never be accepted into such a place, anyways.’ Their points momentarily quell the maddening itch within the child, but not for long. After all, what if the stranger speaks truth? What if the journey is survived? What if one can be transformed to become worthy along the way?

The restlessness continues to boil and burn until one particularly dark night the child can bear it no longer. The yearning for Bliss has become too fierce to resist. Courage takes hold. ‘Ignorance may be where I was born, but it will not be where I will die.’

In one final attempt to get the child to abandon the Plan, the community threatens that leaving Ignorance behind will mean never being allowed to return. Either the child finds their way to Bliss, or is cursed to wander the lonely in-between for the rest of time.

The child hesitates before realizing that, actually, the decision has already been made and the journey already begun.

And so, at dawn, as the first light breaks over Ignorance, the child digs up the forbidden map from where the villagers hid it, shoulders a heavy pack with what seems essential for the journey, and takes the first step toward the promised land.

Let’s run a fun little thought experiment together…

What percentage do you think humans understand about the Universe? So if 0% means *humanity knows nothing about anything*, and 100% means *humanity knows everything about* everything, where would you estimate our species lies? Take a moment to intuit an honest guess. I’ve asked this to probably a dozen friends over the years and have always found it secretly amusing to watch their minds glitch while trying to come up with a reasonable-sounding answer. The majority of them begin at around 50%, first assuming that humans are pretty far along in this whole ‘knowledge’ thing. But the more they think it through, the more their face clouds over with concern. You can almost read their mind: “50%?! Do I really think that we are halfway to having the entire Universe explained?! That’s probably what the people in Isaac Newton’s time also thought, and they didn’t even know bacteria existed yet! We still don’t understand almost anything about the ocean, dark matter, epigenetics, time, or the brain; surely the answer must be way, way lower.” From there, they tend to walk themselves back in percentage points until they reach ~5% or less, concluding—with some hard-won humility— that humans have only just begun exploring the Playground they were born into.

Actually, all are excused for not being confident in their answer, for the thought experiment is fundamentally flawed. For one, you don’t know what you don’t know. Reducing human knowledge to a percentage point would require that you be perfectly informed on how infinite the Game of Life is, which—by definition—cannot be done with a finite brain. You’d need a Universe-sized computer of exactly the same dimensions and complexity as our own Universe just to map all of the information correctly. And if you had that…well…then you would simply be the very Universe that you were hoping to understand!

Are you following so far? Because you and I and everyone else have no way of accounting for what we don’t know—the unknown unknowns—we may as well be at 0% understanding as a species. Perfect ignorance.[[You can think of it like this: anything divided by infinity is zero. That means that the entire sum of human knowledge—every equation, every scripture, every peer-reviewed paper ever published, the whole groaning library of us—divided by everything there is to know—comes out to exactly nothing. The genius and the fool on the same square, equally far from the end of the board. Trippy, and the mind will fight it—surely we know something; look at the rockets and the skyscrapers and the device you're reading this on—but philosophically tidy.]]

And yet, how much of a difference does scientific knowledge make on the world? If you were to travel back in time 1,000 years, where technology was undeniably less than it is today, quality of life would be shocking. Sure, today has got its problems: inflation is inflating, chronic illness is epidemic’ing, and AI is doing whatever the heck it’s doing. But that sure beats literal bullshit leaching into the town’s drinking water, unfathomably high child mortality rates, slavery condoned everywhere, and women being reduced to property.

In other words, ignorance isn't just a theoretical problem—it's a practical one. When engineers don't know what shape the Earth is, they cannot send rockets or operate GPS. When scientists are ignorant of how differently male and female bodies function, women are prescribed drugs tested only on men. When science denies animal sentience, billions of creatures are subjected to vivisection and abuse on the assumption that their suffering doesn't truly count.

The real tragedy, then, isn't just that us humans are ignorant about how the Universe works —it's that we are ignorant about the degree of our ignorance, and so are unable to address (let alone solve) some of the most pressing crises now or in the future.

In order for this book to work on you, it’s very important to understand the point being made here, so let’s consider a further angle: the poverty of the human sensory system. Your eyes, for instance, can only make out a very narrow band of light from red to violet—a measly 1/28,000th of the known electromagnetic spectrum (emphasis on known). Your ears, too, even at their sharpest, can only pick up on pitches ranging between roughly 20Hz (low rumble) and 20,000Hz (dog whistle), even though those frequencies could hypothetically extend out indefinitely in either direction. More embarrassing still, your “precisely-calibrated” taste buds *can’t* reliably tell the difference between cheap, boxed wine and the 100 year-old stuff.

This is, in part, why us humans are so obsessed with technology. One very consequential thing gadgets do is arm us with an ability to peer past—or circumvent entirely—our limited sensory field. We use CT scanners to see inside the living body without having to cut it open. We develop carbon monoxide detectors to alert us when levels rise beyond what is safe (our noses *can’t* smell carbon monoxide, despite it being injurious to the body). We manufacture billions of smartphones to augment the mind’s memory reservoir. Look around with amazement at how virtually every single object you see was, at one point, humanity’s attempt at escaping its own lameness and besting ignorance once and for all.

And yet, the advent of any technology reveals previously unknown problems in its wake— problems society was previously ignorant to. It turns out the CT machines we use to detect and diagnose cancer dose the body with a non-zero amount of ionizing radiation—itself a well-established contributor to the very tumors it hunts. The air quality monitors meant to protect us from pollution require rare earth mineral extraction that themselves pollute the air. The smartphones that allow us to offload mental bandwidth fragment our attention and erode our capacity for sustained thought.

This is the pattern: every attempt to defeat ignorance through technology and so-called knowledge simply births new ignorance. Antibiotics solved infections but created superbugs. Social media cured boredom but mangled human attention. Ignorance, in this way, is a hydra whose heads keep multiplying as we swing the blade of scientific rigor and AI compute.

And yet, why does almost no one doubt this progress-hungry, solution-oriented approach to overcoming ignorance? It is taken as a given that better, faster, fancier technology (along with the necessary scientific breakthroughs that make them possible) will lead us towards a kind of utopia, far, far away from our miseries and blind spots. “Solving problems must be the only way to not have problems,” we assure ourselves minute by minute, day by day.

Scientific breakthroughs and technological innovation make for very sexy headlines, but what's the point when we fear the minds who play God with them? Our species can know absolutely everything there is to know about neuroscience, weather patterns, supply and demand, subatomic particles, and algorithmic efficiencies, and still be ever-closer to the brink of extinction. Why? Because the mind is the filter through which the present is perceived. Without evolving it at the same rate as science and technology, the species is poised to set off an invisible, technological landmine, one that could have been easily avoided through wisdom, prudence and cooperation.

Speaking of which, here’s another thought experiment…

What percentage do you think you know about your own mind? If 0% is you know absolutely nothing about your mind, and 100% is you know everything there is to know about your mind, where would you place yourself on that continuum?

When it comes to the existential stuff—the ’why am I heres’ or ’what does it all means’—this is the more important question to ask. And that’s because though the sciences can offer us a more comfortable life, they *can’t* offer us a more meaningful one. Meaning is generated from the inside-out, not the outside-in, and thus requires a large degree of self-awareness and understanding to cultivate.

To be clear, understanding yourself is not the same thing as accumulating information about yourself. To illustrate the point, imagine an intelligent AI companion whose job it is to track and document your entire life, recording and storing every word, every scene, every feeling, and every thought you have. Now imagine that on your final day amongst the living it compiles and outputs its data into one large document. Ask yourself, would reading this summary alone—no matter how detailed or poetic or psychologically revealing—bring meaning to the trials and tribulations, campaigns and victories you experienced? Of course not. It’s not the words that kindle this fondness for your life; it’s the words in relation to having had a felt experience that does. Remembering or reflecting upon the facts about your life will not bring you any closer to knowing and loving your life.

To put it bluntly: gaining more information about yourself has never been the missing ingredient for cooking up happiness. Look at the past century—humanity has gained more access to knowledge, biometric data, therapy modalities, self-help frameworks and genomics than every previous era combined, and yet rates of depression, anxiety, addiction, and loneliness have only climbed. All this alleged “self-knowledge,” and yet we're more miserable than ever. What gives?

Here’s what gives: *thinking you know is not knowing. It’s thinking*.

The mind can hold a piece of information indefinitely without it needing to change its behavior. Nearly everyone understands that our current consumption patterns are unsustainable for Mother Earth, yet only a slim minority are willing to give up their shopping addictions and chemical-laden self-care rituals in solidarity. Likewise, virtually everyone intuits the health benefits of fresh, organic produce, but few are those who make it the predominant percentage of their diet. And while almost everyone agrees that spending hours per day isolated on social media is a death sentence to one’s mental health, hardly anyone opts out. It’s quite obvious that just because one “knows” what is weakening the body, mind and planet does not mean one will do anything about it.

In the end, then, for the average person to say they know 50%—or even just 5%—of their mind *isn’t* saying very much at all. Who gives a fuck if you can name your attachment style and trace it to your mother if you still aren’t happy? Who gives a fuck if you have photographic memory of a life you were never actually present for?

Such is the fundamental difference between knowing something intellectually and knowing something experientially. Someone can know everything there is to know about Buddhist doctrine, yogic lore, Christian theology, psychedelic therapy, kundalini awakenings, quantum physics, history, brain wave states, and psychoneuroimmunology, and still be a miserable jack-ass.

Which reminds me of our final thought experiment…

How much of an average day do you spend being truly content? If 0% is absolutely no part of your day is spent satisfied and 100% is rampant satisfaction all day long—pure, meditative bliss from eyes open to eyes shut—where would you place yourself?

At this point, you are probably already wondering how this experiment is flawed, so I’ll tell you. For one, whatever your mind’s answer, the time you think you’re spending in contentment is likely an overestimate, for as heaps of psychological research make clear, people’s appraisal of their own overall experience almost always gets skewed when looking backwards.

This is because the unawakened mind is blind to itself, and therefore almost always overlooks the extent to which it complains about the present. So common are the thoughts “this moment would be ever-so-slightly better if only x were y, or y were x” that it fails to see them at all. Such is the classic example of one who, after being asked why they’re so upset, yells back, neck bulging: ‘I’m not upset!!!’[[This was made clear during a happiness study wherein participants carried pagers with them, reporting their contentment in the moment—whenever the thing beeped—rather than in retrospect. When those same participants were later asked to estimate how they had felt across the study period as a whole, the remembered figures came back consistently higher than the real-time ones, and sometimes dramatically so. The conceited mind, it turns out, doesn’t just want to believe it's content—it will actively rewrite its own history to prove it.]]

So rather than answering the thought experiment by looking backward at your day, try answering it right now. In this very moment of reading, are you content? Not ”sort of fine” or “not actively suffering”—but genuinely, fully at ease with what is here? Is there no part of the mind reaching for what comes next, no subtle itch to check the phone, no faint wish that you were a little more comfortable, a little less tired, a little further along in life than you are? If you checked honestly, you probably found a subtle background static of discontentment. And that’s precisely the point. The mind is so accustomed to this low-grade unsatisfactoriness that it *doesn’t* register it as discontentment at all. It’s like a refrigerator hum you’ve stopped hearing, or a persistent ringing in the ears that’s been there so long it feels like silence.

This is worth sitting with for a moment, because it reframes the thought experiment. The issue *isn’t* just that you overestimate how content you are when looking backward—it’s that you *may not know what contentment actually feels like at all*. And if you don’t know what contentment actually feels like, how could you possibly know how much of your day is spent there?

Stay with me here. Imagine someone who has only ever eaten unripe fruit: hard peaches, sour strawberries, bitter apples pulled too soon from the branch. Asked to rate the sweetness of their diet, they might very well say “ten out of ten”. And they wouldn’t be lying. Relative to the sourest thing they’ve tasted, the least-unripe peach they've had seems genuinely sweet. But hand them a single ripe mango on a warm afternoon, and their entire scale recalibrates in an instant. They discover that what they’d been calling sweetness wasn’t sweet at all; it was just the least sour option available.

Contentment works the same way. Most people’s experience of “being content” is really just the temporary absence of acute discomfort—a moment between wanting and wanting again, the gap between finishing one task and remembering the next one, the few glorious seconds after a sneeze. These are pleasant, sure, but not what contemplative traditions mean by peace. There is a quality of ease, bliss, available to human beings so far beyond the ordinary that hearing about it can sound absurd or exaggerated—like trying to describe color to someone who has only ever seen in black and white. And yet every wisdom tradition on earth insists it is real, and every serious practitioner confirms it through direct experience.

In other words, the average human mind hasn't a clue just how peaceful peace can really get. And that’s a problem, because it prevents the very seeking that would lead to the Divine gift of a truly free mind. If you so much as tasted a drop of that true bliss you would be willing to do anything—surrender any pride, any addiction, any resistance—just to savor its sweet nectar again.

This creates a peculiar Catch-22. If you’ve never tasted genuine contentment, you have no way of knowing what you’re missing. And if you have no way of knowing what you’re missing, you have no reason to go looking for it. And if you have no reason to go looking for it, you remain exactly where you are—managing discomfort, chasing relief, and telling yourself that this is more or less as good as it gets.

Which is precisely why the common expression “ignorance is bliss” has it exactly backwards. Ignorance *isn’t* bliss! Ignorance is what prevents bliss! Ignorance is the very thing standing between you and the depth of peace that every mystic, saint, and sage has tried (and failed, because it’s an impossible task) to put into words.[[The phrase should really read: “ignorance is kinda tolerable, so long as you never find out what you're missing.”]]

But even a glimpse behind the curtain changes things irreversibly. Once you taste what real peace feels like, even for a moment, ignorance and complacency become immediately untenable. It’s not that the world gets worse, per se, but because the contrast becomes undeniable: ‘*I had no idea how annoying that refrigerator hum really was!’* Now the seeker is left in an uncomfortable middle: too awake to go back to sleep, just awake enough to notice the suffering, not awake enough to access joy on-demand.

This is why the early stages of any spiritual path often feel like things are getting worse, not better. Fear not: *they aren’t*. You are simply becoming aware (perhaps for the first time) of the discontentment that was always there, like turning on the lights on a pitch-black room you’ve been stumbling around in and bruising your shins on.

This in-between is, for many, the most painful part of the journey, and therefore the part that most people quit during, retreating back to the village of Ignorance and doing their best to forget what they briefly saw. But for those who don't quit—for those who sense, even dimly, that their intuition is real and worthwhile—the only honest response is to keep walking. Not because the Path is even remotely comfortable or fun, but because once you’ve heard the hum, you can't unhear it. And pretending you can is its own kind of suffering.

Which brings us, at last, to the real question this book is asking. Not “what percentage of the Universe do you understand?” or “how well do you know your own mind?” or even “how content are you?” The real question is: Are you willing to find out what's on the other side of your ignorance, even if the finding-out means surrendering everything you currently believe about who you are and what will make you happy?

If the answer is yes!—or even a hesitant, half-terrified maybe—congratulations and welcome. A nice little map has been drawn out for you here. Now the journey home begins.
