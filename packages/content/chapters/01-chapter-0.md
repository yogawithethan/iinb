There is a child, born tn wilderness, thousands of miles away from civilization. [[This is a test to see how things are appearing on the footnote. Will it have the effect we're going for...? Who knows! Only time will tell.]] Perhaps we call this little village ’Ignorance,’ not as an insult, but as a simple fact, for the child and community grow up knowing only what the environment calls for: how to survive the cold, how to hunt, how to tend fire. Sure, living conditions in Ignorance are quite harsh by modern standards, but because no one remembers anything different, no one has any motivation to leave or change. 
This village has existed in isolation for so long that not even the elders carry stories of the past anymore. Accordingly, there is only what one is told is here and nothing more. A few villagers have left over the generations, seeking something beyond the tree line, but because none have returned, it only confirms what everyone already claims to know: nothing worthwhile exists outside of Ignorance.
Years pass and the child matures. Then one day, without warning, the village receives a visitor. The stranger talks with born conviction: “There is a great city—to the East—atop a high mountain. The city is called ‘Bliss’. Here is a map; you can reach it by foot in a matter of years if you hurry. Yes, the Path is long and arduous, but the payoff dwarfs any sacrifice, for it is a place of infinite warmth, abundance, and freedom. A place without even a trace of fear or lack. A place you can call home. Plus, you won’t be walking alone. You’ll meet others who’ve left their villages, too. And together the journey will become lighter.”
Because this claim contradicts everything the villagers have known, they brand the stranger a demon and lunatic, cast them out, and seize the map—burying it where it won’t tempt anyone. ‘Surely, Bliss cannot be real,’ the villagers convince themselves, ‘otherwise, why would anyone choose to stay in Ignorance?’
But while the other villagers carry on, the child is haunted by the visitor’s message. ‘What if it’s true? What if this glorious city in the East is real? And what if I can actually reach it?’
Whenever this curiosity is expressed to family, a number of concerns are immediately brought up: ’The stranger might be lying.’ ‘You could die out there in the wilderness.’ ‘You’d never be accepted into such a place, anyways.’ Their points momentarily quell the maddening itch within the child, but not for long. After all, what if the stranger speaks truth? What if the journey is survived? What if one can be transformed to become worthy along the way?
The restlessness continues to boil and burn until one particularly dark night the child can bear it no longer. The yearning for Bliss has become too fierce to resist. Courage takes hold. ‘Ignorance may be where I was born, but it will not be where I will die.’
In one final attempt to get the child to abandon the Plan, the community threatens that leaving Ignorance behind will mean never being allowed to return. Either the child finds their way to Bliss, or is cursed to wander the lonely in-between for the rest of time.
The child hesitates before realizing that, actually, the decision has already been made and the journey already begun. 
And so, at dawn, as the first light breaks over Ignorance, the child digs up the forbidden map from where the villagers hid it, shoulders a heavy pack with what seems essential for the journey, and takes the first step toward the promised land.

                                                                                                    

Let’s run a fun little thought experiment together…
What percentage do you think humans understand about the Universe? If 0% means humanity knows nothing about anything, and 100% means humanity knows everything about everything, where would you estimate our species lies? Take a moment to intuit an honest guess.
I’ve asked this to dozens of friends over the years and have always found it secretly amusing to watch their minds glitch while trying to come up with a reasonable-sounding answer. The majority of them begin at around 50%, first assuming that humans are pretty far along in the whole ‘knowledge acquisition’ game. But the more they think it through, the more their face clouds over with concern. You can almost read their mind: “50%?! Do I really think that we are halfway to having the entire Universe explained?! That’s probably what the people in Isaac Newton’s time also thought, and they didn’t even know bacteria existed yet! We still don’t understand almost anything about the ocean, dark matter, epigenetics, time, or the brain; surely the answer must be way, way lower.” From there, they tend to walk themselves back in percentage points until they reach ~5% or less, concluding—with some hard-won humility—that humans have only just begun exploring the Playground they were born into.
Actually, all are excused for not being confident in an answer, for the thought experiment is fundamentally flawed. For one, you don’t know what you don’t know! Accurately reducing human knowledge into a percentage would require that you be perfectly informed on how big and complex the Game of Life is, which—by definition—cannot be done from the finite, brain-bound purview. You’d need a Universe-sized computer of exactly the same dimensions and complexity as our own Universe just to map all of the information correctly. And if you had that…well, then you would simply be the very Universe that you were hoping to understand!
In other words, because humanity is ignorant about the degree and scope of its ignorance, we may as well be at 0% understanding, because, again, complete comprehension would require accounting not just for the unknown, but for the unknown unknowns—which is impossible by definition.
And yet, how much of a difference would it make for humans to truly be ”50% of the way there” (whatever that means) as compared to just 1%? If you were to travel back in time 1,000 years, where scientific knowledge was undeniably less than it is today, quality of life would be shocking. Sure, in today’s world inflation is still inflating and the chronic illness epidemic is still epidemic’ing, but that sure beats horse poop leaching into the town’s drinking water, abysmal child mortality, slavery condoned everywhere, and women being reduced to property.
In other words, ignorance isn't just a theoretical problem—it's a practical one. When scientists don’t understand large-scale climate patterns and air current shifts, they are unable to predict future temperature fluctuations, trusting and building upon faulty models of what’s to come. When engineers don’t know what shape the Earth is, they become incapable of sending rockets and operating GPS. When scientists misunderstand bacteria, they create antibiotics that create antibiotic-resistant superbugs. The real tragedy isn't just that us humans are ignorant about how the Universe works—it's that we are ignorant about the degree of our ignorance, and so are unable to address (let alone solve) some of the most pressing crises now or in the future.
Maybe the pill I’m offering can be more easily swallowed when considering just how frail the human sensory system is. Your eyes, for instance, can only make out very narrow band of light from red to violet—a measly 1/28,000th of the known electromagnetic spectrum (emphasis on known). That’s worth repeating: you only see 0.0035% of what physicists currently call reality! Consider yourself blind! Your ears, too, even at their sharpest, can only pick up on pitches ranging between roughly 20Hz (low rumble) and 20,000Hz (dog whistle), even though those frequencies could hypothetically extend out indefinitely in either direction. Deaf! More embarrassing still, your “precisely-calibrated” taste buds can’t reliably tell the difference between cheap, boxed wine and the 100 year-old stuff.
Even one’s prized mind and its admittedly impressive capabilities are laughably inadequate at sussing out what’s real and what’s made up. [Scientific Example] If it can barely remember what the body ate for dinner last Monday, how can it alone be expected to understand the cosmos or the body’s liver or even itself?
Despite almost everyone intellectually knowing and professing their perceptual and cognitive limitations, one really can’t help but treat themselves as omniscient. It’s the mind’s job to believe in its creations, after all. Take any childhood story you'd tell a stranger. Mine: ‘I skipped first grade and really struggled to fit in in my new class.’ This sob story is fourteen words long and takes all-of three seconds to say, but my mind is confident that it’s an accurate portrayal of what happened. Sure, it had to edit out (or downright ignore) months of relatively happy memories that occurred alongside the tougher ones in order to preserve the victim arc of its main character, but to the self-absorbed mind, that’s a worthwhile sacrifice.
This goes for all stories rooted in memory, by the way, not just those arising from childhood. Gossip about a family member, idolatry of a teacher, theories about how and where humans originated. Any story built from what one has observed is—by definition—based on filtered sensory data, which means it is always, at best, an incomplete sketch of things, and, at worst, a total fabrication of what really happened. And yet, if the story is mine, we tend to treat it as pure gospel, ignorantly projecting our convictions out onto an innocent world, arguing—often to the point of violence—that our perspective is the way things really are. It’s not. How could it be? Humans perceive so little of objective reality (at most 0.0035%, may I remind you!) it’s a wonder our species survives long enough to reproduce at all!
This is, in part, why us humans are so obsessed with making better technology. One very consequential thing gadgets do is arm us with an ability to peer past—or circumvent entirely—our limited sensory field. We use MRI and CT scanners to see inside the living body without having to cut it open. We develop carbon monoxide detectors to alert us when levels rise beyond what is safe (our noses can’t smell carbon monoxide, despite it being injurious to the body). We manufacture billions of smartphones to augment the mind’s memory reservoir. Look around and be amazed at how virtually every single object you see was, at one point, humanity’s attempt at escaping its own lameness and besting ignorance once and for all.
And yet, the advent of any technology unearths previously unknown problems in its wake—problems society was previously ignorant to. It turns out the MRI machines we use to detect and diagnose cancer dose the body with a non-zero amount of radiation, a well-known source and contributor of tumor growth. The air quality monitors meant to protect us from pollution require rare earth mineral extraction that pollute the air. The smartphones that allow us to offload mental bandwidth fragment our attention and erode our capacity for sustained thought.
Seen from an outsider's perspective, it appears that the story of humanity, in its never-ending quest to transcend its tiny sensory bubbles, is all about solving problems via the manipulation of its environment. Yet those very same outsiders might also point out that these manipulations always generate a new set of problems in their wake, requiring humanity to create a deeper and deeper involvement with the world—more data to track, more pieces to control, more to systems maintain. What was meant to liberate instead traps, for a problem solved is a problem created, and so humanity extends its reach only through establishing an ever-deeper entanglement with its creations.
Virtually no one doubts this progress-hungry, solution-oriented approach to life. It is taken as a given that better, faster, fancier technology (along with the necessary scientific breakthroughs that make them possible) will lead us towards a kind of utopia, far, far away from our miseries. After all, the world technically looks prettier than it did a few decades ago…and look how happy all the AI models in the advertisements seem! “Solving problems must be the only way to solve problems,” we assure ourselves.
Alas, “looking pretty” is not the same thing as beauty or intrinsic, human fulfillment. Though it’s easy to fool oneself, ask yourself: am I actually happier than I was five, ten years ago? You’re almost certainly more convenienced—maybe a bit less uncomfortable, maybe with a more sterile home. But have humanity’s advancements in knowledge and technology created a fundamentally more meaningful life for you—a life filled with that which makes you unconditionally joyful about being? Without a proper spiritual practice, the answer is almost certainly no. 
Scientific breakthroughs and technological innovation make for very sexy headlines, but they are by no means the only way of progressing. What's the point of nuclear power when we fear the minds controlling it? Our species can know everything there is to know about neuroscience, weather patterns, supply and demand, subatomics particles, and algorithmic efficiencies, and still be on the brink of extinction. Why? Because the mind is the filter through which the present is perceived. Without evolving it at the same rate as science and technology, the species is poised to set off an invisible, technological landmine, one that could have been easily avoided through wisdom, prudence and cooperation.
                                                                                                    

Now for a far more interesting question…
What percentage do you think you know about your own mind? If 0% is you know absolutely nothing about your mind, and 100% is you know everything there is to know about your mind, where would you place yourself on that continuum?
When it comes to the existential stuff—the ’why am I heres’ or ’what does it all means’—this is the more important question to ask, for, again, though the sciences can bring us towards a more comfortable life, it certainly won’t bring us to a more meaningful one, because meaning is generated from the inside-out, not the outside-in, and thus requires a large degree of self-awareness and understanding to cultivate.
[ ]
Despite everything humans claim to understand about their reality, very little of it has apparently translated into positive changes on a psychological and behavioral level. For example, nearly everyone understands that our current consumption patterns are unsustainable for Mother Earth, yet only a slim minority are willing to give up their shopping addictions and chemical-laden self-care rituals in solidarity. Likewise, virtually everyone intuits the health benefits of fresh, organic produce, but few are those who make it the predominant percentage of their diet. And while almost everyone agrees that spending hours per day isolated on social media is a death sentence to one’s mental health, hardly anyone opts out. It’s quite obvious that just because one “knows” what is weakening the body, mind and planet doesn’t mean one will do anything about it. Behavioral change requires much more than just a mind that is educated about and believes a certain correction would be beneficial for it. Rather, the more important ingredient is a willingness to change, which is precipitated by the awareness of fears preventing one from doing so, both of which can only arise as a result of understanding oneself (not the world) better.
Understanding oneself is not the same thing as accumulating information about oneself. To illustrate the point, imagine an intelligent, AI companion whose job it is to track and document your entire life, recording and storing every word, every scene, every feeling, and every thought you have. Now imagine that on your final day amongst the living it compiles and outputs its data into one large document. Ask yourself, would reading this summary alone—no matter how detailed or poetic or psychologically revealing—bring meaning to the trials and tribulations, campaigns and victories you experienced? Of course not. It’s not the words that kindle this fondness for one’s life; it’s the words in relation to having had a felt experience that does. Remembering or reflecting upon the facts about your life will not you any closer to knowing your life…only living your life will bring you closer to that!
And herein lies the problem—the cocky and ignorant mind conflates “knowing“ with “confidence derived from repeated exposure.” For instance, claiming I know I skipped first grade and really struggled to fit in in my new class really means “vivid, painful thoughts often arise when contemplating my early years, my age reflects having been one year younger than my peers, and believing the story is so helps me make sense of who I think I am today.” Or claiming I know 1 x 1 = 1 really means “I’ve been told over and over that 1 x 1 = 1, and believing it is so has helped me solve problems.”
This is fine, as far as things go. But if one becomes brave enough to meditate upon any one of these supposed-facts for long enough, they end up running into a funky dead-end: one technically doesn’t know anything for certain at all—ideas are just strongly believed to be true because the world has repeated them to them endlessly, and acting as if they were true proves extremely useful to one’s survival and success. Instead, all one really knows is that they think they know, which is not the same thing.
In the end, then, for the average person to say they know 50%—or even just 5%—of their mind isn’t saying very much at all. Who cares if you have a photographic memory when __?
And yet, true knowledge of one’s own mind—which is to say having an unfiltered view of what is mentally arising in the moment—does matter. Quite a lot, actually. When one can’t feel the anxiety tightening their chest during a conversation, they lash out at the person they love most and have no idea why. When one can’t distinguish genuine intuition from fear dressed up as caution, they spend decades in a career or relationship that was never really theirs. When one doesn’t understand that the mental defilements of greed and hatred are creating a perpetual cycle of dissatisfaction, and can’t see their presence in oneself, one spends an entire life grasping at temporary pleasures and running from temporary pains, never once considering there might be another way.
Such is the fundamental difference between knowing something intellectually and knowing something experientially. Someone can know everything there is to know about Buddhist doctrine, yogic lore, Christian theology, psychedelic therapy, kundalini awakenings, quantum physics, history, brain wave states, psychoneuroimmunology, and attachment styles, and still be a miserable jack-ass. 
In other words, there is only learn so much one from studying a thing top-down, as with other scientific endeavors. Does hearing about or mapping the neural correlates of anger offer the same insights as having felt it? Does reading a book on meditation automatically make one able to shut one’s mind off at-will? Does being an expert historian on the Middle Ages compare to what it was actually like to be there? It’s like trying to understand the surface of the ocean by solely viewing it from above the waters—from the outside, in. Actually, to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the ocean’s surface, one also needs to submerge themselves into the ocean and look up—to view it from the inside, out.
                                                                                                    

A final thought experiment…
How much of an average day do you spend being truly content? If 0% is absolutely no part of your day is spent satisfied and 100% is rampant satisfaction all day long — pure, meditative bliss from eyes open to eyes shut — where would you place yourself?
At this point, you are probably already wondering how this experiment is flawed, so I’ll tell you:
For one, whatever the mind’s answer, the time you think you’re spending in contentment is likely an overestimate, for, as heaps of psychological research make clear, people’s appraisal of their own overall experience almost always gets skewed when looking backwards.
This is because the unawakened mind is blind to itself, and therefore almost always overlooks the extent to which it complains about the present. So common are the thoughts “this moment would be ever-so-slightly better if only x were y, or y were x” that it fails to see them at all, oblivious to how much discontentment is floating around. Such is the classic example of one who, after being asked why they’re so upset, yells back, neck bulging: ‘I’m not upset!!!’
In a non-anecdotal example, participants in a happiness study carried pagers around with them, answering questions regarding contentment and happiness levels at different times throughout the day whenever it beeped. When researchers later asked these same participants to estimate their overall happiness during the study period, the recalled numbers were consistently inflated—sometimes dramatically so. What the real-time data showed as a 30% contentment rate, participants remembered as 60% or 70%. The conceited mind, it turns out, doesn’t just want to believe it's content—it will actively rewrite its own history to prove it.
Here’s an uncomfortable invitation: rather than answering the question by looking backward at your day, try answering it right now. In this very moment of reading, are you content? Not ”sort of fine” or ”not actively suffering”—but genuinely, fully at ease with what is here? Is there no part of the mind reaching for what comes next, no subtle itch to check the phone, no quiet hum of something that needs doing later, no faint wish that you were a little more comfortable, a little less tired, a little further along in life than you are?
If you checked honestly, you probably found a little something, something: a subtle background hum of wanting. And that’s precisely the point. Not that you should feel bad about it, but that the mind is so accustomed to this low-grade wanting that it doesn’t register it as discontentment at all. It’s like a refrigerator hum you’ve stopped hearing, or a persistent ringing in the ears that’s been there so long it feels like silence. The noise is so constant that you’ve stopped calling it noise!
This is worth sitting with for a moment, because it reframes the question entirely. The issue isn’t just that you overestimate how content you are when looking backward—it’s that you may not know what contentment actually feels like at all. And if you don’t know what contentment actually feels like, how could you possibly know how much of your day is spent there?
To illustrate: imagine someone who has only ever eaten unripe fruit. Hard peaches, sour strawberries, bitter apples pulled too soon from the branch. Asked to rate the sweetness of their diet, they might say “seven out of ten”—because relative to the sourest thing they’ve tasted, the least-unripe peach they've had seems genuinely sweet. They aren’t lying. They’re working with the only reference points they have. But hand them a single ripe mango on a warm afternoon, and their entire scale recalibrates in an instant. What they called sweetness before wasn’t sweet at all; it was just the least sour option available.
Contentment works the same way. Most people’s experience of “being content” is really just the temporary absence of acute discomfort—a moment between wanting and wanting again, maybe the gap between finishing one task and remembering the next one, or the first sip of coffee before the mind kicks into planning mode, or the few glorious seconds after a sneeze. These are pleasant, sure, but not what contemplative traditions mean by peace any more than the absence of a headache constitutes vibrant health. There is a quality of ease available to human beings so far beyond the ordinary that hearing about it can sound absurd or exaggerated—like trying to describe color to someone who has only ever seen in black and white. And yet every wisdom tradition on earth insists it is real, and every serious practitioner confirms it through direct experience.
This creates a peculiar trap. If you’ve never tasted genuine contentment, you have no way of knowing what you’re missing. And if you have no way of knowing what you’re missing, you have no reason to go looking for it. And if you have no reason to go looking for it, you remain exactly where you are—managing discomfort, chasing relief, and telling yourself that this is more or less as good as it gets. The ignorance is self-sealing.
Which is precisely why the common expression “ignorance is bliss” has it exactly backwards. Ignorance isn’t bliss—ignorance is what prevents bliss. It is the very thing standing between you and the depth of peace that every mystic, saint, and sage has tried (and failed, because it’s an impossible task) to put into words. The phrase should really read: “ignorance is tolerable, so long as you never find out what you're missing.”
But even a glimpse behind the curtain changes things irreversibly. Once someone tastes—even for a single moment—what real stillness feels like, what it’s like when the wanting machinery actually stops and the mind rests in pure, undisturbed awareness, ordinary life becomes much harder to zombie-walk through. It’s not that the world gets worse, but because the contrast becomes undeniable: ‘I had no idea how fucking annoying that refrigerator hum really was!’
In other words, the average human mind hasn't a clue just how peaceful peace can really get. And that’s a problem, because it prevents the very seeking that would lead to the Divine gift of a truly free mind. For if one as much as tastes a drop of true bliss—the kind that arises as a result of proximity to God, not the fleeting kind that follows yet another desire fulfilled—ignorance and complacency become immediately untenable. One would be willing to do anything—surrender any pride, any addiction, any resistance—just to savor its nectar again.
And this is why the early stages of any genuine spiritual path often feel like things are getting worse rather than better. They aren’t. One is simply becoming aware (perhaps for the first time) of discontentment that was always there, like turning on the lights in a room one has been stumbling through in the dark. The furniture hasn’t moved; you're just finally seeing what you've been bruising your shins on.
And so the seeker is left in an uncomfortable middle: too awake to go back to sleep, not yet awake enough to be free. The old distractions lose their potency. The usual escapes feel hollow. And yet the destination—that full, unshakeable peace the traditions describe—remains distant, demanding that which one is not yet willing to give. This in-between is, for many, the most painful part of the journey, and therefore the part that most people quit during, retreating back to the familiarity of Ignorance and doing their best to forget what they briefly saw.
But for those who don't quit—for those who sense, even dimly, that what they glimpsed was more real than everything they'd been calling reality—the only honest response is to keep walking. Not because the Path is even remotely comfortable or fun, but because once you’ve heard the hum, you can't unhear it. And pretending you can is its own kind of suffering.
Which brings us, at last, to the real question this book is asking. Not “what percentage of the Universe do you understand?” or “how well do you know your own mind?” or even “how content are you?” The real question is: Are you willing to find out what's on the other side of your ignorance, even if the finding-out means surrendering everything you currently believe about who you are and what will make you happy?
If the answer is yes!—or even a hesitant, half-terrified maybe—congratulations and welcome. You have picked up a map and are ready to begin the journey.
