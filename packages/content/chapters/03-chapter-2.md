The present has this funny way of converting into the past as soon as it’s over. What was once sharp and undeniable is instantly replaced with a fuzzy caricature of what happened, also known as a memory, which is then quickly followed by an even fuzzier caricature of what one thinks happened, also known as my memory.

When one of your memories is summoned, your mind believes it—it confuses that memory for reality. For example, you can remember, I assume, your most-recent meal. That clarity propels your confidence in the memory’s reliability, despite you actually having forgotten, edited or flat-out misremembered many details about the experience. Details like exactly how many times you swallowed during the meal, what the third-to-last bite tasted like, and if you made any subtle groaning sounds while munching.

Imperfect memory wouldn’t be a problem were it not for the fact that these partially-fabricated recordings completely distort how you’re seeing reality now.

The mind pushes back: “Speak for yourself—I’m seeing reality just fine, thanks.”

Are you, though?

Try this. Close your LEFT eye and stare at the cross with your RIGHT eye. Now slowly move the page closer to your face, keeping your gaze fixed on the cross.

+ •

At a certain distance, the dot on the right will completely disappear. This is your literal blind spot, the region of your retina where the optic nerve exits and there are zero photoreceptors. Every human eye has a hole like this in their visual field, and yet when you gaze upon this page, you perceive no hole. Why? Because your brain is filling in that empty region with a visual approximation, assembled from memory plus general assumptions about how environments have tended to look.

What the eye does with its blind spot, the mind does with literally everything else. The first coffee was a breathtaking event; the thousandth barely lets out a whisper. Same with the drive to work, the face across the breakfast table, the body you heave around. Until the spiritual awakening process begins, the mind incrementally fills in its tunnel vision with all kinds of nostalgia and anticipation and abstraction. As a result, Life gets fogged over. Now the mind is increasingly forced to live inside the two places where nothing occurs: back there, and up ahead.

Which is precisely how and why your appetite for novelty and sensations grows. If the coffee no longer registers, one needs the single-origin, hand-poured, prohibitively expensive coffee. If the beach no longer registers, one needs the beach in a country with a visa requirement. If the partner no longer registers—well. The mind reads the object as the problem, rather than with the machinery pointed at it, and so goes shopping. Bigger, cooler, juicier, newer, hotter, more.

Why does this matter? Because it involves your suffering, of course.

Recall from Chapter 1 that desire is, at root, craving or aversion towards impermanent sensations. What that definition *doesn’t* address is why some sensations get craved while others get rejected. The answer is more visible now: memory. You do not psychologically chase or flee sensations because of how they actually feel; you chase or flee them because of what your memory has decided they portend.

In other words, suffering *isn’t* really caused by reality at all, but the reconstruction of reality through the looking glass of your past. The high you’re chasing right now will eventually arrive (as all desires do) and pass (it’s also anicca). And yet if you aren’t being careful—if wisdom is not present—that experience will immediately become a data point that your present life is now compared to and dulled by.

Before you start resenting this whole arrangement, keep in mind that our memory system is both biologically necessary and, I would argue, divinely so. Having access to literally all of recorded history—the locations of each atom as they buzz around, the fluctuating number of hairs on one’s head, the content of every thought from every human across all time—is impractical for an organism who, let’s be honest, is just trying to make it to Friday. Better, biologically speaking, only to reference that which is relevant to worldly survival today than to reference that which is relevant to all worlds tomorrow.[[Besides, if nothing were filtered or edited, there would be no such thing as a finite experience—no first kiss, no last conversation with your father, no Tuesday game night with the boys. Everything would simply be everything, all at once, forever. This sounds magnificent (and it is) right up until you notice it describes a state in which nothing can be met, because there is no one standing apart from it to do the meeting.]]

As such, the nervous system and brain evolved very clever ways of summarizing the past in order to form judgments in the present. We'll call these summaries impressions. And because they are so critical for understanding and overcoming suffering, we’ll spend a little time getting to know them.

## What It Is

Impressions are shortcuts your nervous system uses to recognize, make sense of, and navigate reality without having to reprocess everything from scratch. They are why you can walk into an unfamiliar bathroom in the dark and find the light switch roughly where it ought to be. Though you may have never seen this wall before, you have an impression of other walls to help guide your actions.

What’s crazy is that everything named and plugged into your internal web of associations will have an impression associated with it—no matter how irrelevant or absurd on the surface. You have, for instance, an impression about whether your kindergarten teacher was a cat person or dog person, if you can fit through that narrow hole in the wall, how much more or less attractive the right side of your face is compared to your left, what people in Argentina are like, how sweet a berry can get, if the sex life of your parents is any good, whether the author (me) is worth listening to, which AI company is the best, how long you’d survive in a zombie apocalypse, what will happen at your funeral when you die…

The list of one’s impressions is effectively endless.

Actually, it can help to think of impressions like cute little .zip files on a computer. Double-click to open, and a vast library of information unpacks itself instantly, ready to be used whenever, be it in crisis or contemplation. Actually, you can think of impressions as very complex (yet paradoxically very crude) formulas the mind uses to make informed decisions about a given situation. ‘This plus this seemed to equal that yesterday, so this plus this will probably equal this today.’

For example, it would take far too long for your brain to parse through every single data point from every single interaction with your mother whenever you thought about her, so instead it creates a compressed representation (an impression) called “Mom.” Reference that thought, even accidentally, and instantly—before you've even given permission—a constellation of associations will unpack: her tone of voice and how she laughs, what her hugs feel like, how she goes about expressing concern for your safety (*Mom—I’m FINE!*), on and on. All of this information presents itself in a fraction of a second, not as thoughts you can hear, but as a vision and felt sense of ‘Momness’.

Give it a go; notice how much information is in that particular folder.

These impressions folders don't just contain information. They contain judgment, too. Your ‘Mom’ impression *isn’t* neutral; it comes pre-loaded with a certain feeling-tone, or vedanā, as talked about in Chapter 1. Is the vibe around Mom safe or unsafe? Comforting or draining? A source of approval or disappointment? These evaluations are essentially baked into the impression itself, activating the moment even the tiniest relevant trigger arises.[[Relevant trigger is the key word here, for without these triggers one’s impressions and reactions towards life remain dormant.]]

Please, please, please let this land for a moment, because its implications are seismic. Every time you’ve raged at a partner or stranger, it wasn't really about what they did—it was about your impression of what their actions meant. Every time you’ve measured yourself against someone else and found yourself lacking, it wasn’t an honest assessment of your worth—it was an impression of what enough looks like. Every time you've chased something hoping it would finally make you content, you weren't chasing the thing itself—you were chasing the impression of what the thing would feel like once you acquired it.

Do you get it now?! It means that nothing has ever actually happened to you. Things have happened, certainly. But what reached you was only ever interpretations, already sorted, already stamped good or bad. What reached you has only ever been opinions from a mind which runs on decades-old material.[[Like lovers’ initials carved into tree bark, impressions scratch grooves into our mind, forming clues about our past. If someone is averse to sinking their bare feet into the soil, it suggests something in their history impressed a belief leading to that behavior. Maybe it’s as innocent as the sensations registering as ‘threat’, because they feel unfamiliar. Maybe it’s because their parents yelled at them when they tracked mud inside as a kid. Maybe they got stung by a wasp on the bottom of a foot in a past life. The precise reason is far less important than one’s awareness of the impression being present. Crucially, the letters one sees on the carved tree aren’t actually there—they’re defined by what’s been removed. You’re not seeing the lovers’ initials; you’re seeing the absence of the initials, the negative space of where the wood used to be. Imagine tasting something truly novel—say a fruit you've never encountered before. For a split second, there's just pure sensation: sweet, tart, texture, temperature. But almost immediately, the mind starts carving away at that rawness: ‘It's like a mango but figgier,’ ‘I like this, but I think it’d be better cold’ ‘I wonder if it’ll help me lose weight.’ Each comparison, each judgment removes a piece of the direct experience, leaving only the carved-out impression where direct, un-impressed experience used to be—a useful reference, but no longer the thing itself.]]

Surprisingly, it's utterly irrelevant whether your impressions are true or not. Your impression of "money," for instance, can cause a volcanic eruption of associated ideas and beliefs to surface, some stressful, some titillating, some a real drag, depending entirely on how the subconscious has interpreted the past. *Money is freedom. Money is what tore this family apart*. *Money is what they have and we don't. Money is filthy, and wanting it says something ugly about you*. *Money is the scoreboard. Money is the thing I will finally relax when I have enough of*.

Which of these views are correct? Doesn’t much matter, biologically speaking. A belief that produces behaviors which even sorta-kinda keep the organism alive and procreating has already justified itself. Truth can be expensive and slow to arrive at, and good enough is free and immediate, so it’s allowed to stick around as long as it wants.[[It's much easier in the short-term to have a belief system that sorta-kinda works than to invest energy into becoming financially literate or healing the deep-seated wounds tainting one's view of money.]]

If impressions say nothing about whether something is true or not, how much less are they in a position to say whether something is ultimately good, bad, or neutral?

I’m reminded of the parable of a wise, Chinese farmer whose horse ran away. That evening, all of his neighbors came around to commiserate: “We’re so sorry to hear your horse ran away. How unfortunate.” The farmer said, “Maybe.” The next day the horse came back bringing seven wild horses with it, and in the evening everybody came back and said, “Oh, *isn’t* that great! You now have eight horses!” The farmer again said, “Maybe.” The following day his son tried to tame one of the horses and, while riding it, was thrown and broke his leg. The neighbors then said, “Oh dear, that’s too bad,” and the farmer responded, “Maybe.” The next day the conscription officers came around to enlist people into the army, and they rejected his son because of the injury. Again all the neighbors came around and said, “Isn’t that lucky?!” Again, he said, “Maybe.”

You get the idea. It’s impossible to see the ultimate effect of a relative cause. Therefore, those who are wise never idolize their impressions—what they believe something means—but rather humbly internalize the word “Maybe.”

And yet, the arrogant, unpurified mind reallllly likes to treat its impressions as holy, delusionally believing that because of a few past encounters, it definitely—not maybe—knows what’s best for itself and others. It *doesn’t* take a Buddha to see how such alleged omniscience and moral crusading come at the cost of one’s sanity. “This is good, because I believe it will bring good outcomes; this is bad, because I believe it will bring bad outcomes”—now one has God-level justification to try and force Reality into artificially-labeled boxes and get enraged whenever It pops back out.

Have fun with that.

## Digging In

Are you starting to get an impression about impressions? How they’re absolutely everywhere? How they act as the scaffolding for your entire worldview? If so, maybe you can empathize with why I’m spending so much time trying to help you understand them. Simply put: *If you understand how impressions work, you will understand your mind. And if you understand* *your mind, you will understand why you suffer. And if you understand why you suffer, you will* *understand how to break free*.

Let’s dive back in.

Impressions explain how your idea of something is not the same as my idea of something, even if we both agree on a definition. For instance, what you and I actually mean by ‘dog’ *isn’t* merely “a  domesticated carnivorous mammal with an acute sense of smell, non-retractable claws, and a barking,  howling, or whining  voice.” Rather dog is this indescribable sense of ‘dogness’ ‘dogging’ around, which is entirely based on your and my unique, prior run-ins with other ‘dognesses’ confirming their apparently ‘doggy-like’ or ‘non-doggy-like’ behaviors. I don’t mean to be cheeky there. I wish to accurately describe what happens at the level of impressions. At this depth, the meaning of a thing is not a static block of truth, but an ineffable isness as it relates to the immediate thought of it.[[As a consequence, the feelings and interpretations triggered by the words in this book are entirely based on your unique lived experience and renderings of that unique lived experience, and not necessarily because they’re what I was intending. As but one example, because of most people’s fraught religious upbringings (or lack thereof), whenever the word God is used in this text, it is virtually impossible not to impregnate one’s impression of ‘Godness Godding’ into it, even if that is precisely the opposite of what was meant.]]

Play around with this a bit. Contemplate an idea, any idea, and see that overshadowing it is this ghost-like apparition: a nearly unshakable sense that ‘No, I really do know the thing I’m thinking about’. Camera lens, opioids, education, your ex-partner, the ant crawling up a cedar tree. Is it what you think it is? Where does your certainty come from?

Much like streams flow into rivers, and rivers sculpt into valleys, once an impression is made, it acts like a gravity basin for others to feed into. Once the impression *the government is* lying is in place, every press briefing, retraction, and quiet correction three weeks later drains straight into it. Decide early that *you're bad at math* and every wrong answer confirms it, every right answer feels like luck, and every classmate's ease becomes proof.

This kind of meaning-making occurs day in and day out until, by the time you’re a few decades into life, the topography of your mind is more or less fully formed: *this equals this and* *that equals that*. Sure, new impressions can be made in regards to unfamiliar topics—i.e. when AI first came online no one had a clue what to make of it, because nothing in the past looked quite like it. But it would take quite the flood (or many, tiny rain showers) to reshape the terrain of deeper impressions like politics, religion, and metaphysics.[[Even so, for most people such a dramatic palate cleanser would destabilize their mind too much, the identity upended and unable to float out in open waters for long. Unless one embodies the wisdom of ‘Maybe’, such an awakening may cause the mind to latch onto other belief systems, and cling to it even tighter than the one it held before.]]

All of this, by the way, is what makes technologies like social media and AI all the more double-edged. Even before algorithms and LLMs, the mind sought to cherry-pick evidence to bolster its existing impressions. But now the machine unconsciously identifies which rivers are already running through your mind and then artificially floods those exact channels with confirming content (or, oppositely, it can create the perfect storm to flip one’s beliefs, so that the alternative is now identified with). Every click, every message, every lingering glance trains the algorithm to understand your deepest grooves and pump more water into precisely those valleys.

The result? Impressions on steroids! Strong conclusions that might have taken years to arrive at through detailed research or lived experience now happen in months, sometimes days. “I should quit my job and move to Bali,” “I’m not working hard enough,” “we’re absolutely cooked,” “they’re evil”—whatever impression already exists gets widened, deepened, or flipped at artificial speed, reinforced thousands of times a day through algorithmically curated feeds designed not for Truth but for engagement. And because it arrives tailored to your existing patterns, it can feel like intuition or the world revealing itself, rather than a machine exploiting your conditioning for profit.

And yet, sometimes it is intuition or the world revealing itself! After all, God works just as easily through suggested feeds as the birds and breeze. Therefore, determining what is algorithmically-induced psychosis versus what is actually a sign from the heavens is basically anybody’s guess. A decent-enough rule of thumb I use is that in the presence of a new piece of content, there should be zero smugness in having found ’the way’, or in thinking one is special for having made contact with ‘Truth’. Rather, in its place is a weighty sense of acceptance—“this is it”—followed by a neutral expansion to the energy field and diminishment of one’s ego.[[Unfortunately, even this method is not a foolproof means of discernment, so above all else I recommend freeing the mind of its self-assuredness by continuing to surrender judgments over to God.]] None of this—and this must be said straight to your face, so as to not be misunderstood— means that Truth or Goodness or Facts do not exist, nor that said Truth and Goodness and Facts are not being fastidiously kept track of by God. They do and they are. It’s just that a Creator-sized chasm exists between what is Good and what feels good, between what is True and what the confirmation loop has certified as true, and between what is Fact and what the senses have deemed a fact. The mind, caught in its endless feedback loops, is simply never in a position to bridge that chasm on its own for it can only ever see through its dense fog of impressions. As a general rule, then—and this is not cynicism but practical spiritual counsel from someone who still has many impressions himself—until one has done significant purification work (which will be outlined in Part 2 of this book), it is far safer to assume that one's bodily signals are conditioned rather than intuition, wisdom or signs from God. Not because wisdom isn't there, but because the static is so loud that mistaking interference for signal is all-but guaranteed. The entire trajectory of *the conditioned mind* is organized around selfishness— what benefits me, what threatens me, what makes me feel good, what makes me feel safe. Even one's seemingly noble impulses often have self-interest woven through them at a level too subtle to detect without serious introspection. This is precisely why every authentic spiritual tradition places devotional service—the ongoing, unglamorous commitment to acting in service of something higher than oneself—at the center of the Path. Without that constant, corrective orientation towards the selfless, one has no external reference point by which to measure whether the body's signals are leading towards God or merely towards sexier versions of the ego’s same ol’ bullshit.[[Such mental kryptonite is still a relatively untouched issue within New Age communities, though it is a huge and ever-growing problem. Not the performative humility of “I don't know,” uttered publicly, while privately believing one does know. But the gut-level, nauseating surrender of the small self admitting that one's deepest convictions might very well be nothing more than a well-worn groove in the nervous system, confirmed ten thousand times by sensations that prove absolutely nothing. “Maybe,” as the Chinese farmer might say. Always, always maybe.]]

## Triggered!

Somewhere in the multiverse there is an apartment with a pile of dishes in the sink.

In one timeline, someone named Sunshine skips in, smiles, puts a record on before they run the water, and works their way down the stack in a soapy trance, singing the wrong lyrics with confidence and pizazz. Pleasant sensations arise. An impression forms and files itself: oh, doing *dishes is actually pretty fun!*

In another timeline—the exact same apartment, the exact same stack, the exact same water pressure—it's a different tenant at the sink: Schopenhauer. Shoulders racked up around their ears, something better clearly waiting for them on the other side of this chore, resenting the fact that this apartment *doesn’t* have a dishwasher. Unpleasant sensations arise. An impression forms and files itself: *doing dishes sucks*.

Who's right? Are dishes objectively fun or objectively a nuisance? Clearly neither perspective is realer than the other (though the ego does like to think so.) More accurate is that each mind created an impression based on how relatively pleasant or unpleasant the sensations happened to feel during each pass at the sink.

Impressions, in other words, are *self-reinforcing processes fueled by physical sensations*— sensations which the body produces in perfect obedience to a mind working from partial information.

Now, before one throws the baby out with the bathwater—‘so my body cannot be trusted?’—know that the body is not the problem. The body is, as we will continue to return to, extraordinary. Being that all is connected to all, there is a deep and ancient wisdom running through your nervous system and etheric body that knows things the conscious mind cannot even begin to fathom. The problem is that this wisdom has been buried under decades of accumulated mental conditioning.

This word is worth pausing on, because we'll be using it quite a bit going forward. The classic demonstration comes from Ivan Pavlov, the Russian physiologist who, in the 1890s, noticed that dogs naturally salivate at the sight and smell of food. No surprise there—that's hardware. But Pavlov then began ringing a bell every time he fed them, and after enough repetitions something strange happened: the dogs began salivating at the sound of the bell alone, even when no food was present. The bell, which meant absolutely nothing on its own, had been welded to a bodily response through sheer repetition.

That is conditioning in its most basic form: the process by which a neutral stimulus gets wired to a specific bodily reaction through repeated pairing. And what's true for dogs and bells is true, in far subtler and more pervasive ways, for the human mind and its impressions. The modern human is essentially a walking collection of Pavlovian grooves—salivating at bells nobody else can hear, flinching at bells nobody else is ringing, all of it feeling perfectly organic and self-evident because the wiring was laid down long ago and is now running beneath the threshold of conscious awareness.

This is why a culturally conditioned response can feel indistinguishable from a biological one. The body that recoils from incest and the body that recoils from an unfamiliar ethnic food are using the same hardware, producing the same visceral ‘this is wrong.’ The body that melts in genuine divine communion and the body that melts when the algorithm feeds it exactly what it wants to hear both register as ‘this is true.’ The primal signal and the conditioned reaction wear the same clothes, speak in the same voice, and carry the same “felt” sense of authority. Which is which? The unpurified, ignorant mind genuinely has no way of knowing.

Consider money: the first time one earns a significant sum, a rush of pleasant sensations arises—relief, pride, safety, possibility. Impressions form: ‘money is good,’ ‘money is safety,’ ‘money is critical to my happiness.’ Now, each subsequent encounter with earning, saving, or even thinking about wealth arrives pre-loaded with that warm glow (and, consequently, a cold pit in its absence). The body confirms it, the impression deepens, and before long, one has an entire philosophy about the absolute necessity of financial success for one’s well-being.

But now say that same person takes up a spiritual practice and misunderstands the lessons of non-attachment and the dangers of greed. Now the very same pleasant rush that money triggers gets reinterpreted. That warm glow? No longer evidence that money is good, but that one is still enslaved to it. The sensation hasn't changed one bit—the body still purrs when the bank account grows—but the impression surrounding it has flipped entirely. Now every pleasant pulse in the presence of wealth confirms ‘I’m too attached,’ ‘I’m not spiritual enough,’ or ‘greed is my weakness.’ Same body, same sensation, opposite confirmed belief. In either case —the materialist celebrating their earnings and the seeker flagellating themselves over them— the person is utterly convinced they're seeing reality clearly.

The Indians gave a name to this habitual reactivity: *saṃskāras* (or *saṅkhāra* in Pali), and they are absolutely critical to overcoming suffering. Saṃskāras are the grooves in your mind which inspire it to reach toward positive vedanā or recoil from negative vedanā—craving and aversion.

The full scope of one's accumulated *saṃskāras*—every habit of clinging and rejecting—is known as *the conditioned mind*. This is not a region of the mind one can point to on a brain scan, but a functional layer: the sum total of every Pavlovian wiring one has accumulated, running mostly beneath the threshold of conscious awareness, firing reflexively the moment the right sensation arrives.

To be abundantly, obnoxiously clear: *saṃskāras* are not the aversive or attractive mental tendencies towards specific experiences, but rather towards the sensations a specific experience triggers. This is the crucial distinction, because it reveals where the real leverage in the whole system lies. Impressions are beliefs about what an experience is or means. Saṃskāras are the *habits of reaction towards the sensations* those impressions generate. The two interlock: one’s beliefs about the world color the perspective, the perspective governs whether a positive or negative feeling arises in response, and the feeling opens the door to react with craving or aversion.

For example, if one has a particularly negative impression of a certain band—“their lyrics are uninspired,” “they play too fast,” “their harmonies are ugly”—then the moment one of their songs comes on, the impression fires and the body produces a small cascade of unpleasant sensations: a tightening in the jaw, a restlessness in the chest, a subtle wince behind the eyes. The saṃskāra of aversion is the reflex that jumps to the skip button to make those sensations stop. Not to make the song stop. Not even to make the judgment about the song stop. You skip the song to try and end the bodily feelings your judgment produced—that is the distinction that matters.

Or consider the inverse: one has a deeply-grooved impression that morning coffee is essential—‘I *can’t* function without it,’ ‘this is my ritual,’ ‘this is how I wake up.’ The moment one smells the grounds or hears the machine start, the impression fires and the body produces a small cascade of pleasant sensations: warmth in the chest, a quickening of alertness, a sense of relief that something good is about to happen. The saṃskāra of craving is the reflex that reaches for the mug to prolong and deepen those sensations.

This is why, in the purification work that Part 2 of this book will detail, the primary target is not the impressions themselves, but the *saṃskāras* that give them their bite. The ego’s instinct is to try to rewire the belief directly through techniques like hypnosis or affirmations—to brute-force convince oneself of the opposite until the old belief has been crowded out. This approach, though well-intentioned, is structurally incapable of delivering the freedom it promises, as it fails on two separate levels.

The first failure point is that even if the impression is successfully rewired at that level, the pipeline it rides in on would not be. An impression is the first link in a chain that runs impression → sensation → reaction → misery. Changing the impression only changes the sensation it generates, not the reactive groove in their wake. The person who successfully hypnotizes themselves into believing ‘they are worthy of abundance’ still has, wired into their body, the reflexive pull toward pleasant sensations and the reflexive flinch away from unpleasant ones. They have not become free; they have simply rearranged which sensations get which reactions.

The second problem is deeper and, frankly, inescapable through rewiring. Even if one could somehow flip every painful impression into its opposite—‘money comes in effortlessly,’ ‘my body is healthy and vibrant,’ ‘failure is safe’—the mind would still be operating within the same dualistic paradigm. Every preference implies a mirror rejection. The very act of installing a newer, more positive impression guarantees the simultaneous installation of its shadow. Again, one has not escaped the loop; one has merely rotated it ninety degrees and called the new orientation freedom.

What can be released—and this is the mercy at the heart of the teaching—is the saṃskāra, the reaction to sensation, the bridge that converts sensation into misery. When the saṃskāra goes, the entire chain downstream collapses, and the impression upstream loses its power to delude you. One can, of course, still choose to rewire impressions with the explicit purpose of generating certain sensations. But such a choice no longer becomes a prerequisite for peace. Given all this, one might assume that a saṃskāra, being a misery-producing reflexive reaction, is only a problem the moment it actually fires—that everything goes back to neutral until the next trigger. But that is not how it works, for a saṃskāra that *isn’t* currently firing *isn’t* absent, but dormant.

Take one’s *saṃskāras* around sex, either in the form of craving or rejecting it. The groove keeps its head buried for most of an average day because the subject simply isn't being broached—until someone attractive walks by, or until the mind is free to fantasize late at night, at which point it activates as if it had been there the whole time (which, of course, it had been.) Just because a saṃskāra is not appearing on the dashboard of one's vehicle does not mean it isn't being lugged around in the boot. The existence of any saṃskāra—especially the deeply embedded ones—is taking a small, continuous toll on one’s energy body. Like a battery-intensive program running in the background of one's computer. *At this very moment* some non-finite amount of your total life-force is being spent scanning the periphery for any worldly opportunity to reimpress your beliefs.

Actually, your *saṃskāras* do not merely wait for the world to trigger them. They actively influence the world into creating the conditions for them to arrive. Why? Because your *saṃskāras* alter which part of reality you perceive, thereby impacting the opportunities which present themselves and altering the trajectory of your life. Two people who walk through the exact same door and encounter two entirely different rooms because their *saṃskāras* are steering their attention toward what confirms them and away from what doesn't.

This is why spiritual purification is non-optional for anyone seriously interested in freedom from suffering. So long as one’s conditioning is running in the background—scanning, curating, drawing in its matches—the life one appears to be living is not actually the life one claims to want. It is the life one's *saṃskāras* have arranged to keep themselves fed.

The last feature of *saṃskāras* worth noting is their adaptability—they transition between craving and aversion quite seamlessly. Such is the case of one who drank too much of a specific alcohol (craving), got sick, and now can't even stand the sight of it (aversion). Or one who once detested workouts (aversion), but after some gruelish attempts became addicted to the pride and adrenaline rush it offers (craving). Saṃskāras of craving and aversion naturally get chain-linked to one another, too, as is evident while on a physical or mental diet: one caves to their saṃskāra of lust towards that which has been made “off-limits,” and then gets immediately plunged into their saṃskāra of shame towards their inability to hold the line.

Being present with one’s life as it gets tossed from obsession to revulsion to attraction to disgust to indifference—round and round and round—is the only guaranteed way of revealing just how misery-inducing one’s treasury of *saṃskāras* really are. How much suffering in the face of failure must one feel until one sees that, actually, the clinging to one’s success is fueling the whole enterprise? Sadly, it can take an absurdly long time before one realizes the nature of their own wretched mind, let alone before becoming willing to take the steps necessary to free themselves from this merry-go-round of hell (also known as *saṃsāra* in Pali). Consider yourself lucky if you see clearly the problem of attaching and reacting to duality, for such is a prerequisite to begin climbing the spiral ladder back to Unity.[[Perhaps it should go without saying that some saṃskāras are better and more sustainable for the body, mind and soul than others. Yes, all aspects of the conditioned mind are traps that eventually need to be relinquished (even, paradoxically, the holier ones such as prayer and fasting from sense pleasures.) No, all such relinquishments needn’t happen at once in order to make significant progress towards inner peace. Exchanging an addiction to porn or shopping for a reliance on meditation and the suttas is not going to lose you points with God. In the darkest of nights, it is also extremely valuable to remember that, by spiritual law, nothing is intransmutable. Though some habitual patterns are admittedly more calcified and harder to dissolve than others (especially when brain injuries, diseases, and physical toxins are involved), there is no legitimate excuse for not doing everything within one's power to become less greedy, spiteful, and compulsive. No need to overthink it—just start again.]]

## Goodbye

Now is as good a time as any to retire the word “suffering” from our vocabulary for the remainder of the book, for suffering has been impressed upon us in just one direction—toward misery, pain, and terror. This captures the agony of the lows well enough, but completely disregards the problem of the highs—their impermanence, their relativity, their insufficiency. We need a single word that captures both the agony when preferences aren't met and the unsatisfactoriness even when they are.

Alas, our vocabulary does not have such an all-encompassing word, which is why we are better off borrowing yet another term from our Indian colleagues—dukkha. Dukkha combines both the suffering of the lows with the insufficiency of the highs into a single understanding— that all conditioned experience has built-in unsatisfactoriness to it. Or, as Thanissaro Bhikkhu so aptly put it: “dukkha is the inevitability of unwanted experience.”

Why is unwanted experience inevitable? Once again, we have duality to thank.

Consider a new car. One buys it precisely because it runs, and one intends for it to keep running—that's the wanted experience, purchased outright and driven off the lot.

But what does “run” actually mean? It’s one of two states available to the car, the other one being “doesn't run.” The working and failing are not two separate possibilities, but the same fact reported at two different times, the way a coin cannot be melted down to just the heads. A car that runs now is simply a car that has not broken down yet.

This is duality operating in the world, and it holds for everything conditioned. Whatever is currently in the state one wants is in that state temporarily, by definition, because the state was only ever half of a pair. Chapter one called this impermanence. Here it is again with the mask off: not a sad quirk of an otherwise perfect world, but simply what having two ends means. Now the second half, and the reason dukkha cannot be engineered around.

Every impression is tethered to its opposite, like negative space creating the illusion of a foreground image. Good generates bad, right generates wrong, God generates not God, and deciding that a reliable car means freedom and competence will, in the same stroke, define the breakdown as humiliation and failure.

Impressions, in other words, come pre-loaded with their own catastrophe.

So put the two together: Reality guarantees the flip, because a conditioned thing is a thing with two ends. And the mind has already decided, in advance, that that flip is intolerable, and will therefore react negatively to it.

That’s the whole mechanism! That is dukkha!

And it isn't just one car. Every impression is another two-state proposition with a verdict nailed to the second one. And you are carrying millions of them: your body, your career, your marriage, your reputation, your spirituality. All of them warrantied. All of them revealing and building one’s *saṃskāras* in real time.[[This is, in part, what the Indian traditions meant with the word maya, a Sanskrit term that has been enthusiastically mangled in Western spiritual circles for about fifty years now. The usual translation—“the world is an illusion”— insinuates that the trees and sidewalks and oncoming buses are some kind of cosmic hologram to be seen through. It’s a bold theory that can one test for themselves: go ahead…jump off that illusory roof, and find out just how illusory that pavement feels smacking against your illusory bones! No, it’s not that the world itself isn’t real; it’s that one's perception of the world isn’t.]]

Such a definition clarifies why the Buddha's first noble truth is “life is dukkha,” and not “life is suffering,” as is commonly translated. Tell someone rolling their face off on MDMA that “life is suffering,” and they'll laugh joyously right to your face—‘I’m feeling amazing right now, thank you very much.’ But tell that same someone that woven into the very fabric of life are experiences they'd rather not have—the song that ends, the comedown tomorrow, the tolerance that builds, the high that can never quite be recreated—and they might actually get it. In fact, they might get it at such a profound level that the realization morphs into a moment of gratitude for precariousness itself—for being here now, even as the surface slips away—before their consciousness and heart further expands into an all-encompassing compassion for all those caught in the same impermanent predicament.

This distinction also matters for the Buddha’s second and third noble truths: “the cause and end of dukkha,” not “the cause and end of suffering.” Lows, pains, and deaths are inevitable in this world. Craving that experience look a certain way is not. That one hopes to “transcend suffering” along the spiritual path can make it seem as though liberation were all about feeling good, a position we already did away with in chapter one. While such metaphysical planes surely exist in the wide-open Serengeti of the Cosmos, I am confident this is not what the Buddha meant. Instead, the opportunity of a human incarnation, and the promised end of dukkha, is in learning to see and experience the vicissitudes of life—pleasant or not—as momentary, effulgent ripples of light in an otherwise vast, empty landscape of Mind.

It’s also why dukkha is not a visitor you can simply lock the door against. The mind is literally built on what it thinks things are, and so keeps producing dukkha forever and ever (or at least as long as its contents go unexamined and its reactive grooves go unaddressed.) Life will keep doing what life does—arriving in forms the mind didn't order, leaving in ways the mind didn't authorize, and refusing to stay inside the boxes the impressions have built for it.

The good news—which Part 2 will spend considerable time detailing—is that duality is not the final frontier. Dukkha can end! The *saṃskāras* which give dukkha their bite can get purified and erased, line by line, groove by groove until the one who gave them their power dissolves.
