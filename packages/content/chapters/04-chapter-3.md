Life sure is beautiful and tragic, ain't it? This never-ending series of back and forths, sunrises and sunsets, setups and setbacks, fallings in and out of love. So bittersweet; so sweetbitter. And how amazing is it that you—*you of all people!*—get to be here to watch it all unfold? To be the one who experiences these joys, weathers these sorrows, learns these lessons, grows from the losses, and finds meaning in the mess? What an unlikely state of affairs! What an utter privilege! What a course in miracles!

This, more or less, is where the modern seeker lands after a few rounds of therapy, a psychedelic ceremony or two, and enough long walks to have “made peace” with the human condition. “Oh my God, life isn't about pursuing pleasure or avoiding pain,” one finally proclaims to the heavens, “it's about perspective. It's about how I choose to relate to what happens! The ups and downs aren't the enemy—they're my curriculum!”

Once this revelation has been had—once “I” have graduated from the school of hard knocks into the school of conscious living—one may watch with amusement in how quickly the whole experience gets repackaged into a new identity: a story, a before and after, a testimony of how “I once was lost, but now I’m found,” and “was blind but now I see.”

Don't get it twisted—at this point real healing progress has been made. The body, once treated as a machine to be optimized or punished, is being listened to and honored. Conversations that would have ended in slammed doors now end in even more intimacy. One’s relationship to pain has matured from “make it stop” to “what is this telling me?”

This is no small undertaking.

And yet, the underlying structure may still stand firm: the sense that I am in the center of it all, separated from the periphery by time and space. The only difference is that now it's a spiritual me, a learned me, an evolved me, a me who has done The Work.

Indeed, even humility can become a credential at this stage, if one is not treading lightly. “I don't have it all figured out,” says the one who believes they've figured out more than most. “I’m just a student.” “I’m still learning.” “I’m a work in progress.” Each statement, however sincere, still orbits the same gravitational center—I, I, I—with the quiet, unspoken conviction that this particular “I” has stumbled upon something rather important, and isn't it wonderful that this particular “I” is self-aware enough to remain humble about itself?

How to say this lightly…? The belief one has so painstakingly arrived at from all that introspection—that life is ultimately about my perspective, my growth, my unfolding—is genuinely comforting. And almost entirely wrong.

Not because perspective doesn't matter (it’s all that matters). And not because soul growth isn't real (it very much is). And not because there is no unfoldment (what else could there be?). Rather, it’s because the entire framework—every last rung of it—depends on a deeply-embedded, almost-entirely unexamined assumption: that there is a someone at the center of all this unfolding, growing, and perspective-having. A “me” who is on the journey. An “I” who chooses how to relate to the ups and downs. A “mine” to whom life belongs.

But what if that someone—the very me considered most certain, most irreducibly real—were itself just another impression? Another .zip file, no different in kind from “Mom” or “money“ or “failure,” only so much older and so much deeper that it has come to feel like bedrock rather than software? What if the “I” watching the movie of incarnation was not an audience member, but part of the film?

## I-nstallation

A fetus does not believe in itself. We do.

We place all sorts of projections upon it: who we hope they’ll be, who we hope they won't be, endowing them with names before conception, with personalities inferred from the timing of their kicks, with futures sketched in nursery colors and college funds. We assign zodiac signs based on due dates that haven’t arrived. We imagine the wedding before the first tooth.

The fetus, however, does none of these things.

What it undoubtedly does do is feel—temperature, pressure, the muffled vibrations beyond the belly’s walls. It senses, without narrative, whether its environment is safe or hostile, whether its mother is relaxed or afraid, whether the world outside the cocoon will be loud or quiet, abundant or scarce. All of this information pours into its shape-shifting brain and spinal cord, influencing it immediately—in-utero, long before the first breath. There is awareness here, clearly and undeniably, but nowhere in that awareness are there thoughts such as “my heartbeat” or “I am a fetus who will one day grow old and have children of my own”. This is not because such thoughts have been transcended, but because “I am” is merely a phrase, and the human tadpole hasn't learned English yet.

And then, miraculously, a body is born. Thrust from a safe, amniotic float tank into blinding light, from sea to air, from a world with no edges into one that is full of sharp ones. The lungs, in dramatic fashion, fill for the first time, and the skin, which has never known dryness, meets it head on. Now sound—no longer cushioned by fluid and flesh—arrives at full volume, the mind hearing the body racked with sobs, unsure of where the noise is even coming from. Everything is over-indexed! Everything is sensational!! Everything is happening here, now, all at once!!! And yet none of it has a name.

Fear not, for into this fire and flood steps the world armed with many: “It’s a boy.” “It's a girl.” “She’s beautiful.” “He's got grandpa's chin.” Before the cord drops, from the very first moment, the naming has already begun, the newborn being described from the outside, its features catalogued, its resemblances claimed, its separateness established by those who, for the most part, never stopped to question their own.

As a baby, one is then showered with coos and smiles from family and strangers alike, each unintentionally teaching it of its separateness by relating to it from what they believe is their own. A face disappears, and then reappears, beaming: “Aw, there you are! There's my little guy! There’s my little angel.” The words mean nothing to the infant, of course, but the energy is unmistakable—*you are a thing; I am a different thing*.

As a toddler, a name is learned and repeated until it becomes my name: Ethan! Said enough times, in enough tones of voice, by enough faces—delighted, scolding, sing-song, urgent—and the sound becomes indistinguishable from the being which it refers to. Surely, Ethan is more than just syllables. Surely, Ethan is what I am. Otherwise, what’s everyone pointing to?

Shortly thereafter, whatever falls within Ethan's orbit, Ethan claims. “Mine” enters the vocabulary and gets planted on every available surface—my name, my toy, my mommy, my that stranger's dog. Mineeeee!!!

Even here the self-installation is not at 100%, for young children still often speak about themselves in the third person, like a character in someone else's story. “Ethan wants milk.” “Ethan's turn.” The name has been learned, the territory claimed, but the one who supposedly owns it all hasn't yet fully moved in. That happens shortly after, when—somewhere around three or four—the child makes the developmental leap from “Ethan wants” to “I want,” the first-person narrator having climbed inside the character and built a nest for the long-haul. And then yet another gear clicks into place—one that may very well seal the deal for the self. Somewhere around four or five, it dawns on the child that other people aren't just background characters in Ethan's movie. They have their own movies, their own preferences and secrets, their own reasons for crying in the bathtub. Before this realization, the child assumes everyone sees what they see and knows what they know—a worldview that is, ironically, the closest thing to true Vision they'll experience until Spirit corrects the error (more on this in Parts 2 & 3). After it, the child believes that the world is full of other narrators, each one sealed inside their own character, each one operating from their own incomplete script. The game has officially become multiplayer.

None of this, it should be highlighted, happens at once—not through any single event, anyways. It happens the way a river carves a canyon: one unremarkable day at a time. Also none of this, it needs to be said, is a mistake. Without a functioning sense of self, the organism cannot navigate the physical world—cannot distinguish its hand from the stove, its hunger from someone else’s, its body from the oncoming traffic. There are clinical names for this, and none of them relate to liberation. A child who never develops the felt boundary between this and that doesn't become enlightened—it becomes unable to feed itself, unable to communicate its pain, unable to recognize where the jar ends and the hand holding it begins. The self, at this stage, is not a prison, but a necessary organ—as vital to the developing human as the lungs or the liver, without which the incarnation simply cannot function.

But somewhere along the way, the organ began to believe it was the whole organism. That i am Me. As such, the boundary between “me” and “not me,” which did not exist at birth, is now being drawn with increasing confidence and specificity, reinforced thousands of times a day by everyone and everything in the child’s environment. The “I” starts putting together a hefty résumé.

In most of our early school years, comparisons are drawn between oneself and one's peers through grades, awards, and athletic performance, revealing exactly where “you” rank among “them.” There is a fastest kid and a slowest kid, a smartest and a dumbest, a teacher’s pet and a real class clown. Praise is given for being intelligent, creative, or well-behaved—or criticism received for being lazy, difficult, or distracted. Each judgment etches deeper lines around not just the fact that one is a self, but what kind of self one is, as well as what position it’s in.

On and on this spins, the résumé increasing in credibility until adolescence, after which the project of the self’s lifetime begins: deciding who it wants to be. Or, more accurately: who it wants to be seen as.

Biologically adaptive teens then tend to either conform to fit in or rebel to stand out, both strategies, ironically, reinforcing the same underlying assumption: that there is a separate “you” who must be perceived a certain way by those who are “not you.” The skater boy and the popular girl, the honor student and the one who plays hooky behind the gymnasium—all are playing the same game of self from different corners of the Chutes and Ladders board.

Hormones then arrive with all the subtlety of a house fire—pouring gasoline on an already-smoldering identity crisis. Now the stories start writing themselves. “My parents are, like, super lame or whatever, and might actually be trying to ruin my life on purpose.” “My girlfriend dumped me on Monday (that bitch!), moved on by Tuesday (that slut!), and then imprinted me with an unhealthy idea of what masculinity is and *isn’t* (that worldview I’ll carry for the next 12 years!)”

These are still the surface-level programs—the ones people eventually laugh about over drinks years later. Running underneath them, though, installed by the same machinery during the same volatile years, are the ones that get talked about less. The child who concludes that their neurodivergence or chronic illness means that they themselves are fundamentally broken or especially special. Or the teen who gets sat down by both parents at once, and told that mom and dad still love each other very much but are going to live in different houses now: *I’m the one* who caused this. Or the kid who got picked apart daily for their weight or skin or stutter or clothes, and who learned that, actually, the safest thing to do with their uniqueness is to never, ever let anyone see it.

In other words, the self, now thoroughly convinced of its own reality, simultaneously discovers its own horrifying fragility. As such, it promptly begins constructing the defenses it will carry for much, if not the rest of its life: the cool indifference, the preemptive rejection, the talking-too-much, the never-talking-at-all, the becoming-very-good-at-this-one-thing-so-that-at-least-they-can't-take-this-from-me.

In modern society, by the time one has stumbled out of this assembly line—diploma in hand, personality more or less welded into place—the self is no longer just a name and a ranking. It's an origin myth, complete with villains, turning points, and a thesis about what kind of world this is and what kind of person one has to be to survive in it. “*I’m the one* who got left behind.” “*I’m the one* who doesn't need anyone.” “I’m the funny one, because if they're laughing they're not looking too closely.”

It’s here, at long last, after nearly three decades of calibration, that the initial shaping of one’s nervous system gradually tapers off. By the early-to-mid-twenties the self finally with everything it needs to sustain itself until senescence: a name, a list of possessions, a rank, a story, a set of values, a philosophy about its own existence, and—perhaps most consequentially —a relationship *to the patterns of life itself*. Not just “things happen to me,” but “things happen to me because I’m an empath, because I'm a Scorpio, because of my attachment style, because of what my father did or didn't do.”

Such foundational impressions are very persuasive folktales. It places the “I” (that was largely built by outside forces) at the exact center of the cosmos it constructed, and then retroactively arranges an entire metaphysics around how it got there and what it believes it’s supposed to be doing. What now remains, for many, is something of a shell of an adult body with the same unmet needs and unprocessed wounds it carried at eight or nine.

Of course, the particular story one ends up with varies wildly depending on where and when the installation took place. Life in rural Kentucky looks very different from life in suburban Madrid or downtown Tokyo or the deserts outside Marrakesh. And the nervous system—not knowing where it might grow up or what language it might speak—must be malleable enough to adapt to any way of life, adopt any set of values, and learn any behaviors. This is partly why the boot-up period is so long compared to other mammals. The software needs ample time to calibrate to its specific environment.

What next? After all the adolescent hullabaloo? Well, one gets a ‘real’ job, of course!—or *doesn’t*—but confirms their identity around either. One falls in love—or doesn't—and confirms an identity around that, too. One travels, thinks, accumulates, trusts, achieves, fails, recovers, stagnates—every single experience, however mundane, getting processed through the same tireless question: *what does this mean about what I already know myself to be?* The promotion means I'm worthy. The rejection means I'm not. The spiritual experience means I'm special. The depression means I'm broken. A machine for converting raw life into autobiography, with the autobiography always, always, always being about the same main character.

And then, eventually—because the story will always run into the same wall described in Chapter 1, the same impermanence and insufficiency that no amount of achieving or becoming can resolve—the self goes looking for answers. It picks up a spiritual book, maybe. Attends a retreat or two or dozen. Sits on a cushion. Munches a few grams of magic mushrooms. Reads the bible. And discovers, to its enormous relief, an entire industry waiting to assure it that this, too, is about you: “You are here for a reason.” “Remember who you are.” “You are infinite.” Each statement offered with warmth and sincerity—and none of them wrong, exactly—only that they reinforce the very I that caused this whole mess to begin with.

## Protagonism

Let’s see if you and I can agree on this: What do all of your problems have in common? Crashed car, failed relationship, fat, broke, lost cat—misfortune takes many forms, but what threads them all together? What is the common denominator?

Strip away the specifics—the impermanence, the impressions, the *saṃskāras*, the thirst, the conditioning, the confirmation loops, all of it—and what remains at the center of every moment of suffering is you. Your (or maybe your child’s) crashed car. Your failed relationship. Your fatness. Your poverty. Your lost cat.[[It is no coincidence, by the way, that virtually every study on human wellbeing arrives at the same somewhat embarrassing conclusion: the people who report the highest life satisfaction are the ones who spend the least amount of time thinking about themselves. Volunteers, caregivers, monastics, the grandmother who won't stop feeding the neighborhood birds—the research keeps pointing at them and saying these are the happy ones. Very few seem to want to take the hint.]]

Again, the problem here is not that the mind learned to say I—that much was inevitable. The problem is that the mind believes in I, cannot stop thinking about I, is absolutely convinced of I. Morning to night, year after year, decade after decade, an unbroken stream of self-referential computation: how do I look, I should add more salt to my food, am I safe, did I already use this example, what's in it for me, I’m too old for this shit, I think I have time to pee before the meeting, ugh…why did I do that, what if I'm wrong, what if I’m right, I don’t wanna text back, where do I go when I die?[[Even thoughts not about oneself are still mostly about oneself: “The cashier was rude to that woman”—and yet who is the one bothered by it? “Everyone's lost their fucking minds”—relative to whose? “He needs to get over it already”—his grief timeline is not conforming to my expectations. “How could they do that to her?”—said with an unspoken “I would never” right behind. The mind can dress self-interest in the clothing of observation, empathy, even righteous anger, but underneath every costume is the same body: a me evaluating everything in terms of a me.]]

It is relentless. And exhausting. And, frankly, unnecessary.

Unnecessary because there is, in fact, a way of existing without the placing the self in the middle of things.

Presumably, as you read these words, you do not feel as though you were staring with your eyes at the page, but rather you feel like you are looking through your eyes upon the page, like a driver who peers through their car’s front windshield. This is the rather secure feeling of having (or being) a perspective—a point of reference—from which reality is being viewed from. This is the “I’m over here observing that over there” feeling that we’re all intimately familiar with. But that’s not technically what’s happening, is it?

No, it isn't that you're seeing the page; it's that some of the million-gazillion light particles particles that were emitted from the sun (or a light source that is ultimately being powered by the sun) bouncing off the ink at the perfect angle to unite with your retinas. These retinas then translate that photonic energy into electrochemical signals, which travel through your optic nerves to the occipital lobe where a rough reconstruction of the scene is assembled. That constructed image then gets relayed through various cortical networks, picking up the residue of the past—one's subconscious assumptions and *saṃskāras*—before landing in the prefrontal cortex where definitive conclusions are drawn about the scene being seen.[[A more detailed analysis of this will be presented in Chapter 5.]]

In other words: the accurate, three-dimensional world one believes they are seeing—the one filled with objects and movement—is actually a two-dimensional image projected onto one’s retinas, reconstructed inside one’s skull to seem three-dimensional and filled with objects, then filtered through one’s personal conditioning (impressions) before being taken as “reality.” And so it is with your other sensory channels: the smell of the roses, the ping of a notification, the touch of a hand. None of it is happening over there in objective space. The molecules, air pressure waves, and mechanical vibrations are happening right here—in dimensionless subjectivity—in the only place where experience could ever, will ever occur. Actually, the same is also true of so-called internal sensations—those which happen within the boundaries of one’s skin. The hunger pang in the stomach, the tightness in the lower back, the temperature spike of a fever. Experientially, these things are not technically happening “down there,” or “inside.” Just like sights and sounds and smells, these stimuli are being decoded right here.

There is, however, one apparent exception to all of this. The sense of self. The smell of the rose seems to come from the rose, the ping from the phone, the touch from the hand, the hunger from the stomach—but the sense of “me” seems to be happening at zero distance. It *doesn’t* seem to be coming from anywhere! “I” feels like the one thing that is a point of view rather than something arriving at a point of view.

And yet consider the phrase that many use, casually, without thinking about its implications: “I know myself.” Who is the I and who is the myself here? And which one is actually you?! Such a statement implies that the thing doing the noticing is separate from the thing being noticed. The very act of claiming self-awareness places the self at some distance, “over there”, from whatever is doing the awaring.

“Oh! I get it now!” the mind triumphantly declares, believing it has finally solved the puzzle. “I must be the the witness! The observer! The pure noticing behind all that can be noticed! Life is not happening outside of an unchanging me, but rather that I am happening inside of an unchanging Life! Wow.”

This is where most spiritual seekers happily come to rest, having peeled back enough layers to feel they’ve finally located the real “I”: not the body, not the sense of self, but the silent awareness in which everything else appears.

But notice what just happened. The moment one says “I am the awareness,” awareness has been turned into an object—a thing to be claimed, identified with, taken credit for. Whatever is aware of awareness cannot itself be the awareness.

As such, there is no final hiding place for the “I” to settle in and call home—not the body, not the self, not even awareness. Each one, when looked at directly, dissolves into what was looking. And then that dissolves, too. There is no bottom. How could there be? What would it even mean for something to be happening inside as compared to outside? Where would such a boundary even exist? There is just what is, arising, with nothing standing apart from it.

And yet, nearly everyone feels like such a line can be drawn somewhere. It is very challenging to shake the sense that I am a nested Russian doll called “the observer” or “soul” sitting somewhere inside an even doll called “the personality” which operates a further layer called “the body," which navigates a final one called “the Universe.”

{{Image}}

If any of this is actually landing—if the floor is currently dropping out below “you” to reveal what Is—it almost certainly won’t last. Within seconds, sometimes within a single breath, the self comes back online, fully assembled, as though nothing happened at all.

How does this reconstruction occur? Simply by identifying with the thought which makes it so. With the senses, the “me” feels like an observer—a passive receiver of incoming data. But with thoughts, the “me” feels like an author. There is this unmistakable feeling of your thoughts being produced—as though you, the alleged observer, were reaching into your own belly and pulling out words, one after another, tossing them into awareness like a street performer pulling scarves from a hat. I thought that. I came up with that idea. I decided to think about that just now.

But did you? Can you know your next thought before it arrives? Obviously not! That would be impossible by definition. In truth, a thought arises the way everything else arises: unbidden, unannounced, fully formed by the time it gets noticed and claimed. The only difference is that sensations feel like they’re coming from out there, and so are categorized as not me, whereas a thought feels like it’s coming from in here, and so is categorized as yes me.[[By the way, if any part of you cares to debate such a claim—if a thought just arose insisting “no, I definitely am the thinker my own thoughts”—notice what just happened: a thought appeared, uninvited, claiming to be the author of itself. The defendant is serving as its own witness, citing itself as evidence of its own existence.]]

This is why every legitimate spiritual tradition, every meditative practice, every form of psychotherapy and healing modality starts by working at level of thoughts. Awareness itself is too rarefied to grab onto. The dissolution of the observer is too abstract to even pursue. But thoughts are right there, all day, in plain view, demanding to be believed. They are the neat little bridge between the mind and the place beyond the mind. Begin to question them, watch them, refuse to identify with them—and the entire architecture of subject-object duality slowly starts to loosen until…well…we’ll talk about it in Chapter 7.

Still you may remain unconvinced. Most remain so certain of their own solidified existence —so profoundly, cellularly sure of their own protagonism—that even the possibility of being proven wrong must be banished from the mind. It is simply must be taken as a settled matter that ‘I’ exist in the way I think I do.

And yet, it is by no means settled. The idea has simply been reinforced by nearly every institution, language, relationship, religion and spiritual teaching so many times, by so many voices, across so many centuries, that it has achieved the status of Truth without ever having earned it.

It would not be the first time. For thousands of years, humanity was equally certain that the Earth sat at the center of the Cosmos—an assumption so self-evident that questioning it was not merely wrong but heretical. *The sun obviously revolves around the Earth—just look up, you imbecile!* And yet, when the math was finally done and the observations honestly recorded, not only was the Earth not at the center, but it was revealed that no center exists at all. It turns out that space is not a room with a floor plan and a geometric middle. It is an infinite, expanding, roiling medium of energy, none of it more central than any other. Every point is equally peripheral and equally the center depending on where one happens to be standing—which is to say, depending on one's perspective, which is to say, depending on the very thing one assumed was settled. It is on this single flimsy distinction—on the felt texture of where thoughts seem to originate —that the entire empire of selfhood is built. But the *feeling of being a thinker* is itself just another feeling—a sensation no different in kind from the warmth of sunlight on the arm or the pressure of a shoe on the foot. This means that, like every other felt sense, it can be inspected, dissected, and seen clearly as impermanent (anicca) and unsatisfactory (dukkha). Given the right set of instructions, one can see for themselves how thoughts just spontaneously appear in an infinitely large room that no one is sitting in.

If in reading this a special kind of dread has crept in—a sturdy “well then what the hell am I?”—rest assured that the claim is not that nothing exists. Something is clearly here, reading these words, feeling that dread. Rather, what is being questioned is the extent to which you are what you think you are. Is the “you” that feels so located, so boundaried, so certain, an accurate report of the situation? Or is it just the mind’s oldest and most convincing impression yet? The Buddhists have a word for this kind of inquiry: anattā—literally “not-self”—the idea that no sensation, no thought, no experience, no observer possesses a fixed, independent essence. Again, this does not mean “nothing exists”—a reading that has launched a thousand nihilistic misinterpretations. It means nothing has a set-in-stone identity that survives inspection.

Consider the ancient thought experiment: if a ship sets sail and, over the course of its voyage, every single plank is replaced one by one—the hull, the mast, the deck, the rudder—is it the same ship that left port? And if not, at which plank did it stop being itself? The answer, of course, is that there was never an “itself” to begin with—only the mind's insistence on drawing a circle, and then calling the circle an independent thing.

Now apply the same question inward. How many memories would you need to lose before you stopped being yourself? How many personality traits could be stripped away? How many beliefs revised, preferences abandoned, cells replaced (the body, after all, swaps out nearly every one of its atoms over the course of a decade) before the “I” you are so certain of is no longer there? The riddle resolves itself the same way: ‘I’ was never who ‘I’ thought ‘I’ was in the first place. There was only the mind’s insistence on drawing an arbitrary circle around a stream of ever-changing sensations, thoughts, and impressions, and deciding to call that circle “me.” In new age spirituality, this is known as the ego. Not ego as in chauvinistic or cocky, but the felt conviction of being a separate being which gives rise to its own thoughts. Separate not in the obvious sense that one's body ends at the skin and the table begins at its edge. That kind of distinction is functional, practical, and, as established, necessary. The separation the ego suspects is real is the belief that what it is and what the world is are two different things. It is the assumption that something or someone can be carefully plucked from the tapestry of Reality without the rest of the tapestry coming with it. Separation is the impression that my consciousness is mine, and your consciousness is yours. It’s the nagging feeling of merely being a guest at the party of Life—a party one did not decide to throw, in a house one only rents, wearing a costume one did not pick out.

Such a feeling cannot be held in moderation. It’s impossible to be partially separate—half connected, half adrift. Either the boundary of me vs. you runs all the way around, or it doesn't run at all. The choice, then, however unconscious, is always binary: unity or separation.

Under the verdict of unity—the idea that absolutely everything is one with absolutely everything else—it will be discovered that nothing has ever been at stake. Life, being infinitely interwoven, is seen as already Whole. How could it not be? Now every encounter is simply known as reality meeting itself. Now there is, quite literally, nothing to fear.

But under the verdict of separation, a very specific kind of aloneness arises. Under this paradigm, other people are also sealed off, running their own survival programs, accessible only through the glass of their own isolated point of view. Now even if they claim to love you, they *cannot actually do* so, for love between separate selves is always conditional, always temporary, and always one negotiation away from collapse.

Indeed, a dog-eat-dog world is the only world available when separation is true. If you really are a sealed-off subject in an alien world, then every craving must be a prudent signal to acquire, lest you starve. Every aversion must be an urgent alarm to retreat, lest you get hurt. Every saṃskāra must be a battle-tested reflex, lest you be forced to admit you’ve been defending an empty room.

In other words, if the “me” is indeed a poow, wittle, fwagile, fwuitfwy who woke up in a big, mean, godless, Darwinian arena with no rescue coming, then hoarding, marauding, scavenging, defending, comparing, acquiring, and clinging are the only appropriate actions. It’s that or utter annihilation, anyways.

Of course, it is possible to swap greed for generosity, lust for discipline and arrogance for humility, and still be operating from the exact same premise: that there is a separate “me”—a higher self, say—who is doing the spiritualizing. Not so. The spiritualizing, if you like that language, is simply happening the way a heart beats or a wound heals. Automatically, naturally, and without a doer.

In the end, then, the question is not how to enlighten or improve or remove the self, for such a project is incoherent (the self which claims to want liberation is the very self which is causing the bondage)! The question, actually, is how liberation actually occurs when no one is there doing it, and what, if not a wizard, has been running Oz all along.

## Behind the Curtain

Look around the room you're in. Not to identify its contents—the mind has no problem doing that automatically—but to notice what's between them. The open space between the lamp and the wall. The gap between the edge of this page and whatever lies beyond it. How would you describe that space? It’s not exactly empty, nor full, is it? It's just there, holding everything in position, all without calling attention to itself.

Now try something a bit stranger: instead of looking into your visual field, with its multitude of shapes and colors, try to look at your visual field itself—the screen upon which color and shadow do their little dance. Keep your head and eyeballs completely still, and, using your peripherals, internally trace the perimeter of everything you can currently see—the top, the bottom, the left edge, the right. There must be a boundary somewhere, because your eyes can only receive light from certain angles. Find it.

Once you’ve investigated your visual boundary—or something close to it—take a moment to consider its shape. Shape is the right word, for if your vision does indeed have an outline, it must also have a two-dimensional form; otherwise, you’d be able to see spherically, in all directions at once.

In asking my students this over the years, people have reported all sorts of fun answers back to me. Infinity symbols, rectangles, triangles, amorphous blobs. The most common answer by far, though, and the one you can probably verify right now, is that the visual field is roughly ovular—wider from left to right than it is from top to bottom. A bit like an eye.[[A third eye, some might say!]]

Now roll your eyes alllll the way up, staring almost cross-eyed between your brows. See the overlapping visions of the eyes, as well as the black insides of your eyelids separating light from dark. Now roll all them down toward the bridge of your nose—two blurry, cut-off ridges flanking a color gradient bleeding outward. Now all the way to the left. And right. Tell me: what do you notice about the very outer rim of your vision?

The edges are soft rather than hard, right? The visual field doesn't cut off like the frame of a photograph. It fades out gradually, like a vignette, into…into…what, exactly? Not quite darkness. Something more like the visual equivalent of open air—spacious, edgeless, colorless, blank, empty, free, eternal.

It is possible to recognize this tapering off into infinity but not realize its implications, so let’s make it even clearer with another example.

Take off your shoe, if you're wearing one, and bring attention to your right foot. Feel the whole thing altogether—sole, arch, heel, toes—and ask yourself: where does my foot end and the world begin? At its skin? But what about just above the foot and skin—do you feel anything there? A vibration or glow or aura steaming of the its surface? How far out does that extend? Now forget the foot and zoom in with your attention to just your big toe—the pad, the nail bed, the knuckle. Try to find the exact point where your toe turns into your foot. Is there one? Or does it kind of just…bleed…from one to the other the way night moves from dusk to dawn? Once again, the fact that nothing has a set-in-stone essence, identity or independence—is anattā. Not as dusty Buddhist principle, but as verifiable claim to notice about experience, right now, with nothing but a bare foot and honest attention. Just look for your center! Go on. Try to find it. I’ll wait. I want you to point to the thing that you are that doesn't change. And if you ever think you've got it, notice what's surrounding it, and then watch the whole exercise dissolve. What’s doing the pointing? Nothing has a fixed, independent core. Not the toe, not the visual field, not the self. Each is a wiggly process the mind wrestled into a straightjacket and called a fixed thing—a verb mistaken for a noun.

Under this view, a table is not a table but a tabling—atoms vibrating, wood aging, surface wearing, the whole arrangement slowly becoming something else. The tree outside is not a tree but a treeing—photosynthesizing, shedding, growing, dying, all at once. An Ethan is not an Ethan but an Ethaning—an episodic pattern of cells, sensations, impressions, memories, and preferences, no more fixed than the weather.[[And no less beautiful for it, I might add!]]

If the “you” is indeed a process and not a thing—more of a happening than a will happen or already happened—then unlike a noun (which, by definition, must be static in order to exist) your self should be able to start and stop.

This is, in fact, exactly what we observe. In deep sleep, your self falls into oblivion—no name, no story, no preferences, no “me”. In a fit of laughter so consuming that you forget to breathe, your self is also nowhere to be found. Or during a riveting movie, the you watching vanishes into the watched. If a tree falls in the forest and no one’s around to hear it, does it make a sound? If your self disappears during a peak moment and no one’s there to miss it, did you ever exist in the way you claimed to?

Such contemplation may bring one to know, intimately and often infuriatingly, the mind's habit of drawing boundaries around perfect, seamless, borderless experience, and labeling each one “this” and “that,” or “me” and “thee.” For instance, most people can immediately recognize that what they’re calling “my toe” is actually just a raincloud of micro-sensations, none of which directly announce where “toe” ends and “not toe” begins. Yet, those same people often *can’t* not believe that their toe exists. The mind keeps on circling, labeling, and pointing to what it takes its toe to be. It seems so real!

Consider what drawing a circle requires, though. Not just a line, but a position to draw it from. Boundaries are drawn from somewhere. So when the mind carves “toe” out of the shimmer, it is not only inventing an edge. It is asserting a vantage point called me.

This is what the mind’s circling and labeling and claiming is for. Every boundary drawn— every impression carved—is a small act of accounting: this belongs to the organism, that does not; this must be defended, that can be ignored; this is inside the walls, that is out there. Which means the boundaries are not descriptions of the world at all, but the map of what one takes oneself to be, drawn in the only terms that matter to a creature trying to stay alive: mine and not mine.

But this is where the trouble actually lives, for a boundary being crossed is not a problem unless there is someone on one side of it. Reality strolling across property lines is upsetting only if a resident lives there and is precious about their land. Take away the owner, take away the loss aversion. Take away the self, take away the distress.

All of this has a name, you know. It’s called *taking things personally*. That’s the entire load-bearing structure of dukkha and misery. There is no version of unhappiness that does not include it.

And seriously…just look at all that you’ve taken personally! The body, obviously—it was supposed to hold its shape, its energy, its hair. The mind, which was supposed to stay sharp. Your name, and what people are meant to think when they hear it. Your work, your income, the trajectory your income. Your children’s opinion of you. Your parents’ health. Your sex life. The friend who was supposed to stay your friend. The country you were born in. The plans, the beliefs about how any of it should go, the whole sprawling estate of things marked as yours. Under this perspective, what do you think will happen when the body turns saggy, the mind foggy, and the lifestyle boring? What will happen when work takes an unexpected turn, the children move out, and the fertility wanes? What will happen when your friend dies, the car breaks down, and the stock market wobbles.

Well if you’re taking those changes personally, dukkha will happen, of course! Dukkha all over the place!

What makes this maddening for the spiritual seeker is the meta-awareness that comes online somewhere along the Path: one can watch the mind’s labeling and selfing—and may even understand exactly why dukkha will soon arise from it—and yet cannot stop it. The hand keeps drawing the chalk lines on the sidewalk, reality keeps being a terrible hopscotch player, and the mind keeps having its predictable little temper tantrum.

If only you could snap your fingers and stop yourself from *taking things personally*! But ya *can’t*. And that’s because the one who would let go is the one being let go of. Taking things personally is not something the self does. Taking things personally is what the self is. And that fire just *can’t* put itself out.[[Rest assured there is an escape. More in Chapter 5.]]

Interestingly, what does remain in the self's absence (when the letting go happens) *isn’t* a terrifying void the ego always warned about. It’s also not blankness or oblivion or the cold, existential nothingness one might expect after two chapters of quasi-nihilism. What remains is something the mind has no category for, because the mind only knows how to file experience under “good for me” or “bad for me,” and this is neither.[[There is a reason the sages of Advaita Vedanta refused to say what Realization is—only what it is not. Neti neti: not this, not that. The moment it is named—the moment one says “it's awareness” or “it's consciousness” or “it's God”— the mind draws yet another circle, fashions yet another noose, and archives it under ‘that which can be craved, avoided, or defended’. Ah, our young and foolish minds! The whole point is that what remains when the circles stop being drawn cannot itself be circled!]]

Some call it flow—though that word, having been so thoroughly kidnapped by productivity hackers and TED talks, barely scratches the surface. This is not the usual flow of being “in the zone” at work, the flow of a runner's high, or even the flow of spiritual achievement. This is a flow that existed prior to any of that. It is the undercurrent of bare experience, unclaimed, unlabeled, and uncircled.

It’s fucking brilliant, actually.

Which, when you see it, makes the self a rather strange item to be carrying around, if you think about it. After all, if dropping ‘me’ costs nothing and returns everything, the obvious question is why evolution went to the trouble of selecting for it in the first place?

It’s because the idea of a separate self was very, very useful for our species’ survival. Every organism faces the same problem: the world arrives once, and then it's gone. What was learned this morning is useless by evening unless something carries it across. Nature’s solution is memory—but memory alone is insufficient, because a stored fact is inert without an owner. Fire burns is not good enough to inspire a change in behavior. Fire burns me is.

But just because something was useful does not mean it needs to stay useful. It’s kind of like the tailbone (which was once a tail that slowly shortened, generation by generation) or the wisdom teeth (which a growing number of people are simply born without). Unused biology thins out until it’s gone.

The me is another such vestigial organ. It arrived when the world was a genuinely dangerous place and the only way to survive it was to know exactly where you ended and the tigers began. It kept the species alive long enough to invent language, agriculture, and antibiotics. Every one of us is here because it did its job with astonishing loyalty for a very long time.

The tigers are all-but gone now, though. The ancient dangers that made a separate self worth having—starvation, predation, tribal warfare over the last edible root—have been solved so thoroughly that most of our lives are spent recreating them from the comfort of our couches. So shouldn't we expect the software that came with them to begin to thin, too? What use is a fear-driven, ideologically self-centered human, nowadays?

Indeed, this is what we are beginning to see happen. A generation is drifting out of religions their parents took as bedrock, and yet reporting more spiritual experience, not less. Meditation apps have thirty million users and growing. Psychedelic clinics are opening in cities that used to jail people for possession. Practices that were monastic secrets a hundred years ago are on television. Something is unclenching everywhere at once—a massive spiritual awakening is under way due to fear having lost its practicality.

Amazingly, those who let go of themselves—the ones who undergo spiritualization—do not become vegetables. They do not stop recognizing their children. They can still parse these words. They still know that fire has the ability to burn.

What they are losing is the felt sense of being the small, defended thing in the middle of life. The one who suffers from unhappiness or the one who *can’t* let go of control. The one who can never seem to break free or the one who gets in their own way.

…So…

Would you like to join them?
