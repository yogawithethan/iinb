The Western world has largely taken mind to mean brain—treating the two as synonyms, or at best as two descriptions of the same thing, the way “lightning” and “electrical discharge” refer to the same event. Under this view, the mind is what the brain does, thoughts are the byproduct of neurons firing, and emotions the afterglow of chemical cascades. Understand the brain and nervous system well enough, the logic goes, and you will have understood the mind completely.

To any sufficiently advanced meditator, this is pure nonsense. It’s a bit like claiming that understanding the circuitry of a radio will help you understand the electromagnetic spectrum— the vast field of light ranging from radio waves to gamma rays it sources its music from. Sure, if you studied the radio’s constituent parts, you might eventually explain exactly how certain signals gets converted into sound waves, but doing so would never—could never—explain how the waves or field themselves work (let alone why a particular melody makes one listener want to squeal for joy, another moshpit, and a third want to call their ex). For that, one would need to study the field itself, and then double check their conclusions on the radio.

The brain is not the mind, but its receiver, and mistaking one for the other has led to some rather spectacular blind spots in how our culture approaches mental health, happiness, and the very question of what a human being is.

By analogy, imagine trying to study a brittle clay sculpture by throwing basketballs at it. What could you possibly learn? That clay shatters? The basketball is simply too large, too crude a tool to reveal anything fundamental about what the clay structure actually is. Use grains of sand, or bits of water, though, and you might begin to learn a thing or two: how clay can scrape, how under the right conditions it repels water or changes color or is clung to like static. Still, the finer details, the tiny etchings and hairline curves, the atomic placements would be missed. It wouldn’t be until you use light itself—photons, directed through a microscope—that the sculpture's true intricacy would really begin revealing itself.

This principle holds across every scale of scientific investigation. To image the surface of a metal at the atomic level, one fires electrons through a scanning electron microscope, because electrons are finer than the structures being resolved. To study the fundamental building blocks of matter itself, physicists at CERN smash subatomic particles together at near-light speed, because only something as small and energetic as a proton can reveal what's inside another proton.

The same logic applies to the mind. Using brain scans to study it is like throwing basketballs at clay sculptures. Interesting? Perhaps. Yields practical information? Technically, yes. But capable of unveiling its true essence and structure? Absolutely not. No amount of neuroscience, however sophisticated, will be sufficient to study your mind, because the mind is subtler than the brain. As was discussed in Part 1, knowing about something is not the same as knowing it directly. Being able to list facts about how mindfulness rewires the prefrontal cortex or how ingesting plants help create certain neurotransmitters or how repeating certain mantras shifts brain wave states doesn't mean a damn thing with regards to one’s own personal transformation. No, to truly study one’s own mind, something even subtler is needed. Something like consciousness itself—the aware, knowing quality prior to sensation, emotion, and the very thoughts one is attempting to observe.

This is precisely why the contemplative traditions have always insisted that the study of the mind is an interior science. Not merely because they lacked the technology for brain scans, but because it was understood that the only ‘instrument’ refined enough to observe the mind, and thus to comprehend it, is the very field of awareness—one’s *subjective experience*—in which the mind arises.

The Eastern traditions, drawing upon thousands of years of precisely this kind of direct, introspective observation, make a far more useful distinction than the Western brain-mind conflation. In Sanskrit, the word for mind is citta (pronounced CHIT-tah), derived from the root cit, meaning to perceive, to be aware, or to know. Citta is not reducible to the brain or nervous system, though it certainly operates through them. Rather, citta is the field in which all of inner experience occurs—the invisible medium through which thoughts arise, memories surface, emotions color perception, and the sense of ‘I’ takes shape. What Western psychology might divide into the conscious and subconscious are all movements and patterns within citta.

These mind movements are also known as *vṛttis* (pronounced VRIT-tees with a Spanish roll of the ‘R’ and slight hold of the ‘T’). Continuing our analogy from above, if your brain is the radio, and the citta is the field or medium, then the individual waves of music are the *vṛttis*. The word comes from the root vṛt, meaning to turn, revolve, or spin—and is typically translated as “fluctuations,” “modifications,” or “turnings” of the mind.

*Vṛttis*, or movements of citta (mind), represent every thought, evaluation, memory, and reaction that ripples through the field. Some you hear clearly, the way you'd hear a symphony on a fine-tuned radio. Others are barely perceptible, like distant stations bleeding faintly through the static. But all of them, loud or faint, are fluctuations of the same one mind.

It is precisely these fluctuations that yoga, in its original, undiluted sense, was designed to address, and what the devoted spiritual seeker must learn to quell. Indeed, one of the very first lines of Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras—a foundational text of the yogic tradition, written sometime around the second century BCE—wastes no time getting to the point:

*Yogaś citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ*—yoga is the cessation of the fluctuations of the mind.

That's it. All of yoga—its philosophy, its practices, its ultimate aim—compressed into four words: yogaś (yoga is) citta (mind) *vṛtti* (fluctuations) *nirodhaḥ* (cessation). Not “yoga is the perfection of the body.” Not “yoga is the cultivation of extraordinary psychic powers.” Not even “yoga is union with God” (though that follows naturally). Yoga is what happens when the mind stops doing what it compulsively does: thinking, hypothesizing, judging, fantasizing, ruminating, catastrophizing, and planning.

Some may hear “fluctuations of the mind” and think: *‘Oh, that’s my chatty inner monologue* *that narrates experience, replays conversations, and argues with itself about what’s what.’* And yes, that voice is certainly part of the *vṛttis*. But it is only the loudest, most obvious part—the whitecaps on the surface of a much deeper iceberg.

The subtler (and far more consequential) fluctuations are the ones going unheard, the subconscious ones running beneath the threshold of your ordinary awareness. These are your mind's constant, automatic evaluations—the citta ceaselessly and wordlessly cross-referencing every bit of incoming data against its accumulated impressions, and generating a verdict or prediction before the conscious, vocalized mind even registers that something has happened. These are the ones competing for your attention as you read this very sentence. Hello in there! To state it more cleanly: the *vṛttis* are not merely the thoughts you can hear upstairs. They are also the evaluative machinery in the basement producing the thoughts upstairs. The spinning of the warehouse as it pattern-matches, categorizes, and judges every frame and data point of incoming experience against everything it has ever stored.

Consider: you walk into a room and feel immediately uneasy. You haven't consciously assessed anything—the lighting, the temperature, the people present—but the mind has already completed thousands, perhaps millions, of micro-evaluations: this reminds me of that, that reminds me of this, and wait why is everyone is standing at an angle towards the door? By the time the thought “I don't like this situation” emerges from the vast citta, the *vṛttis* have already done their work. The residual conclusion is simply the conscious mind's clumsy attempt to narrate what the other *vṛttis* already decided several microseconds ago.

In this light, just as a perfectly sound-proofed room still rings with the body’s activities, an apparently “quiet” mind can still be roaring with *vṛttis*. The person who sits in deep meditation and senses that no thoughts are present? Undetected *vṛtti*. The subtle satisfaction of having cooled the mind? Smug *vṛtti*. The meditator who realizes “I am loving awareness.” Beatific *vṛtti*. The citta is astonishingly good at disguising its own activity as stillness, and doubly good at disguising its evaluations as observations. “I’m just noticing that I feel calm” sounds legit, but buried within it is probably a preference (calm is good), a comparison (this is calmer than before), and a subtle claim of ownership (I am the one who is calm)—three fluctuations masquerading as a single neutral statement.

So when Patañjali says that yoga is the cessation of the *vṛttis*, he is not merely recommending that one quiet the inner mockingjay—helpful and pleasant as that may be. He is pointing to something far more radical: the complete stilling of the evaluative machinery itself. Such an “accomplishment” is not done using force, pinning the mind down the way one might try to subdue a bucking horse. Rather, it is accomplished by way of un-impressing one’s citta so that the machinery stops running altogether.

This is the difference between a lake that has been completely frozen over—still on the surface but roiling underneath—and a lake that is genuinely, naturally calm because there are no currents present. The frozen lake is repression, the calm lake is *nirodhaḥ* (cessation), and the currents, as we shall soon revisit, are the *saṃskāras* that have been driving the *vṛttis*, often prior to one’s birth.

What generates these fluctuations, or distortions in one’s citta? Impressions. Picture many valleys indenting an otherwise perfectly flat landscape, causing rainwater to flow down their sides. Impressions of what something ultimately is or means create valleys on the vast terrain of citta which then cause all kinds of *vṛttis*—thoughts, judgments, etc.—to spill off its back. This overall topography is also known as one’s karma.

![A rippling landscape of peaks and wells with arrows tracing how experience flows into its grooves — the mind’s karmic topography](/images/fig-mindscape.png)

This is precisely why adhering to the yāmas and niyāmas, or any truly ethical code, really is so vital: they prevent particularly gross indentations or knots from being introduced into the field, thereby preventing, or at least lessening, new *vṛttis* from being churned out.

Eliminating unconscious tendencies *doesn’t* address the fluctuations and distortions already present, though. That’s a bit like assuming that fixing the leaky roof will somehow dry out the water-damaged floorboards. So how to shave off the mountains and backfill the valleys of one’s citta? To answer that we must point our microscope ever-inwards and zoom in one notch further.

## Chain Reaction

Let's break down the precise chain of events that brings about a singular *vṛtti*—and, just as important, what that *vṛtti* compounds into once it's arisen—for these will give the necessary context for learning how to soften each link, thereby healing and stilling the mind altogether. The first link we'll call contact, or interaction with the outer world via the body's sense doors: sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch. Something "over there" makes contact with what's "right here": the eyes, ears, nose, tongue, and skin. This stream of information may as well be infinite. Consider the fact that over a trillion, trillion atoms surround your body right now, each one bumping against your skin to distribute some small—but not quite negligible—quantity of energy onto it, and you'll get a nanoscopic snapshot of the enormity and complexity of Creation. Contact happens from the inside-out, too, for every single cell and system of your body is constantly sending your brain status updates on what it's up to and how it's faring: the placement of your big toe, what invaders the immune system is currently besetting, how much gall your gallbladder is currently galling, and so on. Like two highly-caffeinated Italians, your buzzing body can be chit-chatting away while the you which sits here reading these words remains completely unaware of what's being spoken.

There is a third kind of contact, though, one that is often overlooked: thoughts themselves. A thought appearing in consciousness is no different, mechanistically speaking, from a sound wave hitting the eardrum. It is as much a 'stimulus' arriving at a sense door as smells and tastes are, only this time the door is not a sensory organ like the nose and tongue, but rather the faculty of cognition itself.

Importantly, all forms of contact happen whether you've consented to them or not. Although right now light particles are hurtling at and through your body at hundreds of thousands of miles per second, there is nothing you can do about it. Stick your hand out and try to stop their movement. Try dodging or protecting yourself from them. Ask them to slow down. Hurl insults. Threaten, beg, scream, cry. See how far it gets you.

This continuity and choicelessness occurs with the other senses, too. Right now pressure waves keep making contact with your ear drums, saliva keeps making contact with your taste buds, and thoughts keep making contact with your mind. Sure, you could change environments or foods or states of consciousness, but that would just subject you to the next wave of unnegotiable contact.

So here you are, awash in it all. Quadrillions upon quadrillions of contact points arriving every microsecond—from the outside-in through the sense doors, from the inside-out through the body's own ceaseless stream of electrochemical dispatches, and from nowhere-in-particular through the mind's endless pontifications. Photons, pressure waves, scent and food particles, hormonal shifts, liver enzymes, temperature fluctuations, doubts, hopes, certainties—an unimaginably vast torrent of raw, unstoried information that has never once, for a single moment in the entirety of your existence, paused. And yet, miraculously, you are not overwhelmed to the point of inoperability. Somehow, out of this roaring deluge, you experience something coherent: a room, a song, a theory.

You can thank the *vṛttis* for that. The pattern-matching machinery from the last section is not sitting around waiting for contact to arrive; it is already spinning, and it meets each incoming point of contact so fast that raw arrival and coherent experience become indistinguishable to you. Is that dark object in the distance a bush waving in the wind or a lumbering bear? Did that person just say "I scream" or "ice cream"? Is my foot touching a slimy fish on the ocean floor or just a piece of seaweed? By the time you consciously wonder, the *vṛttis* have already run through thousands of micro-verdicts and delivered the answer as though it were simply what happened.

It's worth pausing here to acknowledge that this process is even less passive than it might first appear. The mind, contrary to what we are usually taught, is not a neutral receiver dutifully waiting for sense data to arrive before assembling it into experience. It is also a prediction engine. At every moment, the mind is generating expectations of what it is about to encounter— based entirely on its stored impressions—and then comparing those expectations against incoming contact. When the prediction matches what arrives, the mind barely processes the input; it simply accepts its own model as correct. When the prediction misses, the mind updates and tries again.

The implication is sobering: we do not, for the most part, meet reality directly. We meet our predictions of reality, which are then lightly verified or corrected by what actually arrives at our senses. It's only when reality strongly contradicts the prediction that the mind notices and updates. The rest of the time, it lives cemented inside the model it has built from a lifetime of accumulated impressions.

This would not be so bad were the chain to end here. If it did, all one would be dealing with is a noisy mind endlessly labeling and predicting things—inconvenient, perhaps, but not ultimately misery-making. The problem is not what happens after contact so much as what arrives with contact, inseparable from it, in the same instant.

That inseparable arising is vedanā—the feeling tone the contact wears as it arrives. Every point of contact carries one of three tones: pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral. The morning sun warming your face is pleasant; the shrill beep of the microwave is unpleasant; the smell of your pillow is neutral. The tone is not something the mind assigns after the fact—it is the affective weather of the contact itself, felt in the body before the thinking mind has arrived to sort anything out. And every contact wears one, always. There is no such thing as a colorless moment.

Vedanā, just like contact, happens with or without your consent. The body is unable to not respond in the unique way it responds. Why does water bring you joy at cold temperatures and apathy at room temperature, or vice versa? Why does exercise lead you to pleasure but pain in others? Why are you sexually attracted to who you are sexually attracted to? We have many theories, of course: natural selection, psychological conditioning, karma, free will, demonic influence, aliens. But theories, no matter how apparently true, are never essentially true.

To be clear, reality can always be viewed from a different perspective, and it is possible to rewrite conditioned responses to engage with the world differently. But these are not the same thing as saying you can change your physiological reaction to a given scenario once it's already happened. As soon as a physiological command is sent, it cannot be reversed. There is no undo button. Your present feelings are your present feelings. They must be acknowledged and dealt with, or else futilely resisted and repressed to be painfully resolved at a later date.

So if contact and vedanā both arise with or without permission—arriving together, choicelessly, as a single event—what exactly does one have agency over? Where does choice enter the picture?

It enters at the very next arising, and it enters precisely: in the gap between vedanā and saṃskāra. Between the tone alive in the body and the habituated flinch or reach that follows it. Between the sensation and the reflex. This is where the entirety of one's dukkha—and the entirety of one's liberation—is ultimately decided.

Saṃskāras, once again, are *the conditioned mind*’s habituated responses to vedanā—the leaning-toward or pulling-away that fires the moment the tone arrives. Craving toward the pleasant, aversion toward the unpleasant, apathy toward the neutral. The clinging to sensations of pleasure, the recoiling from sensations of guilt, the grasping at feelings of validation, the flinching away from feelings of insignificance.

These reactions are not conscious decisions but reflexes of conditioning. If citta is the landscape and impressions are the valleys, *saṃskāras* are the force of gravity acting upon the topography—that which ensures a particular tone will call forth a particular flinch or reach, in a predictable, automatic, and (almost always) unconscious way. The stronger the impression, the deeper the valley; the deeper the valley, the more compulsive the saṃskāra.

"Compulsive" is the operative word here, for a saṃskāra doesn't occur in the conscious mind at all. That's what makes it a reaction rather than a response. A response implies deliberation—the weighing of options, a strategy, a choice. Saṃskāras fire before reason has a chance to come to the rescue.

This automaticity is why the chain feels so seamless and sensible from the inside. The impression "I'm failing," the corresponding sensation of guilt pooling in the chest, and the subconscious flinch of aversion toward that guilt all appear to be one continuous, unified event. They're not. These are distinct arisings, each of which occurred for mechanically traceable reasons. It's only because the whole thing arrives basically pre-assembled that makes it utterly convincing. The speed and totality of the process leaves no obvious gap through which the mind can question any of it!

Crucially, a reaction doesn't just complete the chain—it confirms it. Its firing manifests as *taṇhā*, the pull of craving in the moment—the reach toward more of what feels good, the recoil from what feels bad. And *taṇhā*, sustained and repeated, is what dukkha is made out of. In other words, when a saṃskāra fires for the first or hundredth time, you aren't merely suffering in this moment; you are actively validating every single link that produced the suffering in the first place. The craving says "this sensation was necessary," which retroactively stamps the vedanā as accurate ("this really is unpleasant"), which stamps the contact as having arrived correctly ("my health really is a disaster"), which solidifies the impression that shaped both ("my health is a measure of my worth"). The entire chain, from contact to craving, hardens into what feels like an objective fact about the world—not a conditioned interpretation of sensory data or a mechanical output of accumulated impressions firing in sequence, but the way things actually, undeniably are.

This is happening to virtually everyone, everywhere, about everything, all the time.

The right-leaning voter who feels a surge of righteous fury watching cable news isn't experiencing a conditioned reaction to sensations generated by impressions accumulated over decades of cultural programming. Oh no…he’s experiencing "the obvious truth that this country is going to hell." The progressive who doomscrolls through climate data and feels a pit in their stomach isn't just experiencing a saṃskāra of despair compulsively firing in response to stimuli filtered through a particular evaluative framework. She's experiencing "the inescapable reality that we're all going to die and nobody cares." The generic spiritual influencer who feels a swell of warmth every time someone comments "truth!" with the prayer hand emojis isn't just experiencing craving toward the pleasant sensations of validation generated by an impression that equates engagement with alignment. They're experiencing "confirmation that I'm on the right path."

None of them are necessarily wrong about what they feel. But they are almost always wrong about why they feel it. Every single one of them believes they are responding to reality, when in fact they are reacting—blindly, automatically, subconsciously—to sensations produced by a chain they cannot see, built on impressions they accidentally adopted, and reinforced by *saṃskāras* they don't know they have. And the most maddening part? The very intensity of the feeling is what makes it seem so obviously, self-evidently true. Of course this is real—look how strongly I feel it! As if the depth of a groove were evidence of the terrain's objectivity, rather than evidence of how many times the water has run through the same channel.

This is why people don't just stay stuck—they get more stuck over time. And more righteous about it. Every reaction is a vote of confidence in the impression that triggered it and a deepening of the saṃskāra which fueled it. With enough votes cast over enough years, the impression stops feeling like an impression altogether and starts presenting itself as identity. "I'm an anxious person." "I've always had trouble focusing." "I don't trust others easily." These aren't naturalistic observations about the real you; they're *saṃskāras* so deeply grooved that they've become load-bearing walls in the architecture of "me."

Enough of this insanity. Time to let go.

## Letting Go

As a child, I once ran at full-speed across a concrete driveway, turned a corner, tripped over a fence post I didn’t know was there, and gouged my right knee straight into the rough pavement. Seconds later I was awash in pain, the cold, grey cement now flowing with rivulets of my blood. Now here I am, safely typing these words into a machine in a cabin out in rural New Zealand, two decades later, with nothing to show for that fateful day but a faint discoloration on my kneecap.

How did my body heal so completely? I wasn’t supervising the intricate process of cells clotting and stitching, collagen fibers weaving and white blood cells marshaling, that’s for sure. If I had been, I would have required a verbosely detailed checklist of what came after what to oversee the whole operation. And even then, I undoubtedly would have skipped a step or two or million out of pure distraction or laziness.

That there is an unstoppable Force guiding life towards wholeness and coherence—drawing electrons into stable orbitals around their nuclei, coaxing matter into the rotating spiral arms of galaxies, coordinating the bloom of flowers with the emergence of their pollinators, knitting wounds closed without being asked—and that this Force asks nothing from us in return but allowance, is perhaps the most miraculous of all miracles. How physicists can spend entire careers obsessing over decay and breakdown (entropy) that they brush by its counterpart (exotropy, or the Universe's magnificent insistence on keeping things organized) is, to me, both laughable and infuriating. Sometimes I want to shake people by the shoulders and scream from the rooftops: *Rejoice! Healing is inevitable! All you have to do is say yes!*[[Or, more precisely, stop saying no!]]

This fact applies to the mind just as much as the body, for, while the mind can also trip, fall, and bleed, it can also be made new. The same Universal Force (also referred to as the Holy Spirit or the Great Aum) is the only thing which can unimpress the very mind which would resist its unimpressing. Sure, after all is said and done, there may be a few faint scars here and there, the past still subtly revealing itself in its structure and personality for a time. But the injury or trauma is no longer to the point of running the show, the way a bashed knee forces the rest of the body to compensate with a limp.

What does it actually look like for the mind to be healed? In its most fundamental sense, it is the total release of fear and return to love. Every saṃskāra, every distorted impression, every defensive grip the mind has ever taken—at their root, each is a contraction around fear. Fear of being unsafe, fear of being unloved, fear of being insufficient, fear of being annihilated, fear of fear. Healing, in this context, is the dissolution of such a fear-based operating system, allowing the mind to settle back into its original condition: open, trusting, generous, and at peace.

How does the mind get back there?

By the time you've arrived at this book, chances are you've already tried a great many things to release the grip of fear and recover that original peace. Perhaps you've journaled. Or meditated. Or paid for therapy, psychedelic ceremonies, breathwork retreats, and somatic experiencing sessions. Perhaps you've taken SSRIs, or quit them. Perhaps you've gone vegan, gluten-free, carnivore, raw. Perhaps you've fasted, ice-bathed, sunbathed, mouth-taped, dopamine-detoxed, Wim-Hoffed, ayahuasca'd. Perhaps you've read the Bhagavad Gītā, A Course in Miracles, the Tao Te Ching, the Pali Canon, Eckhart Tolle, Joe Dispenza, Byron Katie, Ram Dass, Krishnamurti, every Substack newsletter promising the final answer. Perhaps you’ve stood barefoot on the earth at sunrise. Perhaps you've sat with monks or flushed coffee up your bum. Perhaps you’ve reignited your relationship with Jesus. Perhaps you’ve renounced Him altogether. Perhaps you've moved across the world, got married, got divorced, opened up your relationship, gone silent for ten days, faced many of your demons.

And yet here you are—still reading spiritual books, I see…—still hoping, still looking, still suspecting that the thing you're after is real but somehow elusive. Such persistence is actually rather sacred. The seeker's hunger is the very thing that will eventually deliver them home. But it will not deliver them there through finding the perfect technique, the right teacher, or the missing ingredient. It will deliver them by exhausting the seeking itself—by finally collapsing into the recognition that what was being looked for could not be found, only allowed.

This allowance of what is Natural and Good is what the common spiritual advice of non-attachment is all about. In truly trusting Īśvara to govern one’s return to Him, one no longer wrestles over the specifics of life’s unfoldment. Because you believe that you are destined to slingshot back home, you then lose the desperation that your mind need be healed at all. It is understood that the *saṃskāras* which are meant to dissolve will dissolve in their own perfect timing, and that there is nothing to do but permit the process to take place. Ironically, as a result of this realization, the mind relaxes its striving and attains the equanimity it was seeking. To spell it out more clearly, non-attachment is the entire path to liberation. It *isn’t* a technique, discipline or something the self does to itself, though. It is the cessation of the one thing preventing healing from occurring on its own: resistance. Resistance is the bracing, the clenching, the insistence that this moment could somehow be different—better, maybe—from how it currently is.

While one can resist a great number of things in every day life, on the spiritual path, *there is only ever one* resistance to overcome: the resistance to the vedanā which is currently present. When you remain attached to a feeling tone via resistance—when the saṃskāra is allowed to fire in the dark so the impression gets endorsed—you are, in effect, re-opening the wound which has been so diligently attempting to close.[[The body would heal the knee perfectly well if you didn’t keep re-smashing it on the pavement every afternoon! So too would the fear-based impression heal if you stopped believing its accuracy every time it surfaced!]]

It is by no accident, then, that when Patañjali tells us how the fluctuations of the mind are stilled—just a few sūtras after his famous definition of yoga—he names only two ingredients: *abhyāsa-vairāgyābhyāṃ tan-nirodhaḥ*: “through practice (abhyāsa) and non-attachment (vairāgya), the fluctuations cease.” Of the two, the tradition is unequivocal: vairāgya is the more essential, for a practice without non-attachment is just the ego in orange robes, gripping the meditation cushion the way it used to grip the career ladder or the relationship. One can sit for ten thousand hours and emerge more entrenched than when they began if the sitting was performed in the spirit of acquisition—acquiring calm, or insight, or spiritual status—rather than in the spirit of release.

It is, of course, great to hold non-attachment as a virtue going forward. But what about the stockpile the mind has already attached to and holds dearly? What of the many saṃskāric receipts one has stuffed into the back pocket?

Well, obviously, one needs to learn let go of them.[[To be clear, letting go and non-attachment are not two separate things, but the same event described from two different angles. From the perspective of the chain reaction, we call it non-attachment—the mind simply does not grip the sensation that generation produced. From the perspective of the one sitting with the intense sensation which is hard to accept, we call it letting go—listening to rather than fighting what has arisen. Neither is something you do to a sensation; it is what happens when you stop doing something to it. The sensation arises, the saṃskāra reaches for the grip—and this time the hand simply stays open. The chain, for this one revolution, does not complete. The wound, for the first time in perhaps decades, is not re-opened. And the healing intelligence—the same one that grows oaks from acorns—moves in, unobstructed, and does what it has always done.]]

Letting go could not be more straightforward. It is not analyzing, naming, fixing, or transcending, for all of those are still the mind's strategies for taking the wheel. It is also not pushing an experience away, for pushing is just another form of holding. Letting go is what happens when one finally stops trying to do anything. It is allowance. It is consent to everything being exactly as it is. There is nothing heroic or revolutionary about letting go. It is, in fact, the most ordinary thing in the world. It is a profound, unbothered yes. It is the nodding of one’s head towards an eternity of impermanence. Thoughts, sensations, emotions, *saṃskāras*—all are permitted to rise and pass without being chased or chased away.

“I get it, Ethan, letting go is the doorway to peace. But how do I do it? How do I let go?” Finally! At long last! This is the most important and practical question a sincere spiritual seeker can ask on the Path, for in personally answering the question, they will have found the key to ascending to the highest pinnacles of Bliss. How do I let go? How do I trust? How do I not cling to or recoil from my sensational preferences?

The answer is deceptively simple, and can be seen in broad daylight if we simply reframe the concept. Consider someone gripping a pen tightly. Their knuckles are white, their forearm is tensed, and the strain is steadily worsening with every passing minute. Imagine now that such a person screamed to the heavens: “How do I let go of this pen?! It burns! Please, someone help!!” To anyone listening, the question is comically self-answering. What do you mean how? Just let go of it, silly goose! There is no intermediate step between gripping and releasing, no secret technique, no seven-stage protocol for finger extension. The hand already knows how to open—it's been opening since the day you were born. The only thing preventing it from opening right now is *the gripping itself!*

Of course, the one strangling the pen isn't being stupid—they're being honest. From the inside, after years or decades of clenching, a fist doesn't feel like something they're doing, but rather something they are. The tension has become so familiar that it registers as neutral, as baseline, as “just the way my hand is.” Consequently, the instruction to “just let go” sounds either impossibly vague or offensively dismissive, because such a person genuinely cannot locate the part of themselves that is doing the holding.

In truth, the one choking the pen is actually just harboring subconscious worries that first need to be released before the pen itself can be: “But my grandmother gave me this pen.” “But what if I need it later?” “But the pen might be upset if I dropped it.” These aren't foolish objections, but the actual texture of attachment. These are the background stories the mind uses to justify its grip, the elaborate architecture of reasons constructed around what is, at bottom, just a pesky sense of self that one subconsciously refuses to expand and let breathe.

In other words, beneath all of these surface-level objections, beneath the sentimentality and the habit and the so-called practicalities, lies the real reason the hand won't open: fear. Fear that in dropping the pen, it might be lost forever. Fear of what one will be without the familiar weight of the thing they've been carrying. Fear of the inevitable and unpredictable free-fall that occurs between the pen releasing and the pen coming to rest on the Earth.

But it is precisely this ground that the pen is meant to find. A pen held up is a pen in suspended animation—full of potential energy, requiring constant vigilance to uphold, always just one moment of inattention away from clattering to the floor. A pen on the ground, however, is a pen made stable. It has returned to its proper place with nowhere left to fall. It can sit there, undisturbed, for as long as it darn well pleases.

Despite how easy and poetic it can sound on the page, the practice of letting go is the very thing *the conditioned mind* *cannot actually do*, no matter how badly it claims it wants to. This is because letting go does not occur through will-power (the realm of *the conditioned mind*), but through surrender and trust. You will not—cannot—let go unless it has faith that there is something to release into. A person standing at the edge of a cliff does not simply leap into the void; they surrender to the force of gravity, a force that will catch and carry them whether they understand its mechanics or not. A yogi sinking into the full splits cannot relax their groin through clever technique or muscular instruction; they relax only by knowing that the floor is there to receive them, and because the body senses, somatically and prior to any thought, that they will survive the stretch.

The same is true for the healing of the mind via the releasing of attachments. For the mind to truly let go, it must have faith that it is letting go into something—something steady, present, and trustworthy enough to receive what is being released. The mind cannot surrender into a void, however existentially attractive a void might sound. It needs security.

That foundation is what the yogis called Tat—“That”—the unchanging, intelligent presence within all of creation. In the Christian tradition, this same recognition is called Christ, or the Son in the Trinity—God reflected within form, the living foundation accessible to a mind that cannot yet hold the *Unmanifested Absolute*. We will explore this more deeply in Part 3, when the Christian framework comes fully into view. For now, what matters is that your mind is not letting go into nothing. It is letting go into the infinite intelligence that cycles the breath, induces the sleep, and heals the wound.

But knowing that Tat is there to catch the fall is only half of what's required. The fingers only unfurl when awareness itself is seen; when one becomes *aware that one is aware* of the gripping. The distinction sounds pedantic, but it is the entire hinge on which liberation swings. First-order awareness still contains a subtle ‘I’ who notices something—my grip, my tension, my saṃskāra. Second-order awareness sees the noticing itself, and in that seeing, the ‘my’ evaporates. What was ‘my saṃskāra’ becomes ‘a saṃskāra.’ What was ‘my resistance’ becomes ‘resistance, doing what resistance does.’

If one can manage this kind of objective observation—impersonalizing the habit pattern—as well as surrendering the mind to Tat, the hand does eventually open. And when it does, the overwhelming experience is not loss but relief—a sudden, almost embarrassing recognition of how much effort it took to hold on. How much energy did it take to relax the hand? Zero energy. How much effort did it take to feel what was already there? Zero effort. It cost nothing, which means you were paying money to keep your own suffering alive!

But now that the hand has begun to soften, and the pen started to slip, what’s next?

## Keeper of the Flame

As all devoted seekers quickly learn: beneath one’s grip lies a million contractions, each subtler than the last, each requiring a more refined awareness to perceive and an even gentler hand to release. Practically speaking, letting go is not a technique, but a disposition—not a singular act but a moment-to-moment orientation. It deepens, layer by layer, until it reaches the very contraction at the bottom of the self—the original sin which started this mess in the first place.

To meet the increasing demands of surrender, one needs more than just a philosophical understanding of non-attachment. One needs the lived skill of returning to it again and again in increasingly subtle territory. That means flat-out ignoring every fiber of one's conditioning which begs for reaction, fighting back, doing something, anything, other than allowing what is here to be. This skill is built slowly but surely through the most ancient practice human beings have ever discovered for working with their own minds: meditation.

Meditation is not the relaxation exercise it has been packaged as in the West. It is closer to the analogy passed down through countless lineages—the practice of starting a bonfire by rubbing two sticks together. The early phase produces nothing visible. No flame. No smoke. Just two slightly warm sticks, an aching forearm and a very reasonable suspicion that fire might not actually be possible by this method. Of course, sustained effort is the only ingredient that ever brings the spark, but the neophyte *doesn’t* know this. They still believe that fire-starters and gasoline exist; that a singular moment of enthusiasm alone could ignite their particular pile of leaves. Alas, there is no shortcut. The friction is the practice.

What, exactly, are the sticks rubbing against one another? Three faculties of mind: attention, focus, and concentration.

Attention is the mind's capacity to narrow itself onto one thing at the deliberate exclusion of everything else. It operates by selection. To pay attention to your right hand requires that you— at least for this moment—not pay attention to your left. To attend to the sound of your breath, you must let go of the temperature in the room. One can think of attention like the zoom feature on a microscope: it is the act of choosing what to examine by excluding the rest.

Focus is distinct from attention, though the two are constantly conflated. Where attention selects, focus clarifies. Where attention asks “what am I looking at?”, focus asks “how clearly am I seeing it?” Two meditators can technically both be paying attention to their breath, but one can be observing it through a kind of distracted fog while the other is perceiving its precise qualities—the slight coolness on the upper lip during inhalation, the warmer quality on exhalation, the micro-pause between cycles, and so on. The first has attention without focus. The second has both. Focus is the focal knob of the microscope—what takes a blur and resolves it.

The third faculty walking alongside them is concentration. Concentration is the steady holding of attention and focus on a chosen object over time. Where attention selects and focus clarifies, concentration sustains. It is what allows the microscope, once aimed and adjusted, to remain steady on the slide rather than drifting every few seconds to something else. Without concentration, attention scatters and focus dissolves: the meditator finds the breath, observes it clearly for two seconds, then suddenly realizes they’ve been planning a romantic evening with someone they’ve never even actually talked to. With concentration, attention holds and focus deepens, allowing the object being examined to reveal layers it cannot reveal when observation is interrupted constantly.

These three faculties, applied together to a single chosen object—the breath, a mantra, a candle flame, the point between the eyebrows—comprise what the yogic tradition calls *dhāraṇā*. Dhāraṇā is the first stick rubbing against the second. It is the deliberate, effortful placement of attention, focus, and concentration on a primary object while the rest of the mind questions, protests, and tries to wander off. Anyone who has actually tried this knows what it looks like in practice: you sit down, you find the breath, you lose it within four seconds to a fantasy about lunch, you return, you lose it to a re-litigation of a conversation from 2019, you return, you lose it to wondering whether your back is going to hurt later, you return, you lose it to imagining what would happen if you just gave up and became a goat farmer. You return. You repeat this loop several thousand times.[[This is not a failure of the dhāraṇā practice. It is the practice.]]

Why is the mind so unwilling to stay? Why can't attention simply rest on the breath from the very first sitting? Two reasons, and they compound on one another.

The first is craving. Every distraction that pulls one away from the breath is the mind reaching for a sensation other than the one currently present. This is the satisfaction from a retort one didn’t get to deliver, the relief of scratching an itch, the dopamine of a thought about the future. Some distractions are reaches toward more pleasant sensations; others are escapes from sensations that are already here but unwelcome. Either way, the distraction is a vote against the present moment experience.

The second reason is more specific to this particular era of human history. We are the first generation in the species’ existence to have been raised inside an attention economy explicitly engineered to fragment our concentration for profit. Every notification, every infinite scroll, every algorithmically-tuned dopamine hit has been carefully designed to capture attention, splinter focus, and dissolve concentration as quickly and as repeatedly as possible. The average person now switches contexts every few seconds during waking hours, trains their nervous system on rapid novelty, and arrives at the meditation cushion with a mind that has been deliberately shaped by some of the most sophisticated behavioral engineering in human history. As such, being incapable of resting on a single object for more than a few breaths is not a moral failing. It is the result of a coordinated, multi-trillion-dollar effort to ensure that you cannot sit still. The very faculty that the spiritual path requires is the very faculty that modern life has been systematically dismantling.

The work, then, begins with rebuilding what has been broken: attention, focus and concentration. Crucially, these faculties do not return using force. One cannot simply demand that the mind obey and expect it to work, for the mind which demands is the mind which is also being asked to obey. Good luck squaring that. Instead, they return through, you guessed it, abhyāsa (practice) and vairāgya (non-attachment).

In other words, the trifecta of attention, focus and concentration naturally improves as a consequence of letting go. The more one is willing to unflinchingly be with their unfiltered feelings, emotions and sensations, the steadier their mind tends to become, because the mind has fewer reasons to flee the present moment.

Though impossible to see the moment it occurs, there comes a glorious point in each one’s practice where the friction of one’s discipline begins to produce enough heat to generate smoke. Now things that were concealed beneath the hard shell of timber become apparent: feelings and stories one didn't know were there. Emotions one had successfully avoided for decades. Primal memories surfacing seemingly at random. Given that the smoke is black and smells like shit, many practitioners assume something has gone wrong at this stage. In fact, it’s a sign that something is finally going right. The subtler *vṛttis*—the ones that were running silently beneath the audible chatter, the evaluations and micro-verdicts the citta was churning out without your knowledge—are finally rising into view where they can be seen and let go of. The wood is drying out and the fire is approaching. The wood is drying out and the fire is approaching. As the smoke thickens, a fourth faculty becomes essential: mindfulness. Mindfulness is the quality of non-reactive, non-judgmental awareness applied to whatever the other three faculties have brought into view. It is what allows the practitioner to sit with the rising smoke—the discomforts, the surfacing memories, the strange new sensations—without flinching from them, reaching for them, or trying to fix or interpret them. Without mindfulness, sharper attention may only mean sharper anguish. With mindfulness, what is observed is permitted to be what it is. And it is that permission which allows the deeper layers of conditioning to dissolve. Mindfulness is the scientist's neutrality at the eyepiece—the capacity to observe the slide without contaminating what is being seen with their bias. “Oh…Earth-shattering, soul-crushing sadness is present. Isn’t that interesting?”

When all four faculties are sustained for long enough—when the practitioner can remain non-reactive even as deeper and stranger material continues to billow—the distinction between the meditator and the object of meditation automatically begins to dissolve. Increasingly, there is no longer a “me observing the breath”. Instead, it is revealed that there is only breath breathing itself. Congratulations, your sticks have produced a flame.

In yoga, this shift is known as dhyāna—moving from doing meditation to being meditation. Such a delicate, delectable, sustained state is oft described as being like the steady pouring of oil from one vessel to another. Dhyāna is the lived realization and well-deserved fruit of one’s commitment to non-attachment. It’s quite nice.

But don’t stop now—the party’s just getting started! Like a trusted friend, the cultivation of non-judgment towards oneself (and others) offers the mind a safe space in which it feels comfortable revealing every last one of its secrets. Once dhyāna starts coming around, the layers of the mind begin to reach surprisingly far back. Back to 15 years old, 10 years old, 5 years old, 3 years old, during birth, and even in-utero. Apparently, absolutely everything—from the smell of one’s grandmother’s kitchen to the nightmare at five years old—was recorded and stored in the cloud. All it was waiting for was the appropriate moment to be remembered and let go of. Indeed, it is completely normal at this stage for *saṃskāras* to predate one’s current body. As the noise quiets and the field becomes increasingly transparent, what surfaces can be patterns of ancestral grief carried in the lineage for generations, lifetimes of unfinished karmic threads, visceral memories of historical periods rendered in detail too precise and too foreign to be invented. Some practitioners find themselves with full somatic recall of past incarnations—the particular ache of an injury sustained centuries ago, or the texture of a relationship that ended long before this body was conceived. Others encounter what can only be described as contact with intelligences from other planes or solar systems—luminous, communicative, unmistakably real and yet entirely outside the realm of the terrestrial. Still others receive information about future states humanity might enter, or—more sobering—about the timing and nature of their own physical death.

None of this is mystical embellishment, and none of it is hallucination in the clinical sense. It is simply what becomes perceptible when the surface chatter has finally quieted enough to allow the deeper field to be seen. The flame, now well established, is consuming layers of the log that the smoking phase could never have reached.

And yet…even still…even here…the practice remains the same: let go.

In time, the visible flames begin to recede. Granted, the fire is not dying, God no. They’re receding because the process has reached the dense interior of the wood. From the outside, this phase can look almost uneventful: the spiritual fireworks of the earlier stages have largely subsided, the dramatic surfacings have grown less frequent. But the embers are the hottest part of any fire, still annihilating. What may appear as nothing special is, in fact, the most intense and total transformation yet. This is the agonizingly slow, radiant burning of the densest, oldest, most compressed layers of *the conditioned mind*: this sense of being a separate self in the first place. This is the master saṃskāra, the one beneath all others, the one that makes “letting go” possible to speak about. It is here, at this final train station that the self, paradoxically, begins to let go of itself. So begins the rather boring, but sometimes excruciating, death of the ego once and for all, now suffocating in the realization of its own imperfection and the infinite mercy in which it is held.

When even this final contraction is met with sustained, complete non-attachment, something occurs that *the conditioned mind* has no framework for and no language to describe. Here the fire has finally completed turning the log into itself, and there is no longer a separate practitioner having an experience of awareness. There is only awareness, undivided, bright and Self-revealing. This is samādhi, the spontaneous condition that emerges once there is finally no one left to grip. It is also, at long last, the fulfillment of the four-word definition that opened this chapter—yogaś citta-*vṛtti*-*nirodhaḥ*. The fluctuations have not been muzzled or frozen over; they have ceased because the impressions generating them have finally dissolved. The lake is calm, at last, not by force but by nature. We will return to this fully in Chapter X, but for now it suffices to say that this is the destination toward which all of this abhyāsa and vairāgya have been quietly aiming.

Keep going!
